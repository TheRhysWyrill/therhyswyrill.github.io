A sentient pea bounces through Game Boy-pastel islands, towers, caves, trains and castles, capped by an ending card that swaps the title for something greedier, and there is no story to speak of beyond that. The art style lands well, with the original Game Boy's nice pastel green palette executed cleanly, and the pea protagonist jams out when idle. The developer's house style is recognisable immediately from its layout language, and this predates the studio's later, larger titles. The atmosphere is carried almost entirely by per-stage music, initially assumed to be a handful of songs before unique tracks kept appearing. The whole package is cheeky and self-aware.

Bite-sized levels are the game's saving grace, the reason full-coin completion sticks, because even repeated deaths cost almost no progress. Short and sweet stage design makes redoing a level a snap rather than a punishment. Core loop is precision platforming with per-level coin and diamond totals displayed up front, and achievements only pop with full treasure collection, which turns completionism into the game's real challenge and escalates difficulty through the mid-run. The displayed tallies make the hunt legible and compulsive. Roughly twenty-five levels fall in a single hour-long session, and the house style's identity-per-stage music gives even repeated archetypes fresh texture.

Level archetypes repeat across towers, caves, castles, train stages and forests with unique layouts and songs each. Difficulty spikes late, with a bubble-flooded dungeon level nearly breaking patience through repeated deaths, oversized hitboxes and blocked coins. The bubbles are the central complaint: hitboxes far bigger than their sprites, spawn patterns that come from wherever they please rather than any bastard pattern, and a late level where they block coins with no clean route, rendering it literally impossible to avoid them. One hidden seventeenth coin evaded discovery for most of the run before revealing itself after the credits. The completionist loop is genuinely rewarding, but the bubbles test it hard.

No ledge-recovery jump after falling is the design's clearest gap, and the double jump cannot be used once the pea leaves the ledge, a frustration the sequel would later fix. The CRT and tube effect is best turned off immediately, reading as vomit-inducing rather than retro. The X key resets the level and catches the unwary more than once. An early stage displayed one more coin than existed, mirroring a known bug from the same developer's other title. A late middle-finger moment, a floor assumed to hide a secret in a game that does not do secrets, stings but is honestly earned.

The full-coin commitment is self-inflicted, and the frustration aims squarely at the bubbles rather than the design's fairness. Every achievement falls in the same session once the missing coin is recovered post-credits, and the closing wrap is a clean 100%. The difficulty is front-loaded in the mid-run completion rather than the base levels, which are decently challenging but never unfair. The bite-sized structure means the game respects time even when it disrespects patience. The house style's cheek is the identity.

The opening assumption that only a handful of songs exist is corrected naturally as unique tracks keep surfacing, and the arcade rhythm of repeated deaths never once feels like wasted time given how quickly a stage reloads. The displayed totals create a completionist pull the game earns honestly, and the ending card's title swap is the last joke in a package built from them. The mid-run escalation never tips into unfairness because the levels are short enough to retry without cost. The design confidence in its own brevity is the achievement.

Overall, a tough but fair micro-platformer whose bite-sized levels, displayed collectible tallies and per-stage music make full completion genuinely rewarding, with the Game Boy-pastel art style and the pea's idle jamming giving it personality. Oversized bubble hitboxes, a coin-blocking late level, no ledge-recovery jump and a coin-count display bug are honest costs, and the completionist loop is worth the patience for anyone who likes their precision platformers cheeky.
