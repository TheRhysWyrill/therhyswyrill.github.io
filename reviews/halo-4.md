A four-years-later drift wakes a supersoldier and a deteriorating AI onto Requiem, a Forerunner world of cinematic geometry where the Didact and the Prometheans await. The consensus says this is where the series declined, and the reputation is half right: the story is admittedly a mess, with the Didact and Terminus arc full of holes, Elites turning on humanity again without explanation, and Prometheans shifting allegiance with even less justification. Gameplay tells a different story entirely. The sandbox is varied, every weapon is viable, and the emotional core lands harder than the plot rigour ever does.

Sprint and armour abilities arrive here for the first time and earn their place, with jetpack and auto-sentry both seeing real use, and movement feels quicker without breaking the classic rhythm. The weapon sandbox rewards omnivorous play: the suppressor is beautiful, the storm rifle and bolt shot slot in naturally, and the rail gun prompts an honest question about what it replaced. Not every redesign succeeds visually. The Magnum looks goofy, the shotgun borders on tragic, and the sniper divides, even as the new sounds for all three improve on the originals. The plasma pistol takes a nerf, its battery draining fast enough to matter.

The set-pieces carry the campaign's spectacle. The Dawn crash opens with impact, the Mammoth fortress push plays like a rolling siege, the gondola defence and Ivanoff station Mantis fight escalate cleanly, and the Broadsword flight into the portal-hopping Midnight gauntlet closes with genuine scale. Eight missions, a prologue plus seven, keep the pacing tight, and all seven terminals reward thorough exploration, though one hides behind the spawn point with almost spiteful placement. Character work gives the spectacle its weight: Halsey's interrogation prologue and the Librarian's reveal frame the emotional throughline, and the AI's decline gives the final levels weight the plot holes never erode.

Friction is real but scattered. The nerfed plasma pistol joins bad checkpoint placements, including one that saves mid-fall off an edge, endless Promethean harassment in the back half, and tiny melee-lunge hitboxes that punish close-range confidence. Subtitles render in a colour that makes them hard to read against bright scenes, a series oddity carried forward. None of these break the combat loop, which stays the game's strongest suit from the Dawn crash to the credits.

Continuity erosion is the lore-minded player's real complaint. The Brutes have simply ceased to exist, dual wielding is gone, English-speaking Elites are silent now, and beloved weapons vanish without comment, making this the oddest continuation in the collection. The visual presentation, impressive even on original hardware, holds up in the collection's remaster, and the Forerunner aesthetic gives every environment a scale the earlier games only hinted at. The soundtrack deserves its reputation alongside the visuals.

The Cortana arc is the reason this campaign matters. Her decline is followed with genuine investment, and the farewell lands as one of the series' most affecting moments, elevating the muddled Forerunner plot around it. The emotional throughline survives every plot hole, and the ending gives the sandbox combat a purpose the missions alone would not supply. Whatever the consensus says about the story, the character work earns its place.

Overall, this is a very fun, varied sandbox shooter and a genuinely moving AI farewell wrapped around a plot with real holes, silent Elites, missing series staples and a few ugly weapon redesigns. The gameplay remains very fun, and the ending lands; both halves of that split verdict are true at once.
