A city descends into riot and psychosis, and disgraced agent Ethan Thomas walks back into it with bloodied knuckles and a drinking problem. Across museums, a doll factory, an abandoned school and a scrapyard peninsula, the hunt for Serial Killer X pulls toward the shadowy Oro Invictus conspiracy, with City News TV vignettes punctuating the descent. The noir-conspiracy plot escalates steadily, though the sonic-generation lore drifts into science fiction the grittier material cannot fully support, and the story stops on an unresolved cliffhanger rather than a conclusion.

Melee combat is the mechanical heart. Parries, chain attacks, environmental kills and improvised weapons build a system with real depth, and the weapon variety runs from 2x4s and bowling pins to broadswords and battle axes with distinct damage and condition values. Firearms exist but with limited ammunition, keeping the emphasis on close-quarters brutality, and the hand-to-hand system rewards commitment to learning its rhythm.

Forensic investigation gives the brawling a second register. Crime scenes ask for UV lights, blood-spatter analysis and careful evidence collection, and per-level collectibles in the form of sonic emitters and antennas award bronze, silver and gold upgrades. A non-lethal guard objective in the museum offers a genuinely different challenge, and the hunt for ranks sustains engagement across eleven levels.

The alcohol-withdrawal mechanic stands out as both joke and system. Ethan's aim literally depends on drinking, and steady hands after a bottle make the firearms viable in a way no contemporaries attempted. Late-game unlocks add the sonic shout, which scatters enemies at a blast, alongside holster and upgrade progression that rewards the earlier collectible hunting.

Chase sequences and the mutated bear stalk sit at the bottom of the design. Proximity feedback never clarifies how close the threat actually is, and the dread of those sections comes more from uncertainty about the mechanics than from the horror itself. Both are short, but each lands with the thud of an idea the rest of the game handles better.

Physicality turns clunky in specific places. Sprint head-bobbing persists throughout, and throwing physics are finicky enough to make the baby-doll boss and the bomb-disposal tasks through narrow windows the most stressful encounters in the campaign, stressful for the wrong reasons. The final boss against Doland reduces to a quick-time event, a flat payoff after hours of hands-on combat.

Overall, the melee depth, weapon variety, forensic framing and grim atmosphere make this a cult classic worth finishing, and the flaws are era-typical rather than structural. Clunky throws, head-bob and a QTE finale are real costs, and the cliffhanger ending goes nowhere. A bleak, distinctive brawler-horror whose combat still satisfies nearly two decades on.
