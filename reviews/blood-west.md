An amnesiac undead gunslinger is summoned by a soul-totem to purify a supernatural Wild West where the dead walk, gold is cursed and something demonic sleeps beneath the mountains. Three chapters carry the story through canyon, swamp and mountains, with a tree-man, a feral child, a digger, a charlatan priest and a doctor among the memorable faces. The finale lands a genuinely satisfying twist that reframes the whole quest, and the oppressive low-fi western gothic atmosphere does heavy lifting throughout: monster roars, breathing and distant screams keep every corner suspect.

The intertwined level design is the structural star: caves, mines and towns loop back onto each other, every area hides shortcuts, and the realisation that a mine exit feeds back to a canyon entrance is a genuine delight. Semi-open zones span canyons, mines, swamps, towns, a fort and a carnival, each dense with secrets. Swamp navigation is the one genuine frustration in an otherwise immaculately looped world.

The core loop rewards patience: sneak, bonk, loot, sell, repeat, with notes-based bestiary documentation telling you which monsters fear silver and which demand fire. The writing is exceptional for the genre, from the settler diary arc to the Iron Rose expedition logs, the priest's fake cure and a stolen-skin quest, all of it funny, grim and memorable. Enemy variety keeps the wariness sharp: animated dead, cultists, wendigos, skin collectors, shotgun-toting cultists, demons, and one invisible screamer that will never be heard coming.

Build-crafting runs deep: some twenty perks, artifacts with permanent trade-offs (a wendigo heart, a scab of sacrifice, a coin necklace), special weapons including an instakill-chance lever action, cursed gold buried via shovel to break the curse, safes with codes and totem blessings. A mid-game full respec via tincture reframes the whole endgame, and the last stretch becomes a victory lap of instakill slaughter. Souls-like death mechanics (soul flaws, curses, experience debt) exist but stay mild, with curses liftable through totem side-quests.

Gunplay feels punchy but is deliberately handicapped: slow reloads, loud shots, and inconsistent noise detection that alerts some enemies and inexplicably misses others. It is fun to shoot but rarely worth the noise, which is the design working as intended even when it frustrates. Detection logic is uneven at close range, with enemies turning on a dime at the worst moments.

The Chapter 1 boss is the game's one real misstep: a jarring difficulty spike with mid-fight wendigo ambushes, unclear boss health and a fight that punishes the unprepared disproportionately, standing in stark contrast to the much fairer later bosses (a giant stalker, the chasm demon, and a final totem-dragon clash that lands fair). Finding a bed to rest is its own small quest, half-jokingly the hardest thing in the game, and respawn logic stays opaque with some enemies returning on rest and others at random. The inventory system, especially on controller, is fiddly: tiny pockets, no drop mechanic, awkward stacking, plus a duplicated-map purchase mix-up and a misleading valley that turned out to be empty after a dangerous jump. Keyboard and mouse is the honest way to play.

Overall, a genuinely meaty, atmospheric indie immersive sim that rewards patience, exploration and reading, with some twenty-five hours of content at a budget price. A punishing opening boss, sluggish reloads and inventory friction are real costs, but the intertwined map design, bestiary lore and build depth make this an easy recommendation.
