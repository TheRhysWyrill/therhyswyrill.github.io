A small white creature called Baba pushes word tiles around a grid to rewrite the rules of each level, and the entire game follows from that single verb. "BABA IS YOU" defines what the player controls, "FLAG IS WIN" defines the goal, "WALL IS STOP" defines obstruction, and every one of those sentences can be dismantled and reassembled. The narrative is deliberately minimal, the art style deliberately strange, and the tone shifts by world: playful and bright in the shallows, genuinely ominous in the Mountain's music, unsettling by design in the Depths. Nothing else in the puzzle space lets the player rewrite the game's own rules as the primary interaction.

The rule system is a programming puzzle in disguise. Double negatives ("not baba is not keke"), conditionals ("X on Y", "X near Y", "facing wall"), object transformation (becoming the key, the rock, the text, even the level itself) and automation levels with self-moving pieces build a logic vocabulary that grows for eighteen hours without repeating itself. Turning Baba into the word "Baba" to pin him in place, or running conveyor-belt cloning systems, feels less like solving a puzzle and more like writing one. The level-transforming trick, where the map itself becomes a manipulable object, is the standout novelty: applying "LEVEL IS FLAG" to the wrong level can unlock an entire hidden world, and the game intends exactly that.

Most puzzles admit multiple valid solutions, which transforms the design from gatekeeping into generosity. Unintended routes feel rewarding rather than cheap, and the bonus "extra levels" reframing earlier puzzles as harder remixes acknowledge that players will break the intended path and deserve a harder question for it. The overworld itself becomes a puzzle once left behind, its maps turning into levels in their own right. 200+ levels spread across Lake, Ruins, Forest of Fall, Chasm, Volcano, Mountain and the hidden Depths and Meta worlds collect dandelions, flowers and orbs as optional markers of mastery.

The late game is where the cleverness curdles. The Meta world's cursor-based navigation converts exploration into busywork, its brilliant ideas outstaying their welcome until the tediousness overwhelms the wit. Booby Trap, a level that must be re-solved to reach areas of the map, wears out its welcome by the third visit and is barely tolerable by the sixth. The final areas spike hard: One Way Entrance represents the genuine wall, the kind of stumper that tempts a hint lookup for the first time in the whole run. The difficulty is earned, but the navigation friction around it is not.

Aesthetics do quiet work. The overworld theme lodges in memory after a single session, the Mountain area's music turns genuinely creepy, and the Depths' visual design is calculated to unsettle. The hidden Gallery levels unlock concept art and prototype builds, early development mock-ups that reward curiosity rather than padding completion percentages. Presentation is never the point, but the strange art style and shifting audio palette give each world its own identity before a single rule is pushed.

Completion runs deep. Dandelions, flowers and orbs track optional mastery, the super-secret final level demands re-solving levels most players would rather leave behind, and 100% minus one deliberate skip still represents an eighteen-hour investment. The game respects that commitment with the Gallery and extra levels, and it never gates the ending behind full collection. Difficulty ramps hard after the overworld, and the steepest walls sit precisely where the navigation friction is worst, which compounds the late-game fatigue unnecessarily.

Overall, this is a genuinely brilliant puzzle game whose rule-rewriting creativity, multiple-solutions design and hidden-world secrets deliver more "how did they think of that" moments than any competitor, and whose only real flaw is that the late-game Meta world and its cursor system get in their own way. The cleverness survives the tedium; the programming-puzzle framing holds from first hour to last. Endure the Meta grind and the rest of the game justifies itself completely.
