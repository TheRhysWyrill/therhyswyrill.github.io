A small forest spirit descends through a corrupted dream-forest to gather orbs for its mother, guided and misled in equal measure by a deer guardian, a sinister dark counterpart and cryptic talking stumps. The premise is a wordless fairy tale told through tone rather than exposition, and the atmosphere is the star: crying sprite followers chanting in unison, whispering helpers, eyes blinking in the dark, and a blind hermit who burned out his own eyes. Horror is expressed through unease rather than jumpscares, though the occasional pod-pop and grabbing flower still startle. Nothing else on the market blends swarm management with survival-horror tone this successfully.

The follower system delivers investment the premise alone would not. Recruiting the fragile creatures, charging their spells, sacrificing them as bombs and protecting them from corruption builds a genuine protective instinct, precisely because they cannot fight for themselves; every loss is the player's failure. Combat supplements them with dodge-based melee, charged spell shots and a follower-fuelled shotgun, while gate and switch puzzles use the followers themselves as keys and ammunition, so the army is always both resource and responsibility.

The light-as-weapon design is the game's smartest idea. The lamp purifies corruption and attracts it in equal measure, and shining light into darkness invites enemies the player might otherwise have avoided; the stealth-leaning south forest chapter rewards restraint beautifully. Secret red flowers hide seeds and health upgrades, and collecting all twelve unlocks the better of the two endings, a corruption-versus-light duality that gives the secret-hunting purpose beyond completion.

Friction begins with the camera. The fixed, zoomed-in controller view is the single biggest recurring complaint: threats arrive unseen, sightlines are permanently short, and the absence of a zoom-out option constrains tactical planning throughout. The corruption gauntlets compound the problem, spawning endless waves that tip into mess rather than challenge, and sniper enemies firing five-shot bursts can wipe fragile followers instantly, creating stretches that feel unwinnable through no fault of the player's positioning.

The mirror-match final boss is confusingly tuned. It takes almost no damage until the melee-then-shoot rhythm is discovered, and nothing telegraphs the solution, which turns the climax into trial and error where the rest of the game taught through atmosphere. Saving occurs only at chapter entries, which stings during long dangerous chapters, and a solo stretch with no mushroom supply starves the potion economy at the worst moment. The movable title screen, which drags its version number around with it, remains an inexplicable curiosity.

Enemy variety is broader than the roster's basics suggest: swoopers, chargers, kamikazes and mimic pods each behave distinctly, and the mimic pods in particular punish greed with well-earned jumps. Fires warded against corruption, mushroom-hit potion crafting and orb purification give the chapters a rhythm of gather-cleanse-advance, and the eight-chapter structure plus prologue and epilogue paces the roughly three-hour runtime cleanly. The goal of carrying orbs to the mother gives even the messiest gauntlets direction, and the epilogue lands the duality the whole forest has been building toward.

Overall, this is a beautifully strange, occasionally messy indie that delivers a genuinely novel horror twist on follower management, and its secrets reward thoroughness with the better of its two endings. The locked camera, the sniper gauntlets and the obtuse mirror boss are isolated rough patches rather than flaws of the whole, and the cryptic, unsettling aesthetic stays in memory long after the credits.
