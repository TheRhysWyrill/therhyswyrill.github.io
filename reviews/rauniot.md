A natural disaster has drowned the continents by 1975, leaving Northern Finland home to mutated children, cannibals and small trusting communities, and a bleak, darkly comic point-and-click unfolds from there. Rauniot commits fully to its Finnish setting: authentic dialect voice work, Lapland scenery and a deadpan apocalypse that few adventure games attempt. The grim setup is undercut by gallows humour from the first ten minutes, with a blow-up doll spotted almost immediately and a puzzle that involves threatening a printer. The tonal blend is confident rather than accidental, and the regional specificity gives the apocalypse a flavour its American and Japanese peers lack. It is a very unique experience, and the personality carries it.

The presentation is the standout throughout: hand-drawn character portraits, diary entries and moody post-apocalyptic scenery, with a character design for the lead that earns its own praise. A contacts journal of hand-drawn portraits doubles as a cast list and a mood piece. The art direction does the worldbuilding the sparse script leaves unsaid, from flooded shelters to rusted rail yards. Every screen looks composed rather than assembled. Few indies of this scale present this consistently well.

Puzzle design splits into two camps. The satisfying logic problems (the reactor lights, the two-key twist) reward genuine deduction, while others are opaque enough that the correct solution arrives by trial and error rather than understanding, with the sector 4 track-switching puzzle solved by pressing buttons until something worked. The generator-refuelling loop adds its own confusion, with fuel apparently required again immediately after being supplied. The finale (refuelling the nuclear locomotive, generator swapping, the radio tower) is satisfying even when confusing. Cryptic opacity is a fair criticism of the mid-game specifically. The shelter puzzles hit the design's sweet spot.

Interaction hotspots are subtle and easy to miss, causing the longest stall in the package: a heroin tube mistaken for a crayon sent the search for painkillers everywhere else first. The realisation produces the session's best laugh at the player's own expense, which is the game's humour working even against its own pacing. A notebook of auto-notes helps track objectives, and fast travel across the shelter map is genuinely helpful. No sprint button and no map in the reactor depths make the required backtracking tedious. Manual saving at every opportunity is advisable, since whether the game autosaves stays unclear.

The fast-travel map connects shelters, a gas station, a rail yard and a secret reactor bunker, and the classic inventory puzzling across them fills a brisk single-sitting runtime. Authentic Finnish voice acting grounds the setting in a way English or Japanese localisation never could, and playing in a language outside the usual options is genuinely novel. The darkly comic apocalypse earns comparisons to the grimmer end of the Broken Sword lineage. The time-skip epilogue closes the story at the right length. Nothing outstays its welcome.

The betrayal that reshapes the final act lands with weight the puzzle-frustration never undermines, and the supporting cast's fates give the bleakness stakes. The hand-drawn diary entries carry the emotional register between puzzles. Deadpan gallows humour keeps the misery palatable without defusing it. The package earns its recommendation on personality and presentation where its puzzle design occasionally wobbles. For a debut-scale indie, the confidence is remarkable.

Overall, a stylish, authentically Finnish post-apocalyptic point-and-click whose hand-drawn presentation, dialect voice work and darkly comic script reward patience with one of the more distinctive indies of its kind. Cryptic puzzle opacity, subtle hotspots and sprint-less backtracking are real costs, but the personality and the brisk runtime make them worth tolerating.
