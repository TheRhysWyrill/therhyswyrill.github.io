A single division's entire war arc gives this spin-off a shape the mainline entry never attempted: the US 1st Infantry Division from Operation Torch in North Africa through Sicily, Omaha Beach, the Siegfried Line and Germany's surrender. Trading the mainline game's multiplayer focus for a character-driven campaign was the right call, and the squad banter (a Bronx running gag, a doomed replacement the seasoned player predicts from a mile off) is formulaic but charming. The title's meaning, explained through genuine documentary material, gives the whole campaign an identity beyond its shooter bones, and Crucifix Hill's three-grenade-in-a-row ambushes exemplify the spam problem the design never solves.

Mission variety is the standout, and it is remarkable how much is packed into a five-hour campaign: a full B-24 bomber flight, tank driving and tank-crew sections, anti-air and flak gun defence, beach landings, and standard infantry pushes. The structure recalls Battlefield 1's war-story anthology more than any contemporary shooter; it is a genuine relief not to be running from point to point in shooting galleries, and the bomber mission in particular is a high point that justifies the whole venture.

The Military Channel interstitials between acts are the second standout. Genuine documentary segments teach real history, including why the division carries the red one designation, and the framing gives the campaign an earnestness the era rarely managed. Period authenticity extends to the health system: limited health with medkits and health-aware checkpoints is a welcome old-school challenge, and the M1 Garand's no-mid-clip reload remains a period charm.

Friction clusters in the shooting itself. Enemy grenade spam arrives with no on-screen indicator, no warning of blast radius, and frequently in threes; screen shake constantly obscures visibility during firefights, making the two problems compound; and enemy hit reactions are mushy, with bullets knocked down rather than killed and enemies standing back up after several hits. No sprint makes the fall-back sequences genuinely tense for the wrong reasons.

Squad AI completes the era-typical frustrations. Companions run into the player's line of fire with alarming frequency, leading to accidental friendly kills the game notably declines to punish (a mercy its PC cousin did not extend), occasionally bugs out entirely with soldiers standing still instead of following, and enemy counts swamp the medkit economy in the Normandy and Siegfried Line stretches. The controls are chunky PS2-era design: lean on the D-pad, aim and fire on bumpers, an awkward mapping that demands patience.

The ending deflates slightly, closing on blowing up some rockets with an abruptness the preceding campaign does not earn, but the post-game impression recovers: what lingers is the variety, the documentary framing and the complete division's-arc structure rather than the final beat. The formulaic tropes (the rookie's fate among them) are acknowledged by the game's own tone and forgivable in context, and the sound design, from bomber engines to distant artillery, holds up better than the visuals suggest.

Overall, this is a charming, varied and historically earnest PS2-era campaign whose old-school friction is easy to forgive because so little of it is another shooting gallery: the bomber flight, the tank sections and the genuine interstitial documentaries carry it well past its grenade spam and screen shake. As a spin-off it outstructures its mainline sibling's single-player, and the complete division's war arc gives it a cohesion few shooters of its vintage managed.
