The premise remains distinctive: a documentary-style opening about peak oil, pandemic and a 2024 Russo-Chinese coalition war over the last Caspian wells, told through a war-weary press character and towns built in two weeks by oil contractors. The near-future framing genuinely landed, and the front-line system is the mechanical signature: battle lines between the two sides shift as objectives are captured, literally redrawing the map mid-mission. Redeployment-as-canon reinforces it, casting the player as just a grunt rather than a chosen one. British-soldier banter keeps the grim tone from ever becoming self-serious. Nothing else from the Call of Duty 4 era committed to a front line that moves.

The seven-mission tour spans Turkmenistan, Kazakhstan and Russia, and the geography gives each theatre its own texture. Sun-bleached desert towns give way to industrial Kazakh complexes and finally the frozen approaches to Moscow, with the capture-and-hold objective chains adapting to each setting. The front-line redrawing reads clearest in the later missions, where pushing a line forward visibly opens or closes the routes available to the next objective. Helmet-cam presentation echoes Ghost Recon: Advanced Warfighter, situating the game firmly in its era's aesthetic. The campaign never leaves its linear rails, but the rails are dressed well enough to disguise them.

Enemy toughness versus player fragility is the defining frustration from the first mission to the last: enemies absorb enormous damage while the player folds instantly. Enemy snipers compound it with pinpoint accuracy and instant reaction times, obliterating from nowhere in nearly every mission, and enemy tanks soak far more hits than their counterparts. The redeployment system, six lives per level later raised to ten, is the reason the game stays playable at all. Loadouts can only be changed on death, never before, a backwards quirk that turns every respawn into a menu visit. Playing on Normal still swings hard on the health asymmetry.

Vehicle and gadget variety is the mechanical highlight: tanks, a nitrous tank, a helicopter, a recon drone, an RC attack helicopter with rockets, airstrike call-ins, deployable sentry guns and a railgun that can kill its own user at point-blank range. The RC helicopter in particular is a delight, and the railgun earns its place as the campaign's most memorable toy. Vehicle combat is inconsistent around the edges, but the gimmick density keeps the seven-mission arc across Turkmenistan, Kazakhstan and Russia moving. Capture-and-hold objectives chain each mission with vehicle sections interspersed. The front-line gimmicks are what make the campaign worth remembering.

Controller support is technically present but practically broken: no crouch or prone binding, no sensitivity options, no controller glyphs, and turning at a snail's pace. A mid-run switch to keyboard and mouse becomes inevitable, and aim improves immediately afterwards. The PC port lacks Vsync entirely, forcing screen tearing and stuttery cutscenes unless forced through compatibility tooling on an older Proton version. Getting the game running on SteamOS is a project in itself. Port quality is the least excusable flaw in the package.

The weapon pool is thin, roughly three usable rifles, two snipers, an RPG and gadgets that go unused, which narrows the loadout experimentation the front-line system seems built for. The unexplained timer at the top of the HUD sits at zero for the entire game, and railgun mission props despawn without explanation. The music is unexpectedly good, one of the few elements praised outright, and the helmet-cam aesthetic invites comparison to Ghost Recon: Advanced Warfighter. Roughly seven missions in a single session makes the campaign concise, echoing the same developers' later Homefront brevity. The ending stops on an unresolved cliffhanger, clearly cut for a sequel that never came, with Moscow taken and China massing.

Overall, the near-future oil-war setting, the varied vehicles and gadgets and a redeployment system that keeps punishing difficulty fair make this a concise, janky but genuinely fun curio from the developers who would later make Homefront. Titanium enemies, pinpoint-accuracy snipers, a missing Vsync option and broken controller support are the cost of entry, and the cliffhanger ending resolves nothing. Fun with a little frustration is the honest verdict, and the front-line system alone justifies the trip.
