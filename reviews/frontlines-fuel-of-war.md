Frontlines: Fuel of War depicts a desperate, resource-starved future where global stability has collapsed entirely into an endless, brutal struggle for oil. This bleak, post-everything setting effectively captures the exhaustion of a world pushed to its absolute limits by continuous conflict and the total depletion of essential natural supplies.

Stationary turrets and objective-based capture sequences form the primary rhythm of this linear campaign, casting the player as an utterly replaceable soldier. Such a design choice emphasises the vast, impersonal scale of the war, where maintaining the momentum of the mission objective becomes infinitely more vital than individual survival.

Bullet-sponge adversaries frequently undermine the sense of tactical realism, as enemies endure excessive damage while simultaneously demonstrating pinpoint, rapid-fire accuracy. These combat encounters create a persistent imbalance that leaves the player feeling significantly underpowered, often making the basic act of pushing forward against entrenched forces feel like a chore.

Inconsistent control mapping, particularly regarding basic actions like crouching or proning, complicates the experience for those preferring a gamepad over precision inputs. Vehicle operations similarly suffer from cumbersome, unintuitive handling mechanics, which transform the operation of tanks and aircraft into a recurring annoyance rather than a empowering shift in pace.

Unexpectedly high production values define the audio landscape, as the soundtrack provides a polished score that consistently exceeds expectations for this vintage of shooter. These soaring compositions successfully elevate the grim atmosphere, momentarily masking the limitations of a weapon pool that otherwise lacks meaningful variety or tactical impact.

Technical stability fluctuates dramatically depending on the environment, with the Linux-based Proton layer surprisingly offering a much smoother performance than the stuttering, tear-prone Windows release. Identifying the correct configuration is essential, yet once the software is running correctly, the short, gritty journey remains a functional and strangely cohesive diversion.

Overall, this brief military romp provides a frantic, old-school experience that sits uneasily between being an engaging relic and a mechanical headache. Whilst the redeployment system prevents total stagnation, the combination of spongey enemy design and awkward control implementation ensures it remains a curiosity best experienced by genre enthusiasts on sale.
