A bird and a bunny chase a flyer promising that the Celestial Stone will clear their bank debts, and land on pirate-plagued Sunrise Island to find it. The setup is pure Saturday-morning cartoon: cheeky pirates, a grumpy money-garden owner, tourist-committee sign gags, and gravestone jokes about a woman who died chasing a coin into a poisonous swamp. The dialogue is light rather than substantive, and it never pretends otherwise. What the game is really selling is a feeling: the specific pleasure of a 16-bit puzzle-adventure, rebuilt with obvious affection.

That affection shows in the structure. Five themed areas (Lime Forest, Mango Desert, Dusk Woods, Amber Mines, Mystic Mountains) each hand over a new tool, from the shovel and boomerang to the hammer, grappling hook and running shoes, and each builds its puzzles around the current gadget. Key-and-chest progression keeps the loop legible, block-push puzzles and flower-tile plates provide the brainteasers, and environmental hazards including spikes, poison swamps and ice supply the danger. It is a formula executed with confidence rather than invention.

The flower-tile puzzles deserve particular mention. The East/West switch link that ties two distant plates together is an elegant, understated piece of design, and the game's best moments come from exactly this kind of quiet click: two mechanisms, one insight. Mid-bosses and area bosses punctuate each area, and the self-destruct escape finale sends the whole island out with a proper sense of occasion.

Death costs nothing but money. Keys, tools and progress all survive a failed attempt, which keeps the loop completely frictionless and lets even the harshest boss attempts be retried without dread. The money itself is the weakest system on offer: it accumulates constantly with no shop to spend it in, resets on death, and functions purely as score. Some flavour surrounds it, but mechanically it is a number pretending to be a currency.

The boss roster is where the friction concentrates. Contact damage dominates the fights, particularly when a boss can sweep a hand or body across the arena, and the Dusk Woods ghost boss pairs that with instant-death poison combos. The captain fight's sweeping attack sits at the edge of avoidability. Unreliable hitboxes on corners and spikes make all of it feel worse than the design intends, with player, enemy and hazard collisions all occasionally reading as arbitrary. Retries clear everything, though the bitterness lingers briefly.

Presentation compensates for a lot. The chunky pixel art is era-faithful and expressive, and the per-area music is strong enough to earn repeated attention, from the opening theme to the desert's soundtrack, which holds up long past the point most chiptune loops collapse. That strength makes the short looping area music elsewhere more noticeable by contrast. Keyboard controls feel like an afterthought compared to gamepad support, and the running shoes that should make retracing familiar ground a joy instead expose how stiff the movement stays, while the Steam delisting means the game is now effectively unavailable, which is a strange fate for something this committed to its niche.

Overall, this is a short, janky but genuinely charming love letter to a forgotten 16-bit classic that delivers exactly the nostalgia hit it aims for. The hitboxes, the score-only money and the contact-damage bosses are real irritations, but none of them outweigh the warmth of the tribute. As a modern take on a formula almost nothing else attempts, it earns its place.
