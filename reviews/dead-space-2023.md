An engineer answers a distress call from a mining ship and finds its crew replaced by reanimated corpses, and the remake treats that familiar nightmare with more care than facelift expectations suggest. The engineer's search for his partner threads through a Marker-driven outbreak and its following cult, and the remake deepens him with backstory logs and, for the first time, a voice, an initially strange change that settles quickly. Restructured encounters and reworked area connections keep veterans guessing while preserving the original's beat-for-beat structure. This is a remake that studies its source rather than merely repainting it.

Atmosphere is the biggest single upgrade. Darkness, volumetric fog and reworked quarantine zones make the ship genuinely menacing, and hidden threats now land with real force, with enemies lurking unseen in shadows that the original simply never rendered. The oppressive mood holds across the whole voyage, and the scale of dread the 2008 game gestured at is fully realised. Few remakes earn the claim that their horror works better than the original's; this one does, and the lighting does most of the work.

The tools receive equally thoughtful revision. Kinesis now impales necromorphs with environmental debris, with rebar a standout that turns corridors into ambushes, and stasis, no longer gated by cooldowns, becomes a primary verb rather than an emergency button. Free-float zero-G replaces the old rail-based jumps, reworking classic encounters entirely, including a boss fight whose tentacle-jump pattern demanded a complete redesign. Tram fast-travel between unlocked districts and hoardable power nodes round out quality-of-life additions that respect the original's economy without dulling it.

The ship layout remains its own obstacle. Even returning players get lost constantly, and the bizarre geography, deliberately disorienting in the original, still screws with the head despite rework that makes the map more logical overall. That tension between faithful confusion and modern legibility defines the remake: the ship feels more coherent than before, yet the design's intent keeps navigation challenging. Whether that reads as fidelity or frustration depends on tolerance for backtracking through similar-looking corridors.

Technical state is the genuine liability. Shader compilation stutters, single-digit framerate dips and repeated hitching mar the experience from the opening hour onward, and the PC build ranks among the worst performers in recent memory despite handsome visuals underneath. The default control scheme is awkward enough that most players will remap immediately, and the sequel's classic layout is the obvious fix. Oddly, the game boots straight into gameplay with no title screen, a small but persistent annoyance that sets the tone for the port's rough edges.

Side content rounds out the package. The zero-G basketball minigame returns cleared with relish, collectible audio and plot logs deepen the world's cult lore, and a prototype stasis module rewards thorough exploration. Dismemberment remains the design's core verb, and the plasma cutter and pulse rifle still anchor an arsenal that rewards strategic targeting over body-shot panic. The bench upgrade economy and credit-scrap balance hold up with minimal tuning, and the new encounter placements rarely disrupt the pacing the original established.

Overall, this is an excellent, respectful remake that improves on its source at nearly every design turn, from the menacing lighting to the reworked geography and vastly more useful stasis and kinesis tools. PC performance issues, an awkward default control scheme and a no-title-screen boot are the costs, and the ship's disorienting layout remains an acquired taste. The atmosphere and the thoughtful rearrangement carry the recommendation, provided the hardware keeps pace.
