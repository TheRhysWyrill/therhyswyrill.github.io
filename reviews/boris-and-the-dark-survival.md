An abandoned animation studio, a top-down view and a prowling monster make for a compact hide-and-scavenge structure spun off from the Bendy universe. Each day is a randomly generated dungeon floor: collect the listed supplies (wrench, gear, book, radio), dodge a stalking pursuer, and escape via the elevator, with bacon soup serving as sprint fuel and lockers, toilets and little miracle stations as hiding spots. Picking up the final item alerts the stalker, turning every departure into a managed risk. The twisted-fairy-tale tone works, and the mobile heritage shows in both the compact scope and the simple interface.

The stalker roster is the best system in the game, and it is built almost entirely on audio. Each chaser, whether the Ink Demon, Alice, the Butcher Gang or the Projectionist, announces its presence with a distinct signature: heartbeat, humming, aura, footsteps. Dodging becomes a cat-and-mouse game played by ear rather than by sight, and the design rewards listening with an intensity few stealth games attempt. Alice's distant humming in particular is genuinely unsettling, exactly as intended. Identifying which pursuer is nearby by sound alone transforms routine scavenging into a constant state of low-grade alert.

The unlock loop gives the thin structure its longevity. Candles, padlock keys, dances and characters expand each successive day, and the collectible tracks double as a content-unlock system: each find introduces a new stalker or playable character, so progression and threat escalate together. Content updates layered in further variety, with the Wolf Trials, The Unleashed and Symphony of Shadows adding stalkers, characters and odd-fuse minigames such as pinball and breakout. Strategies emerge naturally: baiting a stalker's patrol direction, or leaving items staged near the elevator for a fast exit.

Audio logs carry the lore, and the writing is the reason to keep playing beyond the mechanics. Wally Franks' mopping tapes are the highlight, Miller's messages plead to be found, and the Sammy Lawrence dance notes add texture to the studio's decayed history. The missing subtitles for these recordings are an odd accessibility omission in a game that leans so hard on audio delivery, and the gap matters more the more the lore rewards attention. The tapes remain worth hearing regardless.

Honesty about the core loop arrives quickly: the supplies objective is largely a front, and once a single day has been survived, most of what the game offers has been seen. Random layouts generate dead-end corridors where being cornered means an unavoidable death, with no counterplay once the stalker closes the exit. Stuck-on-geometry jank around doors recurs constantly without ever breaking the run. The loop is basic by design, and the unlock layer exists precisely because the moment-to-moment play could not sustain interest alone.

The ending simply does not exist in the conventional sense. The fuses unlock a breakout-style minigame rather than a finale, no credits roll, and finding everything is treated as the ending, which lands as anti-climactic for anyone expecting closure. The RNG-heavy collectible hunt stretches the final scraps across multiple extra days, making the completionist path a pacing drag compounded by repetition. Chasing the last fuses exposes the loop's thinness at the exact moment the structure should be concluding.

Some of the unlockable characters are strictly worse than the default, with slower walking or no sprinting and little reason to choose them, diluting the value of the character-unlock track. A shuffle option for the stalker roster would also have served variety better than a fixed starting pursuer. These are small misses in a package whose ambitions are modest and whose price was always in proportion. The charm survives the shallow loop because the audio design, the lore and the escalating roster each pull in the same direction.

Overall, this is a brief, charming, lore-rich side-dish whose audio-driven stalker roster and unlock layer earn goodwill that the basic loop, dead-end unfairness, absent tape subtitles and non-existent ending only partially spend. Judged at its scale rather than against full-sized releases, the bark being bigger than the bite is a fair trade, and the universe it serves is better for the extra chapters it tells.
