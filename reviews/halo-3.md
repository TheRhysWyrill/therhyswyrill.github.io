The curtain-call volume of a trilogy announces itself immediately. Truth's broadcast menace, the Ark's reveal and a three-way war between humans, Covenant and Flood give the campaign an epic register its predecessors only built towards, and the humans-and-Elites alliance against a common foe lands as genuinely rousing rather than cynical. Allied Elites and Hunters fight alongside the player, Scarabs loom on the horizon, and the scale never stops escalating. The MCC remaster cleans up the 360 visuals, and the presentation still convinces nearly two decades on.

Vehicle set-pieces drive the pacing. Mongoose and ghost openers lead into the Voi highway warthog push, scorpion tank advances, a Spartan-laser beach defence, hornet-versus-Scarab dogfights and the final Warthog escape, and every one of them plays like a set piece the series had been rehearsing for two games. The Spartan laser is the arsenal's crown jewel, by a distance the most satisfying pickup in the sandbox, and the spiker and brute shot give the Covenant tools real texture. Skulls add a modifier layer that makes collection fun rather than chore-like.

The ten text terminals are the lore hook, and they genuinely engage. Librarian and Didact fragments seed a prequel storyline that later entries would build on, reading them aloud rewards the hunt, and one missed terminal even justifies a mid-run restart to collect. Skull hunting pairs with terminal hunting as a completionist loop, and the two together give the eleven missions a second pass worth taking. The Forerunner fragments are the game's quiet best writing.

The Flood levels are the campaign's emotional trough. Excessive and chaotic, particularly the flood-drones, the worst enemy variant in the series, the swarm sections bury their epic alliance framing under sheer volume, and the levels test patience before the Ark arc restores momentum. The slow-walk Cortana and Gravemind segments irritate in a different way: dialogue delivered while movement slows to a crawl, forcing the player to stand and listen when a radio line would do. Both complaints are real, and neither is fatal to the whole.

Grunt suicides account for a running trend of deaths, and the AI drivers are as unreliable as ever, wandering the longest route to anything. The flamethrower sets the player on fire along with everything else, equipment pickups like bubble shields, trip mines and regenerators go underused without encouragement, and the absence of modern conveniences like sprint dates the movement. The Voi wraith wall in particular demands repeated head-bashing attempts before it yields, the one stretch where the set-piece ambition outruns the tuning. None of this touches the combat loop, which holds up as well as any in the series.

Around four and a half to five hours covers the campaign, notably shorter than nostalgia insists, and the cast gives the finale its weight: Ron Perlman's Lord Hood, Johnson's send-off and the closing image land exactly as intended. The set-piece variety keeps every mission distinct, and the alliance framing gives even the Flood sections narrative purpose beneath the chaos. As a finale, the emotional beats all arrive on cue.

Overall, this is the trilogy's grand finale, with superb vehicle set-pieces, the series' best gun in the Spartan laser and genuinely engaging terminal lore, dragged down by excessive Flood levels, slow-walk dialogue and unreliable AI drivers. Shorter than remembered, still grand, and finished gladly enough to restart for a single missed terminal.
