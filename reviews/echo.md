A woman returns to the planet-sized palace she spent her life fleeing, dragging a cube containing a friend's remains and a sardonic AI across her back, chasing a ritual that promises resurrection. The premise is drip-fed rather than explained: resourcefuls, a breeding programme, and the flesh-to-cube translation all surface through fragmentary hints that trust the audience to assemble them. The palace itself is the atmosphere, with infinite gold-black corridors, blinding airlocks and cavernous throne rooms cycled through oppressive light-and-dark rhythms. No other game builds its world on the premise that the player is the enemy's only teacher.

That premise is the mechanic, and it remains unmatched. Whatever is done during a light cycle (opening doors, vaulting, sprinting, swimming, shooting, grabbing orbs, even sitting on thrones) the palace's echoes copy in the next cycle, and they forget anything left undone for a full cycle. Difficulty is therefore self-inflicted: every capability granted to the pursuers is a capability they were taught. The winning doctrine becomes self-denial, refusing to sprint, holstering the gun in the light, and abandoning water and glass orbs once the clones learn to wield them back. Stealth turns into a discipline of restraint, which is a genuinely novel verb.

The escalation re-shapes the rules repeatedly. Shambling half-formed echoes give way to sprinting, flashlit clones that aim and shoot, then to enemies that steal the very orbs used against them, and finally to one-hit-kill mech segments where the restrictions invert and the pursuers do whatever they please. A late revelation, that a distant rod-carrying robot can simply be sniped from range, rewards experimentation with a delightful shortcut the campaign had been brute-forcing. Each shift keeps the formula fresh, though the difficulty peaks push the margins hard.

Voice acting carries the narrative with only two performers, and the dynamic between the returning protagonist and her acerbic AI companion is sharp enough to sustain the entire journey. The AI's dry commentary lands as both guide and antagonist, and the finale's revelation, that the protagonist herself was the asset being manipulated all along, recontextualises the whole campaign on the ship's memory-set where the game began. The closing loop, a full-circle swap between hunter and hunted, pays off the premise thematically as well as structurally.

Technical state is the real caveat. A mid-section hang forces a level reload, and a resolution bug resets the display to the lowest setting every session, an oversight that must be corrected manually each time. The HUD compounds the friction with radar clutter so noisy that spotting pursuers ahead becomes harder than the mechanic itself demands. The opening hours remain deliberately slow, a pacing choice that tests patience before the first enemy contact finally ignites the premise. None of these flaws touch the design; all of them tax the experience.

Structure runs as a linear descent of orb-collection gauntlets between elevators, save arches and blackout resets, and the rhythm of light-cycle planning and blackout rebooting gives the progression a pulse that suits the palace's oppressive cycles. A legacy hard mode where enemies learn over two cycles rather than one adds replay depth for anyone who masters restraint too quickly. The learning curve, from confused newcomer to disciplined ghost, is the campaign's quietest achievement and its most durable one.

Overall, this remains a singular stealth experience in which the player is their own worst enemy, and eight years on nothing else has taken notes from it. The learn-unlearn core, the two-actor voice work and the palace's oppressive atmosphere all hold up completely. Stability problems, HUD noise and a stubbornly slow opening are the only costs, and none of them diminish what the mechanic achieves.
