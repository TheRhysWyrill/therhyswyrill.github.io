Space Quest 4 yanks its janitor hero from a bar on Magmetheus into a future ruled by a supercomputer possessed by the Vohaul virus, then spends the campaign hopping between its own sequels and prequels as playable eras. The satire is Sierra at its sharpest: software-store parodies of Loom, The Sims and King's Quest line the Galaxy Galleria mall, the Hertz So Good electronics catalogue rewards reading every page, and the game openly mocks its own low-resolution AGI segments with an era-appropriate insult. Full voice acting, a series first, elevates every line, and the sarcastic narrator delivers the roasts. As pure comedy writing it ranks among the series' funniest hours.

The time-pod travel structure binds the eras together. Each jump requires photographing and entering symbol codes, a mechanic that doubles as a soft-lock risk: failing to note what the code says at the wrong moment can strand a playthrough at the very beginning. The fan service lands regardless of nostalgia, with an in-engine recreation of the first game's AGI-era Ulence Flats, complete with a "Mr. VGA" gag, standing out as the cleverest bit of self-reference. The structure turns the campaign into a tour of the series' own history.

The Sequel Police are the campaign's weakest stretch. Their escape sequences, sewers and Skaterama alike, are the hardest part of the game by a long shot, and the hiding mechanic against patrolling droids and police feels unreliable compared with the series' first entry. Deaths in these chases feel like the game cheating rather than the player failing, and they sour an otherwise generous comedy. Frequent saving is the only sane policy, and the game demands it without apology.

The Galaxy Galleria mall restores goodwill almost immediately. The Monolith Burger job funds the campaign in buckazoids through a flipping minigame that escalates in speed and requires two hires to survive, a grind the joke hint book only partially offsets; that in-game purchase offers deliberately absurd fake answers with two real ones buried among them, a puzzle item parodying the genre's own merchandise. The Latex Babes of Estros extend the era-hopping, though a bizarre scenery-stuck bug on that world costs several minutes against a rock. The mall itself is a highlight; the police that roam it are not.

Technical roughness extends beyond the hiding mechanics. The blue-screen risk that can end an attempt entirely hangs over long stretches without saves, making disciplined saving part of the design whether the game admits it or not. Ambience, or the lack of it, compounds the austere presentation: the game is remarkably quiet between its vocal hits, giving the saga's noisiest chase sequences an odd stillness elsewhere. None of it dims the writing, but all of it dates the production's technical layer.

The endgame spikes the difficulty again. The Supercomputer's laser rooms hinge on a 300-degree coordinate keypad puzzle, the campaign's most satisfying logic test, and a final timed fistfight closes matters at pace. The final confrontation carries the Vohaul thread to its conclusion without letting the comedy slip, and the laser rooms balance the earlier chase unfairness with a puzzle that respects the player. Ambience is notably sparse throughout, a quiet production where the voice acting and music carry what environmental sound does not. The contrast between silence and the vocal track is noticeable, though never damaging.

Overall, Space Quest 4 is one of the funniest and most ambitious entries in the series, carried by parody writing, voice acting and era-hopping fan service. The Sequel Police escapes and unreliable hiding make it the roughest ride of the saga, and the soft-lock-prone code entry demands note-taking. Worth it for the writing, approached with frequent saves.
