A commute gone wrong opens Speed Limit, and the gauntlet that follows slams through a different arcade genre every few minutes with a commitment no other game quite matches. Side-scrolling shooting gives way to top-down car chases, motorbike weaving, a chopper stage with missiles, a jet stage with flare decoys and finally a multi-phase boss jet fight, each shift dragging the perspective with it. Everything is an instant kill except the boss's chain gun, a design choice that makes the loop brutal but fair, and dying well over a hundred times on Normal never stops the whole thing being fun. The neon-lit visuals glow throughout, with Tunnel Vision's aesthetic standing out as the campaign's coolest.

The frantic identity separates it from the obvious comparison. Where that other genre-splicing homage pays tribute to old arcade games at a relaxed pace, this one cranks the speed, and the difference reads as deliberate rather than derivative. The motorbike stage lands as the highlight, a Turbo Tunnel-style weaving gauntlet that cements the appeal, and the constant vehicle and perspective shifts are the star attraction even when they disorient. Perspective changes arrive without warning, and obstacles genuinely come out of nowhere during the jet stage, a disorientation built into the difficulty itself.

Easy mode earns its existence through smart tuning rather than mere lowering. Enemy density drops and checkpoints arrive more frequently, cutting a clear from over an hour to twenty-seven minutes, with the hardest stage collapsing from twenty-three minutes to two and a half. Arcade mode, where death restarts the full stage, is not fun to contemplate, and the sub-half-hour Normal achievement reads as near-impossibility, but the standard structure respects the die-and-retry loop it demands. Controls remap early, and the left stick runs imprecise enough for the bike stage that the experience hardens itself unnecessarily.

The final boss is the campaign's one significant sore spot. Dozens of phases, seventy-odd weapons and insta-kill attacks stack into a fight where dying sends progress back so far that nothing gets learned, and the subtle tells that do exist are too faint to telegraph the weapon changes. Every other stage teaches through repetition, while the boss simply grinds, and the fun drains precisely where the campaign should peak. No other part of the game commits this sin, which makes the miss harder to forgive.

The story is essentially absent, and the closing reveal, that the man handing over a gun at the start is the protagonist's own aged, dishevelled future self, lands as an amusingly unexpected time-loop punchline. Achievement-based storytelling adds personality beyond the genre-splicing, with the final boss's arrival communicated through a paint-job achievement rather than a cutscene. The unlockable Neon mode confuses more than it delights, an endless driving sequence with no shooting and no clear purpose, running like a time-limit-free cruise that exists for its own sake.

Roughly an hour of repeated deaths clears Normal, a length the pacing justifies completely, and the arcade authenticity survives the friction intact. The commitment to slam-dunking through genres every few minutes never relents, and the lovingly frantic energy carries even the stages that disorient. As a short, loud arcade experience, the package knows exactly what it wants to be.

Overall, Speed Limit is a frantic, genre-hopping arcade gauntlet whose neon throwbacks, perspective-shifting stages and die-and-retry design deliver a lovingly frantic hour, with the multi-phase final boss as the sole real misstep, saving its worst stretch for last. Everything before it is sick in the best arcade sense, and the time-loop punchline closes the commute with a grin.
