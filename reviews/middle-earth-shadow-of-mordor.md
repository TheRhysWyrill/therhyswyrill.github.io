A ranger bound to an elven wraith hunts Sauron's captains across Mordor, and the revenge framing matters less than the machine built around it. Structurally the campaign runs about twenty main missions, but the captain hierarchy, collectible artifacts, Ithildin memories and both DLC chapters stretch a completionist route far beyond that spine. Tolkien references thread through the side material, from Gollum encounters to Saruman nods, and the outcast story beats turn genuinely dark in places. The narrative serves the systems rather than the reverse, and the systems hold up.

Combat chains from enemy to enemy through executions and counters with a fluidity that has aged better than almost anything else in its cohort. For a release now more than a decade old, the moment-to-moment play still feels contemporary, and the counter window is tuned tightly enough that higher-tier fights demand attention without punishing flow. Stealth complements the melee: trailing captains, ledge kills and silent brandings of tight-knit patrols reward reading routines before striking, and pulling off an undetected chain against a guarded group delivers the game's most satisfying sequences.

The Nemesis system remains the singular, never-imitated hook. Named captains remember encounters, gain power, return from death, hold feuds and climb a hierarchy that gives every hunt personal texture. Weaknesses and fears can be exploited through intel gathering, turning a random patrol into a planned assassination, and an unkillable-feeling tracker who keeps resurfacing across the map builds exactly the kind of grudge the design intends. Nothing since has replicated it, and its confinement to these two games remains one of the medium's stranger squandered ideas.

Branding transforms the entire structure once unlocked. Dominating a captain folds him into a personal army, and the ability compounds until effectively all of Sauron's forces answer to the wraith's will, converting the endgame into a revenge fantasy where the enemy roster becomes the player's roster. Stealth-branding undetected groups compounds the power trip, and the whole economy of fear and loyalty reorganises itself around a mechanic that arrives roughly two-thirds in.

Traversal splits the verdict. Speed boosts and tower climbing generate genuine momentum across the two maps, yet the climbing itself is finicky to the point of being the game's most annoying trait: grip points refuse to register unless the approach angle is exact, and ledges get missed mid-run for no visible reason. Crowd combat degrades under numbers, with targeting a specific foe in a mob becoming an exercise in dodge-roll spam. Hunters, with their constant ranged attacks, rank as the least pleasant enemy type, and persistent enemy spawning keeps interrupting stealth work by dropping fresh soldiers into cleared zones.

The PC port carries a notorious trap: without the free HD content pack, base textures look worse than the console versions, an inversion that catches anyone who skips the download. A bugged dagger challenge, in which torchbearers fail to spawn, soured one of the stealth chains and forced repeated abandoned retries, standing as the worst mission in the set. Power progression is uneven too, scarce in the early hours and meaningless late as unused runes accumulate without a sink.

The Bright Lord chapter locks a half health bar and branding-heavy objectives into a shorter arc whose variety thins to defend-and-attack loops, wearing out its welcome by a finale built around a revive loop; Lord of the Hunt fares better on the strength of Torvin's banter and a Graug-riding finale, making it the clear preference between the two. Overall, the counter combat, the Nemesis hierarchy and the branding power fantasy carry a campaign whose traversal friction, spawn pressure and one broken mission never come close to sinking it, and a systems showcase this durable earns its place on its mechanics alone.
