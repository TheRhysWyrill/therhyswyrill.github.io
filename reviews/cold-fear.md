A US Coast Guard boarding party answers a distress call from a Russian whaler adrift in the Bering Sea, and finds the Star of Sakhalin overrun by things that should not be moving. That premise alone would place the game among the era's more atmospheric survival horror efforts, but the storm does most of the talking before any parasite shows its face. Rain hammers the deck, waves heel the vessel from side to side, and every corridor, ladder and catwalk shifts with the swell. The B-movie framing, in which a cartel-funded research operation has engineered something called ExoCell out of human subjects, is played with a straight face and a stack of collectible documents. It is closer to a creature feature than a exercise in dread, and it makes no apology for that.

The defining idea is the ship itself. Camera, character animation and level geometry all answer to the rolling deck: a Coast Guard officer wades along corridors pitched at odd angles, braces against rails, and loses footing on stairwells as the hull rocks. Whole sections play out across the open deck in driving rain and near-zero visibility, where one-hit-kill storm waves sweep across the walkways and electrified floors punish careless routes below decks. No other survival horror of the period committed this hard to a moving environment, and the effort shows in every creaking bulkhead. It is the sort of single, unifying conceit that lesser productions would reserve for one showcase level; here it is the entire game.

Combat follows the classic mould: fixed camera angles, tank-ish movement and over-the-shoulder aiming against parasites, invisible shades and reanimated corpses. The arsenal climbs steadily from pistol and shotgun through AK, MP5, speargun, grenade launcher and flamethrower, with the AK proving the workhorse and the shotgun ending most close encounters outright. Supply design is unusually forgiving: medkit vending machines and ammo restock rooms keep the pressure off, a welcome counterweight to enemies that collapse and despawn unless looted quickly. Environmental puzzles (codes, valves, electrical boxes, retinal scanners) punctuate the killing, and objectives such as finding the antidote, destroying the generators and planting the C4 give the campaign a working structure.

Inconsistent checkpoints are the campaign's most punishing habit. Some save points arrive two minutes apart; others abandon an entire chapter with no save at all, which turns late-game mistakes into long replays even though the same system hands out strategic saves before the hardest fights. The resistance bar compounds the trouble by doubling as a stamina meter, and it serves almost no purpose beyond being drained during grapples, at which point it has already done its damage. Smaller frictions pile up alongside: interaction prompts that work intermittently, no crouching while moving, no map of the ship's maze-like decks, and head-stomping unavailable on stairs, where the downed corpses tend to collect.

The escort-mission segment, in which a rescued survivor must be walked through hostile decks, is the design's weakest stretch, demanding babysitting through exactly the kind of cramped, camera-hostile geometry tank controls handle worst. A single late-game room outdoes everything else for sheer malice: one invisible enemy, trip wires and explosive barrels scattered across the floor, and a single wrong step ending the attempt. Its difficulty is real but its construction is not, and it stands out precisely because the rest of the level design, storm-swept and disorienting by intent, is usually fair about it.

The two-phase final boss caps the campaign unevenly. The first phase is a competent duel; the second devolves into badly tuned chaos that sends the death count climbing long after the story has said its piece. That sour note aside, the lore scattered through diary pages and documents holds up, charting experiments on humans, the cartel's involvement and a scientist's descent, and the storm-lashed atmosphere of rain, wind and flickering lights never relents. The lineage is audible too: the same studio behind Alone in the Dark, and the comparison fits.

Overall, this is a mechanically dated but genuinely distinctive slice of survival horror that was judged harshly on release largely for sharing a month with Resident Evil 4, and the label never suited it. The swaying ship remains an idea no contemporary rival attempted, and the generous economy, document-driven conspiracy and creature-feature tone carry a short campaign a long way. Judge it beside its own generation rather than its famous rival and it earns its rehabilitation.
