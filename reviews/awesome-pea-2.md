A pea on another coin-hunting platforming jaunt, capped by a jokey ending card, is the whole framing, and the atmosphere is carried almost entirely by the music. Per-stage tracks, later clarified as per-object-set, give the soundtrack a genuine identity, with the cave levels supplying a personal favourite. The pea's deadpan straight-faced expression while the player dies repeatedly is the running joke, and it lands every time. The soundtrack is the game's defining strength, enjoyable enough to hum between deaths. The chiptune-adjacent score is better than the first game's throughout.

The double jump is the sequel's headline addition and a genuine quality-of-life win, saving countless falls off ledges where the first game would have ended a run. Coins doubling as route markers is an elegant design touch, guiding the double jump to ledges the eye might miss. Level variety is a clear improvement, with no reused backdrops across the twenty-five levels, and the full-coin collection per stage gates one achievement per stage, discovered mid-run. The game is easier to fully collect than its predecessor despite being slightly longer. The core loop of precision platforming with route-guiding coins is genuinely well judged.

Difficulty is front-loaded with spikes, saw blades, bats and cannon walls, and the every-five-levels boss or tower stages from the first game are replaced with harder gauntlet stages. One mid-late gauntlet became a personal wall, with over fifteen minutes of deaths and a dwindling life counter reaching the point where entertainment expires. The game is spiteful rather than badly designed, and the intent is legible in every hazard. A skip button would have helped at exactly that moment. The bats remain a franchise nemesis worth dreading.

The double jump cuts both ways: it saves constantly but also kills, tempting risky jumps that the design punishes, and the mixed feelings are earned. Duplicate levels disappoint, with the finale repeating level 23 with minor changes and an earlier near-copy making one stretch trivially easy. Bullets appear to spawn from nowhere with no telegraph, which reads as cheap in a precision game. Edge-detection glitches occasionally drop the pea off ledges without intent, and saw-blade hitboxes swing from generous to unforgiving. The option menu downgrades from the first game, unable to keep scanlines without the tube effect, and level numbering starting from zero are minor oddities.

Roughly an hour and eighteen minutes covers all twenty-five levels in a single sitting, a slight increase over the first game's runtime, and every achievement falls in the same run. The gauntlet stage's difficulty spike is the one genuine design misjudgement, and the duplicate finale is the clearest structural criticism. Neither dents the core loop, which is tight and legible. The coin-as-marker idea deserves to be stolen by better-funded games, and the identity-per-object-set music scheme gives even repeated hazards a fresh coat. The franchise's signature spite is intact, for better and worse.

The jokey "will he return?" ending card lands exactly right for a game this self-aware, and the score's per-object-set structure means the cave theme arrives as a genuine welcome change late in the run. The budget package hides a genuinely competent audio direction. The option-menu downgrade and zero-indexed level numbering are the sort of quirks that only a first-time player would notice, and the score's identity carries through every death. The core loop never overstays. The spite is the point.

Overall, a fun, frustrating, musically excellent micro-platformer that improves on the first game in the ways that matter, with the double jump, coin route markers and varied backdrops as the wins. Spite-placed hazards, one genuinely awful gauntlet level, duplicate finale padding and inconsistent saw hitboxes are honest costs, and the soundtrack alone justifies the trip for anyone who likes their precision platformers with personality.
