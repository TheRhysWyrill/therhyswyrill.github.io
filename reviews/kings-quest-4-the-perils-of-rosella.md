A princess sails for the distant land of Tamir carrying a single desperate hope: return with the magic fruit before her ailing father succumbs, and free a fairy queen from the tyrant who holds her power. That dual quest drives one of the most lavishly produced adventures of its era, a 1988 release that used its new SCI engine to pair a ten-minute cinematic intro with full narrator descriptions and, uniquely for the series at the time, a female protagonist. The fairy-tale furniture is all present, from frog princes to dwarven mines, but the tone is warmer and more character-driven than its peers. The writing is the engine's high-water mark: tombstone epitaphs, genie jokes and banter that still surprises nearly four decades on.

The quest chain itself feels fair rather than cryptic. The frog prince opens matters with genuine fairy-tale charm, the seven dwarves' diamond mine follows, and a Shakespeare-to-loot trade with a minstrel establishes the game's barter logic. The fisherman's fishing pole, Cupid's bow and arrow, and the golden bridle that tames a unicorn each slot into place with an internal consistency that rewards attention. Three errands for the villain, involving the unicorn, the ogre's hen and Pandora's box, escalate cleanly toward the finale. Nothing here demands obscene lateral thinking; most solutions read as reasonable once seen.

The graveyard sequence is the emotional centrepiece, a haunted house whose five ghosts each require a specific offering: a rattle, a locket, a medal and a toy horse among them. It lands as the most affecting stretch of the game, and also the most tedious, a room-by-room chain that tests patience as much as sentiment. The writing keeps pulling its weight through the tedium, which is the only reason the sequence earns its length.

Parser-era friction defines the interface. Every interaction is typed, hotspots are pixel-perfect, and the ladder controls run counter to intuition, demanding up where instinct says down. The score system tracks thoroughness in the classic Sierra manner, rewarding players who examine and take everything. Disciplined saving is not optional, and a walkthrough is nearly mandatory for one trap in particular: the magic fruit, the quest's central objective, can simply be eaten, and the game offers no warning that doing so makes completion impossible. It is the cruelest single piece of design here.

Traversal is the other recurring hazard. Swimming to Tamir and crossing the swamp both demand repeated attempts, landmarks blur together, and the whale encounter punishes hesitation, since lingering too long turns an opportunity into a shark-infested death. Several runs end in the water before the correct approach clicks. None of it is unfair by the era's standards, but it is unforgiving in ways modern releases abandoned for good reason.

Smaller quirks accumulate at the margins. A tiara can be picked up entirely by accident, a mid-game frame-rate jump betrays the engine's age, and the crown timer forces an extended wait while seven dwarves finish eating their soup, an imposition that tests goodwill precisely when pacing should be tightening. These are blemishes rather than wounds, and none undermines what the surrounding craft achieves.

What endures is the combination of production values and emotional generosity. The never-remade status of this entry leaves it standing alone as both the series' SCI showpiece and its least modernised work, and the age shows chiefly in interface conventions rather than in the storytelling. The finale closes warmly, and a hat motif carries cleanly through to the closing title card of the following game.

Overall, this remains a genuinely fun and surprisingly fair Sierra classic whose writing, pacing and fairy-tale charm hold up far better than its parser and pixel-hunting suggest. The edible-fruit soft-lock, the shark-infested swims and the graveyard's room-by-room tedium are real, era-authentic obstacles. As the one entry the fan-remake teams never touched, it stands alone, and it deserves to.
