A freelance operative is framed for a murder he did not commit, and clearing his name means breaking into corporate buildings, erasing evidence and rewiring the infrastructure against its owners. The tone is noir-comedy played with total conviction: corporate satirical emails, an absurd bullfrog-trousers introduction, and press-conference gags that skewer security-camera prejudice. The writing stays sharp from opening to epilogue, and the satire lands because the espionage scaffolding around it is real.

The Crosslink is the headline system. Dragging connections between lights, doors, sockets, cameras and alarms across colour-coded circuits turns every building into a rewirable machine, with new circuit colours unlocking progressively across the campaign. Turning a guard's own light switch against him, opening a door remotely to funnel patrols, or disabling an alarm before it ever sounds: the creativity is systemic rather than scripted, and the first use alone justifies the purchase.

Hyper-trousers supply the physics. Chargeable Superman leaps, wall-climbing and silent glass-smashing drops make traversal an emergent toy in its own right, and the combination with rewiring produces solutions the designers likely never enumerated. Leaping through a second-floor window after rerouting the lights below is a repeatable delight, and the movement never stops being funny. Few systems pair this cleanly with a puzzle layer.

The noir frame earns its comedy through specificity. A security-camera racism press-conference gag, a hat-fancier opening and an endless stream of corporate emails mocking tech-industry self-regard give every mission a satirical spine, and side-jobs dispatch the operative into tangents (surveillance, theft, corporate double-cross) that flesh out the conspiracy without padding the campaign. Dialogue options stay dry and reactive throughout, never breaking the deadpan, and the supporting cast sketches each contact in a few exchanged lines. The writing does as much systemic work as the wiring.

Guards patrol with vision cones and react to light, sound and locked doors, and the stealth layer respects non-lethal play entirely. Mission payouts and side-job emails fund upgrades: jump strength, charge time, drop-shot silence and kick doors, with the unlockable Gate Crash kick reserved for the finale. A-grades reward careful work, and side-jobs like the fake-prototype double-cross deepen the corporate comedy while padding the upgrade economy.

Branching gives the story genuine weight. Two finale routes (Jackson versus Rook) hinge on an unedited recording, and the choice plays as a real dilemma rather than a dialogue token. The bittersweet blog-post epilogue that closes one route earns its register without sentimentality. That the game supports both outcomes with distinct epilogues gives the decision consequence without inflating its length.

Not every gadget earns its slot. Rewiring enemy guns and booby traps surface as purchasable options whose value stays unclear, and several upgrades read as impulse buys the campaign never demands. The final Ops-Centre mission compounds the friction with time pressure and finicky sound-detector wiring, a stretch where the otherwise forgiving systems tighten without warning. And a late-game gag literally launches the system web browser, a joke whose execution startles more than it amuses.

Those frictions are small against what the design accomplishes. The Crosslink plus hyper-trousers combination remains genuinely unique, the noir-comedy writing stays consistently funny, and the campaign respects its own short runtime by ending before the ideas thin. Emergent solutions keep surfacing even in the final missions, and the game never explains its systems because discovery is the point.

Overall, this is a razor-sharp stealth puzzler whose rewiring creativity, hyper-trouser physics and noir-comedy conviction deliver constant delight across a tight campaign. The under-explained gadgets and the finicky finale section are minor costs against a design this inventive, and the branching epilogues close the story with more consequence than its length suggests possible.
