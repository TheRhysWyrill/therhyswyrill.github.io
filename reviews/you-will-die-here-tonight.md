A strike team storms an abandoned upstate New York mansion to apprehend a family-killer, and the classic furniture of that premise is rendered with obvious affection: redacted briefings, diaries of a doomed household, filing-cabinet lore and a parasite-experiments thread running beneath the gothic pulp. A pre-mission headquarters sequence of desk banter, a pet rat and a suspiciously generous cupcake sets the pastiche register immediately, and a permanently zinger-quipping teammate keeps the tone wickedly funny throughout. It wears its old-school influences down to the uniforms, and the homage lands as craftsmanship rather than borrowing.

The presentation is the signature achievement: down-resed top-down exploration in the Diablo vein that seamlessly snaps into first-person light-gun combat the moment a weapon is drawn. Aiming tightens the crosshair at the cost of time, grabs demand frantic mashing, and headshots carve satisfying chunks out of enemies, a House of the Dead cadence bolted onto a mansion crawl with no seams showing. The pairing of the two perspectives is executed so cleanly that switching stops feeling like a gimmick and starts feeling like the correct way for this kind of horror to exist.

Permadeath is the structural heart: when a character falls, the next chosen survivor inherits the dead member's ammo and gear, and the loop continues until the true ending recontextualises the whole system. Dying is not a failure state here but a required story beat, and the design is honest enough to make every death feel like plot rather than punishment. K coins can reset everyone alive through a dining-room photo button, a merciful touch inside a deliberately lethal loop, and the title proves literal with commendable commitment.

The mansion puzzles are pure classic survival horror in the best way: a book-shelf cipher, an 1836 safe, a 4596 padlock in the alchemy lab, a pool-ball and chess-notation shield code, a butterfly and bumblebee door, and an animal-head Magnum puzzle. Weapon tuning kits upgrade pistol, shotgun and Magnum into genuinely different tools, and the fully tuned one-shot pistol turns the second loop into a power fantasy of the first order. Traps, boulders and a lightning-rod pond keep exploration lethal, punishing curiosity so consistently that it reads as a design language, and learning it is the campaign's real arc.

Friction concentrates in the boss encounters, which are badly under-explained: the shield Knight, the spider and the final Catherine duel all dispense enormous damage with no legible telegraphing, and the practical answer, a shotgun disruption philosophy, only becomes apparent near the end. First aid kits are scarce and, worse, do not transfer between characters, so a late run led by an early casualty's successors fights under-healed through no fault of its own. Both are the kind of opaque friction a thorough manual would dissolve.

The map is small and a full loop plus true ending runs about three hours, though the second pass rewards knowledge with dramatic speed rather than padding the clock. A post-credits freeze required a relaunch to reach the title screen, the campaign's only genuine technical blemish. Neither the modest scale nor the crash dents what the loop accomplishes, and the compactness suits the murder-mansion format.

Overall, a bite-sized survival horror whose seamless two-perspective presentation, required-death structure and lovingly constructed mansion puzzles make every demise feel earned, with under-telegraphed bosses and non-transferring first aid kits as the sharpest of its small edges. It is a compact, clever homage that turns permadeath into narrative, and it fully delivers on its title.
