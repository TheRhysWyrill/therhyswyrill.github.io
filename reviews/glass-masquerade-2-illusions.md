Glass Masquerade 2: Illusions is stained-glass jigsaw assembly where the puzzle is the artwork: completing a board paints a picture rather than revealing a photograph, and each finished piece clicks into a contemplative whole. Cryptic riddles frame the collection, dream-within-dream loops and blind-man fables delivered after each board, while the flavour text riffs on witches, vampires, sirens, kitsune and Lovecraftian horrors. The writing is amusing nonsense in the best sense, atmosphere carried entirely by the stained-glass art and the mood it sustains. There is no time limit anywhere, and the absence is deliberate: the whole design protects its chill. It is a puzzle game that rarely raises its voice.

Piece placement is permissive by design. The ballpark forgiveness accepts placements anywhere near the correct spot rather than demanding precision, a leniency that proves essential when playing with a controller, and the progression rewards it with a branching map where solved puzzles grant keys that unlock further boards. Dead-end puzzles grant shards instead, eight per dead end and eighty-eight in total, feeding a finale built on an 88-piece box-art clown puzzle. The structure turns completion into a gentle economy rather than a wall.

Piece counts scale from 30 to 88, yet size is a poor predictor of difficulty; a 60-piece board can outlast an 80-piece one without explanation. Non-circular frames (hearts, diamonds, clocks, arches) double as difficulty levers, and every piece shape in the game is apparently unique, an accomplishment easy to miss until a late board proves it. Auto-rotation on Normal keeps the focus on placement, while Hard disables it entirely, roughly tripling the expected time by the game's own tooltips.

The repetition of picking up the same piece over and over is the core complaint, an instinctive loop born of forgetting which shape is which, and a colour change on pickup would fix most of it. Inconsistent placement forgiveness compounds the trouble: sometimes distant placements land, sometimes near pixel-perfection is required, and the shifting standard scrambles expectations. Early sessions suffer further from an awful piece-tray scrolling system until the right-click alternative reveals itself. None of the friction is fatal, but all of it is felt.

Hard mode confuses more than it challenges. Toggling it produces no visible change for a long stretch, and the eventual discovery that it only disables auto-rotation explains little; the mode reads as a toggle without a tutorial. Black outlines blending into backgrounds occasionally hide where a piece belongs, causing missed placements that the game offers no hint system to resolve. The UI layer is the one part of the production that feels unfinished.

The DLC sets (Temptations, Revelations, Scary Folk) supply themed monsters with short epilogue blurbs, folklore ranging from Slavic myth to yokai, and the Jorōgumo entry alone teaches something new. The sets are smaller, framed differently and faster than the base boards, and they energise the back half considerably. A relaxing, if limited, soundtrack underpins everything, pleasant even in its brevity. Together they extend a game that already earns its completion.

Overall, Glass Masquerade 2: Illusions is a meditative, pressure-free jigsaw whose forgiving placement, unique piece shapes and folkloric framing reward long, calm sessions. The pickup-loop repetition and inconsistent tolerance are real irritations, yet both feel like habits the design could forgive rather than flaws that define it. A well-crafted chill game that justifies completing every board.
