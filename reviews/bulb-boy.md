A bulb-headed boy, his dog and his grandfather live quietly in a bulb-lit house until a dark force possesses the old man, sending the boy through possessed animals and eventually inside the grandfather's own body to save him. The premise is pure dream-logic, and the game commits: a gluttonous chicken that eats its own head, a turd monster that fights back, an angry strawberry, an undead chicken requiring hide-under-the-pig stealth and a maggot-pie bake. Gross-out horror-comedy is the register, and laughs outnumber scares throughout. Even the moon is a bulb, upside down but recognisably part of the same glowing world. It is bizarre in the best possible way.

The electric-bulb headlight mechanic is the design's cleverest idea. Illuminating objects in the dark highlights hidden interactables, turning the light itself into a puzzle verb, and discovering the trick mid-run recontextualises every dark screen that came before. The art style leans green-tinted monochrome with expressive, rubbery character animation, and the bulb-headed protagonist communicates emotion entirely through glow. It is a distinctive look that no other game quite shares.

Character possession and body-hopping keep the mechanics varied. Playing as the dog, the grandfather, a spider and even worms changes the movement and interaction vocabulary, with each segment carrying its own comic logic; the dog's tree-related behaviour is a highlight of absurd storytelling. The segments are uneven in quality, and the spider section's speed feels more chaotic than fun, but the variety itself sustains a runtime of roughly ninety minutes. A multi-stage boss chain (possessed hand, chicken, toilet monster, worm gauntlet, plant boss, internal finale) gives the whole piece structural spine.

Generous checkpoints and instant respawns remove all sting from the constant deaths. Dying, especially in the worm gauntlet where deaths come easy, costs nothing but a second of reload, and the design clearly anticipates frequent failure as part of the rhythm. This forgiving framing lets the gross-out gags stay funny rather than frustrating. It is the single most important design decision in the game.

The sparse interactables cut both ways. Very few objects on screen can actually be engaged, which makes the item logic obscure compared with genre norms, and a joke-adjacent inventory (never-ending, stocked with disposable knives of mysterious nature) underlines how little the game cares about conventional hinting. Trial-and-error item testing is the expected approach. Elegant for some, obtuse for others, and never quite either.

The final encounter's teeth sequence is the game's one real sore spot. Point-and-click movement is approximate by nature, and a finale demanding fine, timing-based dodging with minimal movement precision collides with the genre's own controls, producing deaths at literally every point on the teeth. Overshooting clicks compounds the imprecision. It is the sole sequence that betrays the game's own interface, and a simpler timing challenge would have closed the campaign cleanly.

Overall, this is a short, charming ninety-minute oddity whose bulb-light puzzles, body-swapping variety, generous checkpoints and committed gross-out comedy earn their keep, with one teeth-dodging finale that fights the genre's controls as its only real sin. The mustache-shaped ending beat lands with warm absurdity. A distinctive little horror-comedy well worth the evening it asks for.
