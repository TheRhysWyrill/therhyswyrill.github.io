A schoolgirl is summoned into a ruined castle and told to slay the Dark Lady's followers to recover her sealed memories. Lost Ruins wraps that gothic premise in some of the most detailed pixel art around, with lighting and reflections doing the heaviest aesthetic lifting from the opening minutes onward. Journals from the summoning sorceress drip-feed the truth behind the summoning, and a tongue-in-cheek cast of side characters carries the tone: a meat-hunting warrior, a doomed goblin, a katana-obsessed schoolgirl, an astral-projected aristocrat. The ruined castle sprawls across a metroidvania-shaped map, and the visual identity alone makes the campaign worth the entry price.

The no-contact-damage design is the mechanical foundation. Moving through enemies costs nothing, which makes the early game brutal but fair, and it opens the door to the discovery that transforms the whole campaign: the Shield of Life. Blocking with it heals instead of protecting, and the shield reflects projectiles besides, turning a punishing game into a walk through ninety per cent of the roster. What first reads as impossibly hard becomes a power fantasy, and the build is the emergent joy at the centre of the design.

The equipment matrix is enormous. Weapons, artifacts and outfits with passive effects (the schoolgirl underwear set among them) stack into builds that reshape encounters, and consumable cooking adds another layer of preparation. Bleed and poison status builds reward sustained pressure, and rebound-kill setups with the throwing hammer and cannon turn projectile physics into weapons. Teleporting mirrors and portals knit the castle together, while one-shot statue saves anchor progress without healing or respawning, a design choice that keeps every save meaningful and every death costly.

The early game pulls no punches. The first slime-girl boss is punishing to the point of near-defeat, entering with a sliver of health and surviving on luck, and the difficulty registers as almost unfair before the systems click. Once learned, the game settles dramatically, with later bosses trivialised by the shield build, and the curve reads as intentional: rough while the ropes are being learned, generous once mastery arrives. The optional snow-witch provides the campaign's real optional challenge, and the Dark Lady's domain closes the roster with appropriate spectacle.

Yuri's boss fight is the run's only genuinely resented stretch. Control-reversing confusion scrambles inputs at the worst moments, and the blocking is inconsistent, with some identical attacks blockable and others passing straight through. The inconsistency reads as a bug rather than a design choice, and the frustration it produces is the sharpest note in an otherwise well-tuned campaign. Everything else in the boss roster respects the player's tools; this one fights the interface instead.

MP never regenerates naturally without late artifacts, which hobbles the magic system the game otherwise clearly wants used. The spell roster exists, the curiosity exists, and the mana economy strangles both, forcing a shield-and-melee default that ignores half the equipment matrix. Save points that neither heal nor respawn compound the resource pressure. The Sedora quest, forbidding golden chests until the end, rewards that restraint with a late-game treasure bonanza, and clearing every quest plus all thirteen chests earns the treasure-hunter title honestly.

The campaign runs about six and a half hours to full completion, with the credit sequence earning genuine admiration for its presentation. The gothic register never slips into self-parody, the summoned-schoolgirl premise resolves its mystery with a choice that fits the tone, and the difficulty curve, once understood, is entirely player-negotiable. A girls-with-guns-and-frying-pans identity gives the whole package its personality.

Overall, this is a gorgeous, punishing action platformer that rewards patience, experimentation and one gloriously broken shield, with the pixel-art aesthetic and the emergent block-healing build carrying a campaign that turns brutality into a power fantasy. Yuri's inconsistent fight and the mana-starved spells are blemishes, but everything else works, and the credit sequence alone deserves its praise.
