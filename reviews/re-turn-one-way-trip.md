Five friends camp before adult life pulls them apart, an earthquake hits, and a derelict rusting train becomes the stage for a tightly woven ghost story. Saki wakes alone in a ruined car with bodies, a swinging noose behind a locked cabin and an eight-year-old's ghost leaving clues, and the mystery deepens when the narrative shifts to a brand-new 1940s wartime train carrying the same locomotive decades earlier. A composer's secret love for a woman promised to the janitor, jealousy, an illicit affair, a hanging and an air-raid bombing tie the two timelines together. Few indie horror games braid two timelines of the same train through a single mystery with this much care, and the final beat is genuinely poignant rather than scary.

The dual-timeline structure is the game's real engine. Understanding the 1940s love triangle retroactively reframes every present-day scare, and the moment the player realises they are invisible to the wartime cast is a genuine hook. The narrative twist that recontextualises the monster and the girl's role lands with real weight, and the flowers-and-doll gesture at the end gives the ghosts a tenderness the genre rarely attempts. What begins as a simple ghost story becomes a tragedy about regret, and the payoff earns the four-and-a-half-hour runtime. The dread of the boarded cabins and hanging bodies holds throughout.

Puzzle design is where the game most consistently lands. The onion-skin overlay drawers using Teru Teru Bōzu dolls, the piano-note melodies, the mini-safe mazes, the mask-on-door reflection gags and the wedding-doll display that swaps heads are all distinct, fair and satisfying. None of the puzzles are terrible to figure out, and the item-combination inventory logic (rusty knife to shears, shears to cut curtains, paint thinner to reveal hidden writing) keeps progression tactile. Chapter structure jumps between the ruined present and the pristine 1940s with confident pacing. The puzzle-first design is the game's true genre.

The horror label is overstated. There is almost no threat, a couple of early jump scares and a single chase, and the manual save candles never earn their keep in a game with no fail states. The design is unusually kind, which reads as odd at first but probably fine once the game's real genre is accepted. The absence of mechanical danger means the dread is entirely narrative, and the scares that do arrive are sparse. The horror fan expecting tension will not find it, but the puzzle adventurer will find a warmer, cleverer game than the label promises.

One mid-game scare nearly broke the run in a different sense: discarding the crowbar appears to soft-lock the game, and the heart-sink moment of being three and a half hours deep with no path forward is real. A generous two-person wall crack resolves it, and the relief is enormous, but the design should never allow that scare in the first place. Some numbers in the Teru Teru Bōzu drawer puzzle are ambiguous enough to cause second-guessing. Neither issue is fatal, and both are outweighed by the puzzle craft. The ending's all-chapters-cleared return to her friends is a satisfying close, and the series has a clear origin story worth continuing.

The save candles sit at shrines across both trains, and the flashlight cone does honest atmospheric work in the derelict cars. Audio cues carry the early dread, and the boarded cabins hide the hanging bodies until the worst possible moment. The campfire prologue earns its foreshadowing, and the earthquake's aftermath sells the isolation. What looks like a horror game on the store page plays like a locked-room story with ghosts, and that mismatch is the only real barrier to enjoyment.

Overall, a warm, clever puzzle adventure that trades scares for story, with the onion-skin and piano puzzles and the two-train timeline structure as the standout strengths. The absent threat and redundant manual saves are honest costs, but the tender final payoff makes the trip worth taking for anyone who arrives expecting puzzles first and horror second.
