A satirical near-future city under siege by the Burner gang provides the stage, and the story is told almost entirely through Channel 7 News broadcasts that evolve as the campaign progresses. Public approval of the T-Zero unit collapses, scandals pile up around the mayor and Commander Wolf, and the escalation runs all the way to a WMD threat, with corporate employer Shift It planted as the structural surprise. The tone is deliberately over-the-top zero-tolerance satire, GTA-adjacent in its media framing, and the enemy dialogue leans into absurd vulgarity with memorable commitment. Few games before or since have committed this thoroughly to a reactive fake-news frame.

The shield-based combat loop is the heart of the game and it is ridiculous in the best way. Advancing behind an almost-invulnerable riot shield, bashing enemies, arresting leaders with the stun gun for bonus medals and disarming the rest to steal their weapons never stops being satisfying. The medic companions with limited heal packs keep the difficulty fair for most of the campaign, and the loop stays engaging from the first mission to the safe-house finale. It is the rare mechanical hook strong enough to carry an entire game on its own.

The unlock and medal progression genuinely changes how the game plays. Better weapons, more ammo, shotgun and assault rifle upgrades, riot grenades, a grenade launcher and extra clips all arrive through skilled play and optional missions, and the upgrades have a tangible effect on damage output and capacity. Head shots, arrests and incapacitations form two medal tracks per mission, with hidden masks feeding a third layer. The system rewards engagement at every step, and the sense of tangible growth is constant across the campaign.

The replay value is rewarding in principle but frustrating in execution, with arrest quotas like thirty per mission reading as insane and the mask collectible hunt abandoned partway through. The six optional emergency VIP-rescue missions unlock the best prototype weapons but are structurally identical: a five-minute time limit, fight to the hostage, fight back, with only the location changing. Late-game magnum-wielding shield enemies deal absurd damage, dropping half a health bar through a heal, and read as outright unfair. The subway mission's instant-execution hostage ambush behind double doors is such a dirty trick to pull, even if a fresh run softens the memory.

Mission variety is decent where it counts: burning apartments with the thermal breather, a moving-train defence, a helicopter crash, a crane-scale dockyard and a skyscraper climb each supply distinct textures. The sewers emergency mission simply sucks, and NPC pathing failures in the apartment fire strand trapped civilians in loops that block completion. Ladder climbing is iffy, and the low default aim sensitivity with auto-centre is best disabled immediately. The campaign runs roughly three hours, shorter than expected, and the finale re-energises after the mid-game friction. As Rocksteady's only non-superhero game, its cult status is well earned.

The shield is both armour and weapon, and the game never forgets it. The stun gun's arrest bonus incentivises risk over comfort, and the thermal breather turns the apartment fire into a heat-vision hunt. Prior attempts can end in a crash right at the bloody finish, which is exactly the kind of cruelty the medal system makes worth retrying. The finale's safe-house assault re-energises after the mid-game friction. Few debuts announce a studio this clearly.

Overall, a short, punchy, unlock-rich arcade shooter whose near-invincible riot shield loop and reactive Channel 7 News satire make it a cult curio well worth the time. Identical emergency-mission templates, late-game magnum enemies and occasional pathing failures are real costs, but the core is simply fun from start to finish.
