A post-extinction fable where humanity consumed the world and the last spirit, Gaia's child armed with a living heart, must free the life force trapped in eight animal vessels. The allegory is told wordlessly through memory fragments and dream visions, and it reads clearly without a line of dialogue, humans destroyed the world, and something small and bright is putting it back. The ending lands as genuinely moving rather than heavy-handed, and the whole thing is scored by an underwater-chant soundtrack that is properly haunting, drawing fair comparisons to Journey.

The movement system hooks instantly and never lets go: sprint, double jump, float, air dash, wall ride and vine-surfing, most of it handed over before the first vessel, and the sprint in particular is fast rather than sluggish. Every area's life-burst transformation is a spectacle that never stops impressing across all eight regions, as dead ruins flood with instant greenery and released spirits roam the world permanently. The collectibles are the resurrection: freeing a spirit does not tick a counter, it releases an extinct animal into the world where it can be petted, a small joy worth returning to in every single area.

Each vessel area has its own identity and gimmick, the bee zone's electricity and TV-teleport puzzles, the countryside's black rain, the park's saw blades, the whale's harpoons and swimming, the pig's meat-hook factory, and the variety keeps the hub-and-spoke archipelago fresh throughout. The LifeLink sing ability reveals nearby spirits, exactly the player-optional guidance every collectathon should have, and fast travel via oases lets areas be finished completely. Combat is minimal by design, redeeming corrupted devourers from behind while dodging their thrown bricks and TVs.

The vertical saw-blade platforming is the run's sharpest frustration: optional-spirit heights demand precision jumps from a deliberately floaty character, a combination that turns traversal bliss into a torture device at altitude. Collectible legibility splits unevenly, spirits are tracked but memories are not, producing stumped, map-squinting hunts across simplistic maps whose layouts make the nearest collectible an entire level away. The too-bright protagonist starts as a running joke and becomes a real problem in the bright factory finale, and performance mode chugged at times with no straight 1080p60 option offered.

Two hard technical incidents mar the experience: a crash-on-death in the quarry, and a fast-travel bug that spawned the character through the world, killed her, and removed the pause and LifeLink menus until the next area load. Smaller friction includes goop-versus-water visual ambiguity, unclimbable look-alike walls, aim assist that fights the heart-throw, and devourer children that spawn from nowhere in barrages. None of it is fatal, but the fast-travel bug in particular tests patience at its worst moment.

A full 100% run, every spirit, memory, vessel and achievement, takes closer to eight and a half hours than the genre norm, with one area alone consuming roughly two, so completionists should budget accordingly. The finale's shared Garden, where every completionist worldwide plants a life seed in a common space, converts the whole grind into a warm communal conclusion, a genuinely distinctive easter egg that reframes the achievement hunt as participation in something shared.

Overall, a gorgeous, movement-first resurrection fable whose traversal kit, instant world-greening spectacle and collectibles that come permanently alive outweigh floaty precision platforming, untracked memory hunts and two hard technical incidents. The haunting soundtrack, wordless allegory and shared Garden ending make the grind feel like something genuinely warm, and the world is worth every squint.
