A lost Data West curiosity, fan-translated and largely forgotten, opens on a goddess pulling light from chaos to form twin crystals of Malfala, one of life and one of human development, whose escape unbalances the world. A silent warrior pursues the black crystal to reseal it by reuniting four elementals and their chapters of the Malfala Chronicle, opposed by a goofy-sprited being of darkness with a taste for ye-olde-English villainy. The localisation is awkward and endearing, the prose terse to NES degrees, and the story twist serviceable but thinly told, all of which gives the whole thing the charm of a relic that never expected preservation.

The combat is the revelation: top-down real-time action with directional stab and dash combos, charge attacks and a PG gauge for elemental specials, playing like a fighting game disguised as a Zelda-alike. Combos are genuine rather than a single repeated slash, move variety holds across sixteen hours, and discovering that heavy attacks and stagger tactics work elevates the system further. Few games of this era, and few of any era at this budget, build combat with this much deliberate depth.

The elemental-companion system gives progression real texture: four swappable spirits, Felia, Siren, Boatswain and Ten-Ryu, level via crystals, switch for enemy matchups and dramatically widen special-attack coverage, and regaining them after story beats paces the campaign. The music is a genuine highlight throughout, including tracks placed in caves where players will barely hear them, and a final boss theme in the One-Winged-Angel register crowns the soundtrack. Equipment is found rather than bought, a blacksmith sidequest unlocks sword upgrades, and element-themed dungeons pace the run from Crystal Caves through the Salem Shrine, fire realm, fifteen-floor wind dungeon, Lost Woods, Earth Temple and Chaos Tower.

The dungeon design is the structural burden: mazes built on recycled assets with no in-game maps, easy to get lost in even with hand-linked fan maps at hand, and the Windhold Prairie's looping teleport maze plus a stone-face puzzle that soft-locks on the first attempt mark the game's nadir. The jank of the era persists throughout: stunned enemies take almost no damage, hitstun juggling comes without i-frames, hits land from off-screen at area transitions, and loot despawns on a five-second timer. Arrow weapons claim ranged damage they plainly do not deliver, and money accumulates with almost nothing to spend it on.

The final boss is an unbalanced mess of lasers and near-undodgeable sweeps that demands multiple attempts and a stockpile of instant-heals, a spike that tests goodwill right at the finish line. The XP curve runs stingy early, and the mid-run discovery that a proper d-pad controller transforms the experience says everything about how this game wants to be played. None of the friction is surprising for 1998, but all of it is real.

Roughly fifteen to sixteen hours cover a completionist sweep across three eras of attempts, the kind of game that rewards persistence with a hard-won, joyful conclusion, and the awkward, barely-speaking cast gives the writing an endearing awkwardness that modern localisation would sand away. It is a flawed, beloved relic, and the flaws are inseparable from the charm.

Overall, a lost fan-translated PS1 Action, RPG whose fighting-game-style combo combat, four swappable elementals with screen-clearing specials and excellent music outweigh its maze-like recycled dungeons, era-typical jank and unbalanced final boss. It demands printed maps, a good d-pad and patience, and repays all three with sixteen hours of genuinely deep combat and a soundtrack that deserved a wider audience.
