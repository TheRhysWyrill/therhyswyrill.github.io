The Covenant-heresy storyline remains the series' narrative peak, and the Anniversary remaster gives it the presentation it always deserved. Intercut openings tell the story's structure before a word is spoken: an award ceremony on the human side, nothing but punishment on the Covenant side, a tonal split that frames everything that follows. The split protagonist structure deepens it further, alternating between the supersoldier and the alien commander whose faith fractures, and the political tragedy underneath the shooting has never been matched by the entries that came after. The remastered cutscenes turn it all cinema-grade.

The Blur Studio pre-rendered cutscenes are the remaster's triumph. Every one plays like a film, the upgrade over the originals is genuinely staggering, and a decade of expectation is not only met but exceeded. The remastered lighting is more of a mixed bag: bright scenes look superb, but the darker Flood levels suffer real visibility problems, where the old graphics actually show enemies more clearly. The terminals, fifteen of them, reward lore immersion with Arbiter and Elite backstories and the Unggoy rebellion, and hunting them all fits comfortably inside a six-hour campaign.

Dual wielding and the battle rifle remain era-defining. Dual-wielded combos reshape every encounter, the rifle anchors the sandbox with mid-range precision, and the pair together explain why this entry is remembered so fondly and why its multiplayer culture shaped a generation of console shooters. The plasma pistol takes a proper nerf, the Scorpion and rockets feel weaker than memory serves, and the Ghost handles stiffer than its reputation, balance quirks that date the sandbox slightly. Nothing touches the core rhythm, which stays the series' most influential.

Level variety carries the campaign's momentum. Cairo Station's bomb set-piece opens with spectacle, the Outskirts and Metropolis stretch is a sustained high point, gondola sequences and Scarab climbs vary the pace, the Flood quarantine zone tests avoidance tactics, and the finale's Ghost and Banshee-heavy run alongside the Hunters lands as one of the coolest levels in the series, fighting side by side with formidable former enemies. Johnson's humour threads the whole thing with charm, and the Gravemind's arrival remains one of the most memorably grotesque scenes the series has produced.

The Flood strategy of avoidance is legitimate and often wise. Running past the swarm rather than fighting it works across most of the quarantine zone, and the game respects the choice rather than forcing attrition, an unusual concession that keeps the optional burden light. Marines remain friendly-fire-prone, Brutes berserk unpredictably, and the vehicle quirks add friction without breaking anything. Difficulty at Normal sits comfortably, chosen deliberately, and the campaign never stalls.

The cliffhanger ending is the famous complaint, and it holds up. The campaign builds to a stop rather than a conclusion, and even a returning fan who knows what follows finds the cut-off jarring; without the sequel's existence, the ending would rank as a genuine flaw rather than a historical oddity. Judged today, with the continuation available, it reads as a bold structural choice that pays off in hindsight, but the abruptness is real and unavoidable.

Overall, this is a phenomenal remaster of the series' best campaign, with movie-grade cutscenes, an era-defining dual-wielding sandbox and terminals that reward lore immersion, marred by an abrupt cliffhanger, dark level lighting and a few balance nerfs. Still the favourite by a distance, and the cutscenes alone justify the revisit.
