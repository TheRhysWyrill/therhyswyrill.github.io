The premise is the whole game and the whole appeal: spray, ping, move on. Dirt dissolves in visible percent, checklists tick off panel by panel, and the serenity of waves and gentle music leaves no time pressure anywhere. Turning a chore into a 90-hour zen ritual sounds like a joke until the hours disappear, and the loop holds because every surface answers the nozzle with immediate, measurable feedback. Oddly satisfying and very relaxing is not a bad mission statement for a game to nail completely.

The sheer volume of content exceeds any reasonable expectation, with all free updates and DLC pushing a full clear past ninety hours. Jobs vary wildly in scale, from quick vehicle cleans to multi-hour marathons like the mini golf course, and the DLC levels rank among the most satisfying in the entire catalogue. The framing narrative of text messages from increasingly eccentric clients (a haunted mansion, a cruise ship, Santa's Workshop) builds to an absurd time-travel finale about a lost city of pacifists and Mount Rushless, played entirely for light comedy. A cleaning job that ends with the world saved is exactly the tone this game should have.

Nozzle choice matters enormously to pacing, and the triple nozzle transforms the late game: without it, some of the largest jobs would be outright torture, with it, they become flow states. Technique develops along with the hours, and by the end the difference between an early session and a late one is dramatic. The feared final pyramid came in under two and a half hours thanks largely to that equipment progression. Mastery here is gradual, invisible and real.

Progress percent and checklists keep momentum, but the find-it button deserves a caution: it can be aggressive when hunting the last specks, pouncing on missed dirt without warning. That hunt for invisible residue is the design's central gamble, meditative when the dirt is visible and tedious when it hides. For the most part the balance lands on the right side, and the closing credits replay every completed job as a back-to-back time-lapse that doubles as the game's own victory lap. Murals depicting each finished job as prophecy give the whole thing a strange, self-celebratory identity.

Cleaning geometry is the main friction: items that hide streaks, dirt that blends into same-coloured surfaces, and unreachable angles that force awkward nozzle positions. Same-coloured grime in particular throws off the visual read, turning what should be a simple pass into a pixel hunt. Free aim compounds the problem, imprecise in straight lines and difficult to control for vertical or horizontal sweeps. None of it ruins the flow, but every large job contains at least one stretch where the interface fights the relaxation.

The final pyramid job's payout of $2,250 comically undersells the effort invested, a joke about scale that the game seems aware of. Career pacing handles the long tail well regardless, doling out new locations at a rhythm that keeps ninety hours from ever feeling thin. Zero jobs across the entire campaign actively dislike is a rare statistic for any game to claim. The recommendable quality here is not novelty but reliability: the loop never sours.

Overall, the meditative satisfaction loop and the astonishing breadth of free content make this a genuinely relaxing celebration of making things clean, despite hidden streaks, same-coloured dirt, fiddly free aim and an aggressive find-it button. Ninety-plus hours of visible, incremental progress reward patience like almost nothing else in the medium. Every single minute lands, and the recommendation comes without reservation.
