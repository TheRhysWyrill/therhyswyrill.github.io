This is the origin point of tuner culture in racing games, the era of deep-orange neon, aftermarket rims and Vin Diesel-era street racing rendered in 2003 tech. The garage is half the game, with winning really just the currency for making the car yours, and the customisation loop (vinyls, rims, neons, performance brands) remains its enduring charm. Wheel size jacked as high as it will go is a rite of passage, and the long quest for custom purple paint becomes a genuine emotional through-line, finally granted three progression tiers in. The night-city atmosphere and era-authentic soundtrack carry the mood completely. The series that shaped a generation's music taste starts here.

Drift events are the clear favourite discipline: consistently winnable, generously scored and the only mode where skill reliably beats luck. Drag races travel the opposite arc, degrading from a favourite to the most painful event as performance parts shrink the perfect-shift window to near nothing, with over-rev restarts and one-time-use NOS with no refill compounding the misery. The stock-car workaround, reverting builds so opponents match the pace, bypasses impossible drags entirely. Circuit, sprint, lap knockout, tournament and time trial modes fill the rest of the menu. Drift purity survives the decades intact.

Race outcomes lean heavily on luck rather than skill: random traffic varies wildly between pain and invisibility, and rubber-band AI cheating becomes blatant as faster cars arrive. Blind-jump hills hide oncoming traffic, shortcuts are inconsistent, occasionally a real edge and frequently a trap, and the reset-car button is the godsend that keeps restarts bearable. Roughly one hundred and eleven races build to a finale that squanders its buildup on a single sprint race against the headline rival, ending on a dream-frame reveal with no credits. The build was massive; the payoff is a wet fart. Concessions to the era are fair, but the anticlimax is design, not age.

The car roster is purely aesthetic, with performance identical across every model, so the Dodge Neon, the beloved underdog Miata and the higher-top-speed RX-7 differ only in loyalty. Magazine-cover rewards (a rotating set of tuner publications) give constant forward momentum, and rival rank-races per discipline structure the progression. The laconic rival chatter and character web (a trusted friend's betrayal, a restoration favour) provide more narrative than the menu-driven structure promises. Underground 2's open world would arrive next; this entry's all-progression-is-a-menu primitiveness is part of the period charm. Structure from a shoestring-budget era is visible everywhere.

The soundtrack deserves its own paragraph, because it is doing more than accompanying the races. Era-defining electronic and hip-hop tracks line the menu and the streets alike, and the sequencing lands with a curator's confidence, seeding new artists precisely as the campaign's moods shift. For a generation of players this was a formative playlist, and the tracks still carry that charge. Menu music, race music and replay music are distinct, a level of care few racing games attempt. The audio identity is as foundational to the game's legacy as the neon.

The modded Definitive Edition transforms the visuals, though maxed reflection settings make the roads look like glass, and lowering that detail restores sanity. Custom colours arriving only at a later progression tier is the period's most baffling omission, an absence felt from the first garage visit. Long races switched to easy purely for time-saving are a legitimate strategy in a campaign this restart-heavy. Total runtime splits across two roughly five-hour sessions with heavy restarts, and the last race is won through sheer dumb luck. The flaws are all legible as 2003 design.

Overall, the customisation loop, the drift events and the era-defining soundtrack make this foundational tuner racer worth revisiting even as luck-flavoured races, shrinking drag windows, rubber-band cheating and a one-sprint finale date it badly. The garage charm and the Miata's underdog reliability carry the whole campaign. The years have been kind to the car culture and less kind to the race design, but the fun survives both.
