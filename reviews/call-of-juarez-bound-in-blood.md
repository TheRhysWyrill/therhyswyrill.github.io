A prequel told through the framing narration of the youngest McCall brother, the future preacher of the original game, spanning the American Civil War through the deserts of Mexico and Arizona, and the family-betrayal arc (brothers falling out over a woman and cursed gold) resolves into a satisfying explanation of the first game's status quo. The opening scene's connection to the finale rewards attention, and Ray's transformation into the preacher gives the epilogue an emotional landing the genre rarely attempts. Villain dialogue leans on caricature more than the leads deserve, but the storytelling ambition outshines the era's usual western-shooter template.

The brother-choice system is the structural signature, and it is genuinely substantive. Choosing Ray or Thomas per mission changes levels materially: distinct escape sequences, different intro objectives, divergent traversal (Ray is a slow, close-range revolver specialist with aim-assisted concentration; Thomas is faster, climbing, using knives, a bow and the lasso with a manual concentration mode). Two firing lines exist through the world, and alternating between brothers confirms the divergence is real rather than cosmetic. It is the rare character-choice mechanic that earns its billing.

Concentration mode is the gunplay's character, with Dead Eye echoes (whether intended or not). Shoot enemies to charge, then enter slow-motion gunfights whose implementation differs per brother, and the ability arrives early and stays useful throughout. The horse handling deserves separate billing: responsive enough to turn on a dime, across both streams of play, the traversal is the best of its era and embarrasses most successors. Mid-game, the structure shifts to large open-zone levels with bounty, escort and defence side missions, a scope expansion that evokes later open-zone shooters.

Duels are the recurring boss mechanic and the controls' worst enemy. Keep the hand near the holster, wait for the bell, draw: the reach-for-the-gun rhythm is learnable, and the sweet-spot internalisation eventually lands, but on controller it remains awkward throughout, and the original game's mouse-based implementation demonstrably worked better. Aim-down-sights cancels whenever the character moves, baffling at first and disruptive to strafe-aiming, though holding the trigger toggles a fix that should have been the default. Both quirks are era-typical and both irritate.

Shaky cam during the wagon and Gatling-gun set pieces makes shooting genuinely difficult, and enemies are frequently hard to spot, with the returning body-twist hit reaction from the first game again making hit tracking frustrating. Characters snag on level geometry for no apparent reason, and money resolves into a simple ammo-and-weapon currency at oddly placed mid-level shops. An instant-fail knife-throwing stealth passage backfires into retry spam, and the subverted-expectations trend (open zones, side missions shallower than anticipated) cuts both ways.

Checkpoints are plentiful and difficulty is generally fair, which keeps the mechanical friction from souring the experience. Secrets per level reward exploration, and the epilogue lands emotionally in a way that justifies the whole framing device. As a bridge between the original and the series' later peak, it holds its own: more ambitious than its predecessor structurally, more characterful than its era demanded, and confident enough in its brothers to let levels fork around them.

Overall, this is a confident, characterful western prequel whose brother-choice divergence, concentration gunplay and pre-ordained-narration payoff make it the series' structural high point before its finale, despite controller-hostile duels, an ADS quirk that cancels while moving, shaky-cam set pieces and era-typical geometry snagging. The family-betrayal arc lands, the horses still embarrass the genre, and the whole amounts to a prequel that justifies its own existence.
