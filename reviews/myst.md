A lone island, a handful of strange books, and no instruction whatsoever: the setup that defined an entire branch of puzzle design remains intact in this 2021 remake, and the modernisation work sharpens rather than softens it. The premise hands over a desolate age of deserted walkways, a library of linking books, and two imprisoned voices each claiming the other cannot be trusted, then leaves the deductions entirely to the person holding the controller. Nothing signposts where to go, what matters, or which pieces of the world conceal mechanisms. That refusal to explain is the point, and the remake honours it while quietly fixing the rough edges that made the original punishing for the wrong reasons.

The most significant fix is structural: red and blue pages can now be collected in a single run, removing the original's demand to replay entire ages twice for the two endings built around them. Subtitles, an inventory system, and a photo mode round out the modernisation, each addressing a friction point that had aged poorly. The photo mode in particular earns its place, because photographing clues and notation is exactly the kind of aid this design always implied but never provided. Visuals benefit most visibly, with the island's lonely beauty rendered in crisp detail that preserves the original's aesthetic rather than replacing it. None of this dulls the mystery; the remake simply stops wasting effort on obstacles that were never puzzles.

Sound carries more of the puzzle load than visuals do, and the remake treats its audio-driven design with care. The Mechanical age's compass clicks, the spaceship's ping-and-chirp direction finder, and the Selenitic rocket's tone-matching keyboard all turn listening into a primary verb. The tone-matching keyboard is the standout: a sequence of sounds must be transcribed and replayed, and the design permits a shortcut whereby the keys' physical notches can be counted instead of the tones learned, a loophole that rewards observation over rote transcription. That an alternate route exists through pure attention to detail says everything about how these puzzles were constructed.

Myst Island itself functions as the central hub and the first puzzle, requiring power to be restored and the tower rotated to extract hints before any linking book becomes usable. From there each age runs its own self-contained chain: gear coordinates and compass codes on Mechanical, a flooding lighthouse and compass vault on Stoneship, water-routing lifts through Channelwood's tree canopy, and a tone-locked tunnel out of Selenitic. The ages can be left unsolved and revisited freely, a flexibility the original withheld, and the freedom transforms experimentation from a commitment into a casual act. A notebook and screenshots remain the genuine tools of the trade, exactly as intended.

Narrative delivery is almost entirely environmental. Journals scattered across each age, messages from Catherine, and the brothers' competing confessions from their prison books construct a story of guilt, deceit and ambiguity, and the writing holds up as more than puzzle-window dressing. The Channelwood funeral scene lands with real weight, and the Rime journals sketch a warmer paternal portrait than the main ages allow. Trusting neither imprisoned voice while still reading both is the correct stance, and the game is built so that scepticism is a mechanic rather than an attitude. Reading everything is genuinely rewarded, since the journals double as hint sources.

Not everything modernises cleanly. Legacy-style pointer navigation persists even with a controller attached, and the muscle memory of steering a cursor with the analogue stick produces constant small errors, enough that a dying controller cost real progress mid-movement. The Stone Ship age stonewalls hardest: a drained generator silently extinguishes lights and locks doors, and without connecting the power failure to the symptoms, hours of wandering achieve nothing. Vague objectives are the design's identity, yet the absence of any feedback when a puzzle is solved, or fails to be, tips from cryptic into opaque. Rime, the bonus age added post-launch, compounds this with one long lens-combination contraption that frustrates more than it rewards, saved only by the cosmetic delight of its Aurora Borealis room. The finale, by contrast, closes strong: a fireplace pattern puzzle and a final descent that restore the momentum the bonus age drains.

Overall, this remake proves the foundational puzzle box still works when the cracks are filled in without touching the mystery. The sound-driven puzzle craft, the environmental storytelling and the island's lonely beauty all land as intended, while dual-page collection, photo mode and free age traversal remove the friction that was never the point. Rime remains skippable and the pointer controls remain stubborn, but neither dent the achievement.
