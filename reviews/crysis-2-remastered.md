A virus-ravaged, partially flooded Manhattan under corporate martial law provides the stage, and the story unfolds through news broadcasts, radio chatter and a dying suit's transmission to its dying host. The plot rewards close attention: a corpse-walking protagonist, a century-old immortal executive's betrayal, a CIA double-agent turn, and alien invaders revealed as processors of human biomass. Set-piece escalation still stuns, from a flood barrier bursting to an entire park lifting out of the ground. The campaign's identity as something apart from the standard military shooter survives intact.

The nano-suit cloak sandbox remains the heart of the experience, and choosing stealth over combat is genuinely satisfying throughout. Entire encounters can be bypassed invisibly, sniping from the shadows works, and the vertical Manhattan map suits the suit's traversal beautifully. Cloak infiltration, armour tanking, power kicks and armour-enhanced health all draw from one energy pool, and the symbiotic fantasy of a dying man kept alive by armour that learns from its enemies remains the series' most affecting premise. Few shooters offer a real choice between stealth and spectacle at every checkpoint.

Nano catalysts harvested from alien kills buy modules spanning armour enhancement, mobility, cloak tracking and deflection, and stocking up is entertaining even when the pool disappoints. Some options, such as highlighting enemy routes, are simply not worth their cost, which drains purpose from the kill-currency loop. The merged speed and strength modes and sparing quick-time events are welcome modern conveniences carried into the remaster. Progression is serviceable rather than rewarding, and the modules rarely change how an encounter plays.

Enemy tuning is uneven across the campaign. Corporate troopers can feel oppressive in numbers while some alien ranks fold instantly, and difficulty swings appear within the same mission. Grenades do nothing against armoured foes, and the machine gun disappears for long stretches with no equivalent replacement, leaving loadouts thin exactly when they matter most. Armour mode at its best makes the wearer feel invincible, which sharpens the contrast with the moments it does not. The pistol viewmodel rides oddly high on screen, a small but persistent visual irritant.

The remaster's technical friction is real. A mid-session crash interrupts play, and frame-rate dips are pinned squarely on ray tracing, which has to be disabled to restore stable performance. The install size dwarfs its siblings while the underlying content is unchanged. Visually, the remaster preserves the scale and the lighting, but the cost of enabling its headline feature undermines the point of the upgrade. Playing with ray tracing off is effectively the intended configuration.

The sandbox structure remains the game's quiet achievement. Checkpoints are frequent enough to encourage experimentation, corridors open into vertical playgrounds where multiple routes coexist, and the alien invasion backdrop justifies the escalating chaos. The submerged streets, collapsed freeways and evacuated districts give the suit's mobility constant new texture. Replaying a favourite from the previous console generation confirms the design has aged better than its tech, and the core loop holds against modern competition.

Overall, the nano-suit stealth sandbox, the set-piece spectacle and the still-distinct identity carry this remaster past its technical stumbles and thin progression options. Ray-tracing stutter, a crash, uneven enemy tuning and weapon-balance gaps are genuine deductions, but none touches the cloak-first fantasy at the centre. Played with the cloak on and ray tracing off, the campaign holds up completely.
