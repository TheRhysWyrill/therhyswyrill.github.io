A rogue squad of operators goes underground to expose a cabal embedded within the agency that created them, and the 1991 Gulf War setting gives the espionage its era-authentic dressing. The campaign follows a defector-hunting cell recruiting a broken veteran, a hacker and a forger across a globe-spanning chase, and the continuity work rewards long-time followers with call-backs that land rather than decorate. The mole thread builds to a twist whose personal history behind the betrayal genuinely lands, and the era flavour, from analogue gadgets to period dressing, holds throughout. Beneath the spy games sits a shooter that stays recognisable even as the structure around it mutates.

The safe house hub is the structural centrepiece. The Rook houses upgradeable stations purchasable with mission money, and its secrets behave like a treasure hunt: black-light searching, radio ciphers, piano codes, safes and a boiler-room mystery each reward poking at every wall. One safe's puzzle chain alone justifies the detour, and the hub turns mission downtime into gameplay rather than a menu. It is the rare franchise addition that respects curiosity, and the payoff loops back into the campaign's own continuity puzzles.

Mission variety follows through on the promise. The Avalon assassination two-parter layers stealth over spectacle, the DC gala infiltration offers three selectable leads with the auction route playing as its own con, and the open-world Iraq desert scatters SAM sites and Scud launches across genuinely free navigation. Casino Lutati turns a heist into card sharping with marked decks, the Kuwait airport chase and tank ride deliver kinetic spectacle, and the Vorkuta sabotage rewards eavesdropping on guard dialogue to plant dud warheads and dupe the radar operator. Each mission commits to its idea rather than window-dressing a corridor.

The Kentucky research-facility level is the left turn nobody sees coming, descending into a drug-trip horror gauntlet with boss fights scored by unique, era-inappropriate music that somehow fits. The mind-dive interrogation finale mirrors the psychedelic register, though its Harrow-and-zombies climax tips from inventive into punishing, swarming the player with high-damage attackers against meagre health. White-on-white quick-time prompts are easy to miss in the chaos, and the final hour sours what the previous seven built. The sequence remains inventive; it is simply brutal on top of everything else it asks.

Cutscene pacing leans modern. Story beats front-load cinematic screens at the expense of hands-on time, a ratio older entries inverted, and the campaign's otherwise strong structure pays a tempo tax for its storytelling ambitions. Armour plates carried over from recent entries remain divisive, Pantheon elites and juggernauts absorb ammunition like sponges, and grenade-heavy enemy kits keep pressure constant rather than varied. None of this breaks the shooting, but the friction is consistent across the back half.

Technical state is oddly split. The campaign ran rock-stable throughout, a notable contrast with the series' recent stability record, yet cosmetic graphical bugs persist: enemies and glass erupting into black smoke puffs on AMD hardware, a variable-rate-shading artefact still unpatched a year after release. Relentless lock-pick tutorial pop-ups add small UI noise. The bugs never break gameplay, but the visual glitches recur often enough to distract from otherwise strong presentation.

Overall, this is the most structurally ambitious campaign the series has fielded, held together by a puzzle-rich safe house hub, genuinely distinct genre missions and a mole twist with real personal weight. The punishing zombie-swarm finale, the white-on-white quick-time prompts and the persistent cosmetic graphical bugs are the costs, along with cutscene-heavy pacing. The invention underneath outweighs the jank, and the campaign stands among the series' best.
