A companion story running parallel to the Fellowship's journey through the north of Middle-earth is the pitch, and few licences have attempted anything comparable since. A Black Númenórean sorcerer spreads his reach from Fornost to Mount Gundabad while the larger war unfolds off-screen, and the three-hero structure, a human ranger, a dwarf fighter and an elven mage, borrows deliberately from the Snowblind lineage of couch-era dungeon crawlers. The earnest 2011 fantasy tone is worn openly, complete with comic-voiced companions and lore woven into every corner. Locations stretch from Sarn Ford through the Barrow Downs and Rivendell to Mirkwood and the Grey Mountains, giving the campaign genuine geographical range. It is fan service in the best sense, built by a studio that understood the material.

Ragdoll physics elevate every fight into physical comedy, with enemies and companions alike bouncing off walls and each other in ways that never stop being entertaining. The core hack-and-slash loop mows down goblins, orcs, Uruk-hai, trolls, spiders and undead with an energy that holds across the full campaign. Belaram the eagle and Radagast's badger friends add moments of genuine delight amid the carnage. Combat demands little thought but delivers consistent spectacle, and the pacing keeps arenas flowing one after another. Few licensed action games sustain this much momentum.

Elf stones slot into weapons alongside gems, set bonuses reward committed builds, and loot arrives with stat requirements that make every pickup an evaluation decision. Respeccing through skill trees allows mid-run pivots, such as rebuilding from two-handed weapons to dual-wielding, and unused gear can be dumped directly onto AI companions. Three characters can be swapped between, each carrying their own inventory and progression, and mounted crossbows plus eagle summons punctuate the standard loop. The gear treadmill is genuinely addictive, filling long stretches with constant evaluation and rebuild. Progression here outclasses what most licensed ARPGs of the era attempted.

Rivendell deserves special mention as the campaign's finest hour, and not for its combat. Talking to every canon character from Bilbo to Elrond consumes nearly an hour on its own, the dialogue deep enough that the lore-devouring becomes the destination rather than the detour. The tombs scattered through the campaign echo earlier Middle-earth game adaptations in their design language. Mount Gundabad's late heist delivers a proper climax to the northern campaign. The hub design understands that the licence's greatest asset is its world, not its weapons.

The downed-companion crawl-away bug plagues the AI throughout, with revive attempts chasing companions who inch helplessly further away and challenge waves collapsing into farce as a result. Faren, nominally the tank, spends entire fights unconscious and becomes a running gag through sheer reliability of failure. The post-game challenge arena tips from power fantasy into over-tuned slog, its later waves ragdolling the player endlessly until an attempt becomes abandonable. A scroll side-quest ends at six of seven with no clear answer available. The AI friction is the one flaw that genuinely damages the experience.

Quality-of-life gaps accumulate around the edges: no gear-compare screen, no repair-all button, no mid-level character switching, and a map item that appears to add nothing at all. Individually these are minor; collectively they mark 2011-era design that a patch could have modernised in an afternoon. The linear, level-segmented structure leaves little room for exploration, which suits the combat but undersells the worldbuilding. All of it is forgivable in the moment and worth cataloguing for the record.

Overall, this is a dated but genuinely fun companion to the Snowblind tradition, whose addictive loot loop, ragdoll comedy and deep-cut Tolkien fan service outweigh janky companion AI and missing UI basics. The Rivendell hub alone justifies the campaign for anyone invested in the world, and the combat sustains momentum throughout. Co-operative play would smooth the AI friction entirely, but even alone the adventure satisfies.
