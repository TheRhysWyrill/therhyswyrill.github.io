October 1970, northern Quebec. A private detective arrives to investigate vandalism against a local industrialist, finds his client dead in the general store, and is trapped by a supernatural blizzard in a village slowly freezing to death. The mystery unfolds entirely through found documents: a store owner's blackmail diary, a doctor's medical files, a rebel's manifesto, and an indigenous legend of the Wendigo. Together they paint a portrait of exploitation, repression and buried guilt. The narrative structure moves from murder investigation to supernatural tragedy with a confidence few adventure games manage.

The narration is the game's core identity, delivering a literary detective novel as playable prose. The voice-over carries the melancholy register of 1970s rural Quebec noir, and the bilingual French and English world-building gives the setting a cultural texture almost nothing else attempts. The Cree legend lore lands with genuine weight, and the document chain never reads as collectible filler. Each note advances both the mystery and the portrait of a dying town.

The survival layer is genuinely integrated with the investigation rather than bolted on. Cold, warmth and mental-health meters tick constantly, and reading notes in the open literally freezes the detective, a lesson the game teaches with quiet cruelty. Fires must be lit and sheltered behind, and the snowmobile must be rebuilt from scattered parts before the map opens up. Environmental puzzles (generator wiring, a safe combination, magnet-and-wire retrieval, a dynamite-blasted cave) punctuate the driving and reading. A UFO side-gag rewards thorough exploration, and an optional treasure hunt is the only content left on the table.

Atmosphere is masterful, built from whiteout snow, oppressive silence, phantom visions and frozen corpses caught mid-action. Wolves and hallucination sequences punctuate the quiet without ever becoming action set-pieces, and the dread accumulates through stillness rather than spectacle. The reveal reframes the entire mystery as one man's grief-driven vengeance, and the finale chase into the water lands with the urgency the slow middle has earned. Black bars frame the early game before gradually disappearing, a presentation quirk that suits the cinematic framing.

The technical presentation is the campaign's weakest pillar. Constant area-loading interrupts the exploration rhythm, and frame-rate collapses entirely on the snowmobile, turning open stretches into slideshows. Driving through whiteout blizzards is atmospheric but practically miserable, with poor visibility and fiddly turning controls that make even short journeys frustrating. The reload button is genuinely undiscoverable, resisting every conventional input until stumbled upon late. Each flaw is survivable, but together they interrupt an otherwise seamless mood.

Runtime is short, covering every house and note in a single sitting, and the memory of the campaign tends to exaggerate its length. The narrative pivot from vandalism investigation to frozen villagers and spectral vengeance recontextualises every document collected beforehand, rewarding attention without punishing haste. The town's portrait of industrial greed and quiet repression gives the tragedy its foundation, and the indigenous lore thread is treated with more care than genre convention expects. The finale's literary closing line lands exactly as written.

Overall, this is a narrated detective novel dressed as a survival game, brief and beautiful and slightly janky, with a mystery that pivots convincingly and an atmosphere that justifies every frozen minute. Area-loading, snowmobile performance and an undiscoverable reload button are real deductions, but the document-driven storytelling and Québécois cultural texture carry the whole experience. What the campaign promises in voice and mood, it delivers completely.
