Modern-day pirates seize a cruise liner and take the president's daughter hostage, and the rescue operation that follows treats the entire ship as an arsenal. The premise is openly disposable and played entirely for absurdity, which is precisely the point. What follows is a brawler built on the conviction that any object within reach, from a loaf of bread to a household appliance, is a legitimate instrument of violence. The tone is pure late-90s arcade cinema, and the design commits to it without reservation.

Combat forms the backbone, and it carries clear lineage from the side-scrolling brawlers of the previous generation. Strikes land with weight, combos flow quickly, and the camera dramatises every exchange with exaggerated cuts that give even ordinary throws the feel of an action set-piece. Difficulty scales through mission length and enemy damage output rather than new systems, so the second half asks for sharper play rather than different play. The core loop stays legible throughout, and momentum rarely stalls.

QTEs punctuate the bigger moments, breaking up brawling with brief timed prompts at set-piece boundaries. They are light touches rather than obstacles, and they reinforce the arcade rhythm the whole structure follows. A clothing-damage system accompanies the fighting: characters visibly lose garments as punishment accumulates, so the state of a fighter's outfit doubles as a health read. Few games of the era committed to a gag this specific, and fewer still made it mechanically informative.

The weapon sandbox deserves its own accounting, because it is the reason the whole enterprise works. Baguettes, pepper that fells enemies through sneezing, a hairspray flamethrower, a plunger, a fish head, a toilet, a fridge and an umbrella all function as armaments, and defeated enemies themselves can be hurled at their colleagues. Almost nothing on screen is inert. The improvisation this produces gives every encounter a comic texture that ordinary fisticuffs could never supply, and it sustains the entire run.

Three missions structure the campaign, each assigning a different character and a different insertion method: parachute, boat, and underwater. Mission two is the only one contained entirely aboard the ship. The characters play distinctly enough that the repeat content stays fresh, and costumed enemy designs, including a crab, a fish and a giant shell, keep the bestiary from turning grey. A hard-mode Showdown repeat of the same missions exists for anyone wanting a stiffer test of the same material.

Teleporting cyborg bosses interrupt the flow at mission boundaries, and their sudden relocations force more careful spacing than the regular mobs demand. The cutscenes stitching the plot together run mere seconds each, an abruptness that borders on parody and suits the material's total disinterest in substance. Map areas repeat across missions at different times of day, so the environments are familiar by the third pass even when the routes through them differ. Structure, here, is clearly subordinate to spectacle.

The friction is modest but real. Regular enemies carry no health bars, which makes gauging remaining punishment feel arbitrary during extended brawls. Health pickups restore so little that they barely register as rewards when they appear. Reused layouts compound the sense of thin construction underneath the spectacle, and the hard-mode repeat recycles rather than expands. None of these faults undermines the central invention, though each narrows the margin around it.

The console this shipped on deserves mention in its own right. Hardware of the era produced this level of animation, camera work and sheer object density, and the achievement reads clearly decades later. The game stands as evidence of what that machine could do when a development team chose invention over scale, and its identity remains distinct within the brawler lineage it inherits.

Overall, this is arcade brawling at its most committed to a single ridiculous idea, and the commitment pays off in full. Every reachable object becoming a weapon, outfits degrading into damage gauges, and bosses blinking across the deck add up to an hour of invention most games never approach. The thin structure and missing health bars register as blemishes on work that succeeds where it matters.
