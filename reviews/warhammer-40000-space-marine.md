Few games make sheer mass the core appeal, and this one builds everything around it. Playing a Space Marine means feeling every pound of power armour: walking reads as heavy, sprinting shakes the whole screen, and the one-man-army fantasy lands because three Marines hold the line against millions. The forge world Graia supplies the backdrop, all gothic, oversized Imperium architecture that dwarfs the camera, and the campaign tours orcs, Imperial Guard and Chaos in a single arc. It has aged into a classic largely because nothing else commits this hard to the simple pleasure of being enormous.

Combat is melee-first and unapologetic about it. Guns exist across a proper roster, bolter, stalker bolter, vengeance launcher, melta and plasma, but the balance tips decisively toward the chainsword, power axe and thunder hammer, with ranged options feeling feeble by comparison. The signature loop builds fury through damage, stuns with one button and executes with another, and executions restore health, keeping the player inside the horde rather than behind cover. The power axe in particular outperforms expectations, and enemies detonate into puffs of red smoke that make every kill read as impact.

The narrative pivots from orc invasion to Chaos incursion around two-thirds through, earlier than memory suggests, on the back of the Inquisitor Drogan arc: a wounded psyker revealed as long dead, his body a puppet of the demon Nemeroth. Civilian audio logs give the invasion a tragic register, and the Imperial Guard subplot earns affection precisely because they are weak individually and strong only in numbers, the setting's orcs in all but name. The odd story logic of a key item handed off moments before its holder's death notwithstanding, the drama builds to an accusation that sends the captain to the Inquisition, a gut-punch ending that doubles as a perfect bridge to the sequel.

The servo skull hunt gives the campaign its structure. Forty-eight skulls are scattered across seventeen chapters, and chasing them justifies a revisited run that runs an hour longer than the first pass. The payoff disappoints: the audio logs they contain run a few words apiece, far too brief to matter, and the collectibles become a completion goal rather than a lore reward. The hunt itself stays engaging, but the thinness of the logs is a genuine design miscalculation.

Enemy design sours in the late game. Laser cultists vaporise shields and health in a single volley, one-shot rocket orcs punish positioning without telegraph, and the finale's champions outpace a sprinting Space Marine, which reads as pure spite rather than challenge. The Nemeroth fight compounds the frustration by anchoring its climax to quick-time events, demanding retries where the rest of the campaign rewards aggression. None of these appear before the two-thirds mark, so the first half stays uniformly fun, but the last act asks for patience the rest of the game never needed.

Technical performance holds up well on Linux, where the notorious Windows launch problems never materialise; a single freeze-frame on a cultist kill is the only blemish across the run. Weapon variety narrows in practice as specific enemy types render certain guns useless, funneling loadouts toward the melee defaults, and fury tends to be hoarded rather than spent because its window rarely feels necessary. Neither quirk undermines the combat's core rhythm, which remains the most convincing translation of power-armour weight in the genre.

Overall, this is a power fantasy that has aged into a classic, whose screen-shaking weight, execution-driven melee loop and gothic scale outweigh spiteful late enemies, trivial collectible lore and a QTE-anchored finale. Guns lose to chainswords and the last act stumbles, but the trip remains well worth taking.
