A bomb-threat campaign unfolds across Las Vegas, told in parallel with the first game's story, a structure that reveals itself gradually and recontextualises events from the other side of the timeline. The latter half's chronology gets messy, but the parallel design remains genuinely interesting and a carry-over save from the previous entry grants bonus experience, a touch no other entry in the series attempted. A betrayal in the ranks gives the back half its tension, with the traitor's motive rooted in spite over a botched first mission, a petty and human reason that suits the grounded register, and a finale confrontation that closes the arc the parallel structure opened.

The tactical loop is the heart: tag targets through doorways, order breaches, direct the two-agent squad and clear rooms methodically. Loadout depth is massive, with enough equipment variety that the tutorial alone revives appreciation for it, and the stealth-then-firefight rhythm of the convention centre and monorail levels is the series' signature at its best. Generous checkpoints keep methodical play rewarding rather than punishing, and the whole campaign runs roughly seven hours across two sittings. The experience tracks reward repeated play so naturally that replaying a level to chase a weapon unlock never feels like a grind.

Progression is the dealmaker: three parallel XP tracks (Marksman, CQB and Assault) tick constantly, unlocking weapons and armour as each grows, with marksman levels climbing fastest for anyone favouring headshots. The constant sense of advancement is the kind of hook that made the era's shooters feel rewarding, and finishing a campaign with one track maxed and the others partway up is its own small completionist satisfaction.

Squad AI is the persistent flaw: teammates get stuck on each other in doorways, ignore orders at critical moments, and occasionally leave whole rooms to be cleared alone. Effective at times and maddening at others, the inconsistency itself is the problem rather than any single failure mode. Solo missions amplify the weakness, stripping away the safety blanket the team provides and exposing how much of the design assumes two capable partners, and a couple of levels end up played entirely solo out of frustration rather than design.

The final encounter is a punishing gauntlet of respawning enemies and grenade spam that takes many attempts, with the grenades the single most frustrating element of the whole campaign. Even at full armour the protagonist goes down remarkably fast, which undercuts the armour upgrades the progression system dangles. Difficulty elsewhere is fair, but the finale's escalation is abrupt and demands patience.

It is a pre-modern tactical squad shooter, a genre now all but extinct, with a single-player focus and no live-service obligations. The series' later pivot left this kind of campaign without a successor, and the cancelled Patriots project remains the era's last unfinished breath. That extinction is part of what makes this return land: nothing else plays quite like it any more, flaws and all, and the genre's disappearance says more about market incentives than about the design's enduring appeal.

Overall, a flawed but deeply enjoyable slice of the lost tactical-shooter era, with a tag-breach-clear loop, three-track XP progression and parallel-story structure that outshine janky squad AI, a brutal grenade-spammed finale and fast deaths under full armour. Patience for the janky teammates is the price of admission, and the campaign remains worth it.
