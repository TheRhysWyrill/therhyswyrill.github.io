A caped wannabe hero works through a death-list of the world's six greatest heroes to claim the top spot, only for an upstart villain to steal her credit. The plot is charmingly dumb and knows it: tongue-in-cheek hero bios, fourth-wall-breaking dialogue and pop-culture nods to classic boss design fill the margins. The bio cards are a highlight of the writing, from a slug henchman to a puzzle-platformer-parody worm couple to an indie homage robot, and the bright, chunky pixel art carries the whole thing with a soundtrack worth complimenting on its own. The satirical hero-ranking fiction gives every fight a reason to exist beyond spectacle.

The core identity is three verbs: jump, double-dash and bullet-time slowdown, with twin-stick aiming layered over the top. Every encounter becomes a choreography puzzle of slowdowns, dashes and rocket jumps, and the time-slow mechanic hooks from the opening seconds. Levels last four to five minutes each across seven missions, deaths respawn the player nearby, and the whole thing keeps arcade momentum without ever punishing experimentation. The Mega Man and Sonic nods in the boss design are part of the choreography's lineage, worn openly rather than hidden.

Boss and level variety keep things fresh: a bullet-train race, a timed dragon interior, a hive-mind swarm and a factory alarm each bring their own gimmick, with the battalion fight standing out as the design's most memorable set piece, an egg-flavoured homage to a blue hedgehog's nemesis. The finale is a boss gauntlet remixing every prior boss as robo-copies, then a mirror match against the hero's own male double, and it all culminates in one of the most memorable credit sequences in the genre, full stat cards for every character including the dragon's hobbies.

The shop between levels sells weapons (pistols, shotgun, revolver, flamethrower, RPG) and health upgrades, and forced weapon swapping between levels drives real loadout experimentation, with dual pistols and foam a particularly fun pairing. Agency between levels is genuine, and the shop loop gives the short campaign a reason to replay for score and speed. Equipping new purchases takes a moment to parse the first time, a small onboarding hiccup, and the initial equip confusion is the only friction the shop itself offers.

The control scheme is unusual: right trigger jumps while the right stick aims and shoots, and it takes real adjustment, with fingers slipping off the stick and inputs fumbled through the first stretch. It is a quirk rather than a dealbreaker, but the learning curve is genuine. Enemy telegraphing could be clearer in places, with falling rocks, hatching eggs and the train level's timing balls catching the eye too late.

Length is the obvious criticism: the main run is astonishingly brief, comparable to the shortest classics of the handheld era. It is also entirely by design, an arcade structure with speedrun and score-chasing loops, and the brevity lands as a feature for anyone who buys in on those terms. The budget price matches the span, and the stats-filled credits are a reward in themselves.

Overall, a brilliant, bite-sized arcade shooter whose bullet-time choreography, varied level gimmicks and glorious credit sequence make it easy to love despite an unusual control layout and a main run measured in minutes rather than hours. Buy it for the movement, stay for the boss gauntlet, and the credit sequence alone justifies the trip.
