A grim, rain-soaked, distorted world strongly implied to be Britain follows two children through the wilderness, a school, a hospital and a signal-blighted city. Little Nightmares II commits as hard to scale-based horror as anything in the format: everything is enormous, twisted and elongated, and both the comedy and the dread flow from being mouse-sized in a hostile world. The chanting carried over from the first game's soundtrack still puts the nerves on edge, and the visual detail rewards attention, down to the sound of running on carpet. Glitchy remains as the traces of abducted children is genuinely dark worldbuilding. The wordless storytelling survives the addition of a companion intact.

The competent AI companion is the playthrough's defining feature and the best change from the first game: Six mirrors the player's actions, solves co-op puzzles and even dispatches enemies, appearing to learn from behaviour rather than follow scripts. The dynamic lightens the tone compared with the first game's solitude without defusing it, and the companion's presence turns environmental puzzles into genuine two-partner constructions. She rescues the player from fights the protagonist cannot win. The Six twist lands with emotional weight at the music-box finale, aided by the score. For a genre built on isolation, giving it a companion is a real design statement.

The TV teleportation puzzles anchor the back half, creative and multi-layered, with the Thin Man's television maze the standout. The four monster encounters impress across the board: the Hunter's shotgun ambush, the Teacher's stretching neck, the Doctor, and the Thin Man himself, each staged as a chase or stealth set piece rather than a health-bar fight. The Hunter alone sets a high bar as a first encounter. The hands fights later are far more manageable than the school's bullies. Escalation holds across the whole campaign.

Quality-of-life fixes stand out everywhere: keys go straight into pockets, balance beams lock the player on so falling off is impossible, and checkpoints are gracious throughout. Not carrying keys everywhere is the kind of small mercy that changes how a whole game feels. Depth perception remains an occasional issue from the first game, though noticeably less frequent. Grabbing mapped to the right trigger feels odd throughout, causing fumbles at bad moments and forgotten hold-to-climb inputs. The game never explains the TV-corridor mechanics, expecting blind experimentation, which suits the wordless design even when it costs progress.

The school chapter's bully combat is the one serious complaint: slow, weighty swings that whiff by millimetres, collisions with invisible foreground geometry, and instant death on contact. The combat reads as clunky by accident rather than intent, and the "intentionally clunky" defence rings hollow. It occupies a single chapter, and the game would be stronger if the school had stayed puzzle-only. Getting lost in the cluttered hospital hiding sequences adds friction, with foreground elements obscuring the character's position at the worst moments. Neither issue sinks the experience, and both are scoped tightly.

The roughly four-and-a-half-hour campaign runs slightly shorter than the first game including its DLC, but the density compensates: stealth set-pieces, hide-and-seek chases, co-op environmental puzzles and the TV transmissions rarely repeat a beat. Performance mode holds the visuals impressively on Series X. The store description spoils the companion's identity before the reveal lands, an avoidable marketing misstep. As a sequel it is a clear step up in presentation and design across the board. The pocket-sized flashlight's perfect convenience is the kind of detail the series trades in.

Overall, a clear step up from its predecessor, carried by companion AI that genuinely learns, creative TV teleport puzzles and scale-based horror executed with total confidence. The school's clunky bully combat and occasional depth-perception deaths are real blemishes, but everything else is incredible, and the wordless dread earns the recommendation wholeheartedly.
