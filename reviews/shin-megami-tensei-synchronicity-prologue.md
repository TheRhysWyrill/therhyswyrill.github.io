A full-length, fully realised Metroidvania released entirely free as an advertisement sounds like a contradiction, yet this fan-translated promotional piece from the Touhou Luna Nights developer delivers exactly that. A human consciousness is synchronised into a snow imp through an unknown program to open a path towards the future, while the world outside hints at a larger crisis familiar to series devotees. The buddy dynamic with an amnesiac fire demon drives the narrative, and the backstory rewards close attention with a genuinely poignant thread. Retro Metroidvania structure carries the whole, all snowy base camps, poison-gas zones and night-switching candle puzzles. That something this polished was ever disposable marketing remains the game's own best punchline.

Dual-character switching is the mechanical core: the snow imp freezes water into traversable ice, the fire demon melts it back, and hover-versus-ground traversal splits the map between them. Elemental skills are bought with in-game Maka currency, demon-weakness combat rewards experimenting with the familiar roster, and the Demonica suit unlocks gas zones, guns and environment resistance. Stat-boosting rings hide across the stages, HP and MP upgrades pad survivability, and finishing the campaign unlocks a boss-rush time attack. The systems are complete, not promotional gestures. Nothing here plays like an afterthought.

The soundtrack is exceptional, repeatedly the game's best quality, with stage 4's score standing as a genuine highlight of the year's pixel-art releases. Series fan service lands throughout, with familiar demon cameos, elemental weaknesses to exploit and lore ties that reward franchise knowledge, including a suit whose significance to a certain landmark dungeon-crawler only clicks in hindsight. The buddy dynamic's payoff is tracked closely and lands with real weight. The compendium of callbacks never overwhelms the newcomer. Both audiences are served without compromise.

The ring-hunt completion side sours in its final stretch, with the last missing rings devolving into pixel-hunting dark rooms and destructible billboards with no guidance whatsoever. Boss encounters mix clever patterns with dead waiting time, their invincibility periods dragging, which hurts the boss-rush time attack most. A teleport-triggered softlock under Wine can crash the game and the entire desktop session, forcing a full restart. Untranslated text lingers in places, though the light text load mostly hides it. Enemies drop only experience and money, never health, making resource management notably punishing.

Roughly two hours cover a 100% run including the post-game time attack, tight enough that the structure never oversteps its brief. The retro presentation is crisp and smooth, running the same engine lineage as the developer's better-known work, and the difficulty curve respects the format without spiking unfairly. The self-terminating distribution window adds urgency that the quality makes genuinely absurd. Everything about the packaging says disposable; everything about the execution says otherwise. The gap between the two is the whole story.

Stage 4's suit upgrade and its score mark the campaign's peak, the moment where the fan service, the music and the traversal design all crest together. The ending's message-sender thread ties back to the opening's news reports with a quiet callback that rewards the attentive, and the buddy dynamic resolves with a poignancy no promotional material has any right to attempt. The compendium of series callbacks lands without ever gatekeeping the newcomer. Everything fits, which is the most damning evidence that this was never a mere advert.

Overall, this is an outstanding freebie whose exceptional soundtrack, tight dual-character traversal and genuine series fan service outweigh opaque ring hunts, dragging bosses and a Wine-breaking bug entirely. It plays like a paid release and deserves far more than its life as disposable marketing. Short, polished and completely worth the time of anyone who can still find it.
