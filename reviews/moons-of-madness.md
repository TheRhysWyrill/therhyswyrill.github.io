An isolated corporate Mars station receives strange dreams, and Moons of Madness builds its cosmic horror from the collision between space-age infrastructure and ancient Martian machinery. The corporate frame is genuinely committed, with Orochi marketing material, memos and NDAs wrapping a Lovecraft mystery where the cosmic and the corporate turn out to be the same enemy. Readable notes litter every surface, from NDA documents to journals and psychiatric files, and the lore drip forms the game's spine. The Mars-suit survival texture (air, heart rate, one helmet at a time) sits closer to a NASA simulation than most cosmic horror attempts. Atmosphere is the core appeal, and the game never stops feeding it.

Puzzle design carries the mid-game. Solar panel calibration, water reclamation maths, circuit rerouting, centrifuge formula mixing and periodic-table codes build an engineering-fiction texture that grounds the horror in believable station work, with a photo-order mystery adding a detective beat and a gravity-manipulator assembly puzzle escalating the scale. The environmental storytelling puzzles work as well as the mechanical ones, and at no point does the difficulty spike into unfairness. The variety keeps a first-person walking structure from ever feeling static, and the puzzles reward the same attentive reading the notes demand.

Organic chase sequences land well when they arrive. The greenhouse creature and the sand-worm exterior walks during the descent generate real tension, threats that pursue rather than pop. The greenhouse pursuit in particular converts curiosity into anxiety without a single cheap trick, and the exterior sequences use the Martian surface's exposure to great effect. These set-pieces prove the game understands dread as pursuit rather than startle, which makes the horror's later reliance on startle all the more noticeable.

Horror pacing fades after a strong opening. The first couple of hours deliver genuine spooks before the effectiveness wears off, and the tension that remains is atmospheric rather than frightening. Jump scares carry the weight the atmosphere drops, and they are a crap way of doing horror, briefly startling without producing the sustained edge that good dread manages. The recurring astronaut apparition loses power fast, since only so many random appearances can spook before the pattern becomes furniture. The tension never disappears, but the distinction between tense and scary defines the whole campaign.

The final boss and its QTE-prompt fight are genuinely unclear, a clunky encounter where the required actions resist comprehension and success arrives through trial, error and generous checkpoints rather than understanding. Pop-in and load hitching compound the technical friction, with plants spawning bare metres ahead during area transitions and chugging accompanying every load. Sprint gating as an unlock adds absurdity, forcing the ability to move legs faster out of a progression tree in a suit full of advanced technology. The suit doors refuse to close automatically, a small texture complaint that fits the survival fiction but not the convenience.

Both endings exist and both are worth seeing, with the drifting-ship epilogue closing the story on a haunting note either way. Roughly five hours covers the campaign, and the alternate ending is short work to reach. No combat exists until the scripted final sequence, keeping the walking-horror purity intact. The oxygen refills and BioGauge heart-rate system give the survival texture mechanical teeth without ever dominating.

Overall, Moons of Madness is a lore-rich corporate Lovecraftian horror whose committed worldbuilding, readable notes and engineering-flavoured puzzles build a tense, intriguing Mars mystery, even as the scares fade after the opening hours and a clunky final boss blunts the finish. The atmosphere sustains where the startle fails, and the package rewards attentive reading throughout. Judge it as cosmic-horror tension rather than sustained terror and it lands warmly.
