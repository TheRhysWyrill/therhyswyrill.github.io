A wordless tale told through photo-fragment memories: a little Yarny journeys from a cosy Nordic home through gardens, seashores, mountains, abandoned industrial sites and snowy wilds, piecing together a family's story of love, loss and renewal. The conceit remains genuinely singular: the platformer whose health bar is its own body, spending yarn to solve puzzles and traverse, with resource management and physical fragility fused into one mechanic. No other game does this. Photographic Nordic realism still looks fresh years on, and the peaceful atmosphere is the most consistent quality across the whole campaign. Melancholy but warm is the exact register.

Yarn-as-resource puzzle design makes every chapter a distinct economy: thread refills at spindles, knots anchor swings, and bridges cost length that must be spent wisely. Solutions range from fruit bridges to weighted seesaws, and checkpoint knits restore the body between hazards. Roughly twelve chapters (Thistle and Weeds, The Sea, Mountain Trek, Off the Rails, Down in a Hole, Winter Sun, Rust, Last Leaf) each fill a photo frame in the hub world, ending with a picture-book recap of everything touched. The puzzles themselves never stop satisfying. Water, mercifully, is not instant death.

The story's delivery is as distinctive as its mechanics. Each chapter's collected photo fragments assemble into scenes of a family's arc, and the game never interrupts platforming to explain them; the narrative exists at the edges of the screen for anyone who lingers. Letters between chapters carry the quieter beats, and the hub world's completed frames become a scrapbook of the journey rather than a menu. Wordless storytelling risks emptiness, but the restraint here pays off, with the yarn-as-love metaphor landing precisely because it is never spoken aloud. The epilogue's closing letter earns its sentiment honestly.

Collectible hunting is mostly well-hidden but fair, with two exceptions that sour the completion loop. One level hides its secrets so well that not a single one surfaces after the first, and another demands foreknowledge of a one-use breaker to reach a box that cannot otherwise open. Firefly-latch momentum sections in the late chapters make their collectibles genuinely unfun, to the point of abandonment. Hidden-but-findable is the right philosophy; a few placements miss it. Completionists will want an external guide for the worst two levels.

Environmental hazards escalate nicely across chapters, from gentle crabs and birds to acid pools, crows and a blizzard weathered by latching to lanterns. The late-game difficulty spikes occasionally feel cheap, with crow gauntlets arriving without pattern and precision sections tightening abruptly. Bridge-ordering puzzles mid-game demand trial-and-error finesse that sits oddly against the otherwise gentle tone. Minor physics inconsistencies (fall damage on a creature literally made of yarn, occasional lasso-input drops) amuse more than they aggravate. The ramp is welcome; its sharpest steps are not.

The EA app is the campaign's most persistent friction: re-verification after every update, an un-disableable achievement overlay, and Linux/Proton fragility that makes playing the game at all harder than it should be. None of it touches the game itself, which runs clean and short at roughly six hours. The soundtrack is a standout, called out repeatedly and unprompted, and the closing letter about endings hiding new beginnings lands as the perfect tonal exit. The credits' sincerity reads as genuine rather than corporate. Publisher friction and game quality live in separate worlds here.

Overall, the yarn-as-body mechanic, the peaceful Nordic atmosphere and a standout soundtrack make this a gorgeous, warm puzzle platformer that more than carries itself past EA launcher headaches, unfindable collectibles in two levels and the occasional cheap difficulty spike. The puzzles satisfy throughout, and the visual craft has aged gracefully. A short, lovely journey worth revisiting or discovering fresh, with the music alone justifying the trip.
