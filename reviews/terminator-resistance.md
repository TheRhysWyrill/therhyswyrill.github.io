A post-Judgement-Day 2028 Los Angeles sends Jacob Rivers, a lowly survivor elevated into the resistance, through Skynet's war, and Terminator: Resistance turns a modest budget into a surprisingly complete open-sandbox shooter. Big open levels recall Metro Exodus in scale, loop back through earlier areas for satisfying recontextualisation, and scatter loot heavily enough to make scavenging a genuine loop rather than a chore. The human drama lands better than licensed conventions suggest, with Jennifer's confession, Aaron's missing husband and Baron's backstory all affecting, and choices to persuade survivors out of shelters shape the ending epilogue mechanically. Infiltrator paranoia, where discovery means hunting a disguised machine, provides the standout tonal beat.

The weapon-progression arc is the campaign's engine. Ballistic firearms give way to plasma once the rail plasma arrives within the first few hours, and the power curve from there is gleeful, one-shotting machines and tearing through Terminators with escalating damage. The Violet plasma closes the fantasy, and the chip system sustains it: harvested neural-net chips slot into weapons through a matching minigame, boosting damage, clip size and stability. Hacking adds a second loop, with Skynet turrets and terminals cracked through a Frogger-style minigame that turns the level itself into an ally, and security codes reprogram enemy weapons outright.

Mission design stays inventive throughout. Run-and-gun escape missions play as near-continuous rolling gunfights, outposts invite complete destruction with everything exploding in satisfying chain reactions, and returning to Pasadena reframes earlier ground. The music earns unprompted praise repeatedly across the campaign, from level themes to the finale's score, and the escalating Terminator roster, scout drones, spiders, silverfish, T800s, flamers, T47 walkers and HK tanks, stages a proper escalation. The infiltrator boss caps the roster with the franchise's signature dread.

Stealth is viable but mechanically discouraged. XP flows from kills, so avoiding fights starves progression, and the infested hospital's stealth section, while tonally strong, sits inside a system that punishes the patience it demands. Side quests split between charming and pointless, with simple fetch structures and an odd reward timing that pays out on completion rather than on turning the quest in, decoupling the payout from the delivery. Neither issue sinks the campaign, but both read as systems that needed one more balancing pass.

Audio mixing is the technical sore point. Dialogue and explosions blow out the ears while quieter cues vanish entirely, the imbalance recurs across specific missions, and the inconsistency is genuinely unbalanced rather than simply broken. The lock-picking stick control runs oddly, small-calibre ammo drops stay oddly sparse for the most common weapons, and the Annihilation Line DLC never released on Xbox at all, a sore point repeated at the start and close of the campaign. The skill tree, spanning hacking, lockpicking, crafting, toughness and weapons, rounds out a light RPG layer that works well.

Choices matter mechanically as well as narratively, with failed quests possible and the truth told to Aaron framed as a meaningful fork, and collectibles reward a thorough pass across the roughly ten-hour runtime. The stranger protecting Rivers, revealed through the time-travel thread dovetailing into the film timeline, gives the story a fan-pleasing spine. For a licensed game, the production values punch well above the budget weight.

Overall, Terminator: Resistance is a scrappy, surprising looter-shooter whose plasma power curve, chip-based upgrades and clever revisiting mission design make it far better than its budget suggests, even with unbalanced audio, XP-discouraged stealth and the missing DLC as honest costs. A licensed game that respects its source and respects the clock, and that combination is rarer than it should be.
