A city is being hollowed out by an amnesia plague: victims lose their memories, then their humanity, rendering as grey shadows indistinguishable from background crowds. A Void Hope follows an already-infected husband searching for the wife who returned to the city to give the scientists what the cure needs, her own healthy memories of love. The premise inverts the rescue narrative, and the finale's role reversal lands as the story's emotional pivot, seeded across a series of hidden memory vignettes that build the couple's history in fragments. Neon-lit melancholy carries the atmosphere: rain-soaked streets, a brewery, a cable-car depot and the church where the couple married. The soundtrack is one hell of a vibe, praised from the opening minutes to the final facility and the single most consistent positive throughout.

The hostile-NPC randomisation is the best design idea: the same shadowy crowd that walks past on one visit turns hostile the next, with nothing signposted to distinguish friend from threat. The crowd is the enemy and the crowd is the scenery, and the uncertainty is a mechanical metaphor for the plague itself, quietly reinforced by the grey-versus-blue art direction. It avoids the sparkly-object misdirection that plagues the genre. Few games make NPC hostility itself a puzzle input. The unpredictability gives every traversal a low hum of tension without ever resorting to jump scares.

The interlocked-level structure rewards attention: every area's item (crowbar, keycard, rope, fuel siphon, shotgun) unlocks progress somewhere previously visited, and shortcuts everywhere make returns painless. Each district carries a fixed checklist (item, memory, exit, vault computer), with every vault needing to open before the endgame vaults unlock. The crowbar's retroactive opening of an earlier district proves the weaving early. Deaths come from fall damage and the fast bird transformation rather than puzzles, and the difficulty stays gentle throughout. It is cool how interlocked everything is, and the design never once demands tedious backtracking.

Combat exists but barely: the shotgun arrives, some enemies die, but shooting never rises above crowd control, and the balance reads as a puzzle platformer with a weapon bolted on. Aiming locks the player in place, the single most-grumbled mechanical gripe, raised during fights and minigame sections alike. A research-facility finale briefly switches to first-person dungeon-crawler navigation for the organism extraction, a weird but workable change of pace. Controller UX adds friction (keyboard prompts while playing on pad, d-pad input drops), and the lack of a map in a metro-linked world is strange. The fast bird transformation kills more often than any enemy.

The ending is deliberately unresolved: the wife's fate, the Beast's purpose and the partially-unrecovered NPCs all stay hanging, and the national park's invincible shadow beast menaces the opening and is never explained. Post-run completion displays 82% after every checklist item is found, with the sparkle indicator never explained either. The ambiguities read as intentional for the story but sloppy for the systems: an unexplained completion counter is not mystery, it is missing UI. Both truths coexist in a package this brief. The memory vignettes (the wedding-church scene above all) earn the emotional weight the ending spends.

Elden Pixels' pedigree promised another Alwa-style platformer and delivered a moody virus thriller instead, a pleasant surprise despite the brevity: a single three-to-four-hour sitting, notably shorter than the studio's previous work. Comparisons to The Final Station's normal-looking enemies flatter the atmosphere. The pacing suits the story, and the density of ideas per hour is high. The memory-collect device gives completionists something to chase. It is really short, enjoyable but very brief, and the brevity is the only real complaint about scope.

Overall, a brief, soundtrack-driven puzzle platformer whose interlocked district design, NPC-hostility mechanic and memory-vignette storytelling deliver genuine atmosphere in a single sitting. Aim-locked shooting, an unexplained 82% counter and an ending that leaves the Beast and the couple's fate open are real costs, yet the vibe and the interlock make the detour worth taking.
