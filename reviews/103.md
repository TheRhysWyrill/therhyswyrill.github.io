A first-person corridor loop in the P.T. tradition: the same short stretch of hallway walked again and again while the room mutates around it. Dolls reappear, mirrors crack, writing changes, a teddy bear relocates, and a blank-faced doll watches from doorways. The featureless design of that doll, two eyes and a mask with no other facial features, is the creepiest thing in a package that never once needs to call itself horror, unsettling rather than scary, and better for it. The title itself is a piece of the puzzle.

Music and art style are the twin pillars. The soundtrack turns slowly from soothing to sinister as the descent deepens, a progression that rewards listening closely, and the art carries the cutesy-surface misdirection that makes the unsettled moments land harder. Atmosphere is built on creeping discomfort rather than jump scares, and the tension is sustained and real, the sort that makes a relocating teddy bear genuinely ominous rather than a gag.

The walk-and-stare loop is observe, trigger, loop, observe again. The mid-game introduces the mirror-crack mechanic, cracking mirrors in a specific order to escape the loop, and a drinking sequence whose inebriation degrades movement and vision as the horror escalates. The drinking mechanic is the standout idea: the game makes the player complicit in the impairment before it explains the crash. It is also the one moment of genuine fun, staring at a glass until it mysteriously disappears, and the willing surrender to it, letting it happen rather than fighting the logic, is exactly how the sequence wants to be played.

The reveal is environmental and devastating: a truck sound, a crash, newspaper clippings and a final judgement that reframes every hallway walked as guilt playing itself out. The loop was never the character's prison; it was the penance. No exposition up front, one sharp environmental gut-punch at the end, and the whole thing clicks into a coherent tragedy that rewards the hour spent wandering, the kind of compressed storytelling that turns confusion at the halfway mark into satisfaction at the credits.

Opacity cuts both ways. There is no guidance system of any kind, which preserves the mystery but costs several confused minutes at the mirror puzzle, the loop's one mechanical hurdle, with no hints and plenty of circling. The interaction system is finicky, with focus tied to camera-pointing rather than a dedicated button, felt most during the drinking sequence. Movement is floaty, a curiosity at first that becomes charming, and there is no sprint for the corridor grind.

Price is the whole argument. Under an hour of game at full asking price is steep by any honest measure, and the tension between craft and cost never resolves at list price; only a sale price closes the gap. No sprint button exists for the corridor grind, a passing wish rather than a grievance. The experience itself is genuinely sharp and enjoyable throughout, but the sticker asks more than the length supports, and that split is the honest verdict.

Overall, a genuinely sharp sub-hour corridor loop whose devastating guilt reveal, soothing-to-sinister soundtrack and creepy featureless dolls make it easy to admire and hard to price. The mirror puzzle's opacity and finicky focus interaction are minor costs, but the sticker price at full rates is the real obstacle, and only a discount makes the arithmetic honest.
