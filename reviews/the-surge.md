Creo sells atmospheric restoration as a corporate utopia, and the factory floor tells a different story: rogue workers, rogue AI mascots, and audio logs of Utopia nanites consuming human flesh, like Dr Murphy's body progressively overtaken by thick black veins underneath the skin. The Surge builds its sci-fi Souls-like on corporate-dystopia satire delivered through propaganda broadcasts and environmental storytelling, with the Creo World DLC's dark amusement-park failure as the sharpest satirical cut. Small human stories thread through the plant, from a hide-and-seek quest with a heartbreaking second outcome to the suicidal broadcaster Don Hackett, whose late-game segment lingers longer than most bosses. The premise of an exo-rig operator waking into carnage is lean. Everything interesting happens in the combat's anatomy.

Targeted dismemberment is the whole show: cutting specific limbs earns weapons and schematics, turning every fight into a planned loot run where the enemy's body is a menu. Targeting unarmoured parts deals damage while armoured parts yield schematics, and the slow-motion dismemberment kill-cam (tunable to fire only on new schematics) reinforces the loop without overstaying. Weapon proficiency and 2.0 variants earned through dismemberment conditions add depth, a double-edged system that is cool and kind of dumb in equal measure. No other Souls-like makes combat a loot game, and years later the loop remains distinctive. Planning gear routes around specific enemies for specific pieces is the core appeal.

The interconnected, shortcut-driven facility design earns its praise, unlocking lift after lift and shortcut after shortcut until journeys back collapse into seconds. Medbays bank tech scrap, a stress-relief upgrade over the usual corpse-run that lets aggressive play stay affordable, and implants hot-swap or inject for build flexibility. Armour classes (Lynx, Rhino, Goliath and beyond) mix and match for speed-versus-defence trade-offs, and core power upgrades at Medbays gate progression cleanly. The Good, the Bad and the Augmented testing chambers integrate fast travel naturally. Drone combat modules round out a systems suite that respects planning over grinding.

The lock-on system is both the best combat feature and the biggest mechanical gripe: switching body parts works, but switching between multiple targets is near impossible, and the trigger-based method stays undiscovered until late. Against grouped enemies the camera fights the targeting, and the biggest fights punish the interface as much as the reflexes. Stamina management demands relearning after any absence, with attack spam punished hard. Fall damage is wildly inconsistent, sometimes killing from short drops, other times not triggering at all from much greater falls. None of these break the loop, but all of them add friction the design never earns back.

Only five bosses inhabit the base game, and the Big Sister and Rogue Process fights stand out as the stand-out frustrations rather than highlights. The finale's nanite-fused confrontation on the launchpad closes on an ominous government-strike-team epilogue, and the story's corporate horror lands better through its logs than its set pieces. The DLC structure annoys: A Walk in the Park cuts off partway and sends progress back into the main game, and the Fire & Ice weapon DLC ambushes the opening hours with enemies beyond early builds. The security-clearance door sweep and Chrysalis mark-five armour set reward full completion. Completion is achievable across roughly twenty hours with everything.

The audio-log storytelling carries the horror, none better than Murphy's narration of his own consumption, and the propaganda broadcasts keep the satire humming between fights. Arina and Sally's side threads give the facility a human pulse beneath the machines. The arena episodes extend the combat loop after the credits without narrative pretence. What ages best is the anatomy of the combat: every enemy's body a menu, every limb a potential build decision. What ages worst is the interface that mediates it. Both truths coexist comfortably in a game confident enough to be strange.

Overall, a sci-fi Souls-like whose limb-cutting loot loop remains distinctive and satisfying years later, wrapped in smart interconnected level design and a genuinely creepy corporate-horror narrative. The finicky target switching, inconsistent fall damage and DLC gating are real seams, yet the dismemberment combat and bankable scrap economy justify the trip regardless.
