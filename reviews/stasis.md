Aboard the Groom Lake, a deep-space research vessel where John Maracheck wakes from stasis into a slaughterhouse, the horror is corporate and clinical rather than visceral. The tone is grim body horror built almost entirely from recovered crew PDAs, with nurses, janitors, engineers and mad doctors each documenting the ship's collapse in their own voice, from child experimentation under Project Seed to a quasi-intelligent fungus. The signature is the PDA-archaeology structure: the actual plot is assembled entirely from dozens of interlocking first-person logs, letting the tragedy be reconstructed in any order. The corporate-satire texture of the Kane Corporation, budget-cut fire-axe notes and the Visitors' Center propaganda gives it a flavour closer to retro sci-fi horror literature than jump-scare games. Spending most of the playtime reading is the intended experience, and it works.

The hover-to-inspect mechanic and item descriptions earn immediate praise, delivering context without a click. The point-and-click puzzle design on the whole lands, mixing inventory-logic chains such as wiring the DNA sequencer, the pump-station directional diagram and the vaporiser pheromone with genuine atmosphere. The breaker-box infirmary, the Yuri hand biometric scan and the turret-reprogramming stealth corridor are particular highlights, and the self-surgery PDT removal sequence carries a dread comparable to a certain eye-poke scene in a famous sci-fi horror. Inventory oddities range from a dead man's toe tag to eyeballs, queen pheromone and an acid glove, all of which combine across multi-screen chains. The length runs well past genre expectations, and the density rewards it.

The worldbuilding density is the engine: the Groom Lake's corporate history, the Eugenics War backstory, and interconnected crew dramas across the Harry, Isabella and Grant triangle, Erica and Sadie, Dr Al Raji, Buckman and D Santos supply constant material. Sarah the nurse's horror at cracking a pod with a child inside, the Samantha creature's containment failures and Dr Milan's God-complex final entries build a dread that peaks in the hybrid birthing lab. The logs' content is genuinely disturbing, and the darkness is earned. The crew's voices stay distinct throughout, and no two documents read alike. The methodical pace is the correct way through.

The horror label is the honest mismatch. Creatures rarely threaten directly, appearing in scripted glimpses and one vat sequence, and never present in any meaningful way. The multi-pod stasis-dosage puzzle is incredibly cryptic, and the PDA-attached clues do not specify body parts, producing the game's one real head-scratcher. Navigating screens is disorienting, with John's on-screen position after transitions unpredictable, and running via double-click is undiscoverable for hours. Loud, frequent stingers and creature noises repeatedly drown out dialogue, and subtitles carry the audio. The Tea and Hensley betrayal twist lands more as shock than satisfaction. The bleakness is a feature, but the scares are not.

A late-game crash roughly ten minutes from the ending costs real progress because the game lacks crash-proof autosaving, and per-area saves do not protect against a crash-adjacent failure. Settings are barebones to the point of absurdity, with no in-game options at all and a separate launcher for anything beyond window size. The stinger volume and the cryptic dosage clues compound the friction. None of it is fatal, and the short redo softens the crash's sting. The experience rewards the patience it demands.

The stinger-heavy sound design is the honest cost of the atmosphere, and the per-area autosaving means a crash costs real progress. The double-click running discovery arrives far too late, and the where's-Wally navigation between screens taxes patience the puzzles already demand. The horror label is the one thing the game cannot quite deliver, and the creatures are atmosphere rather than threat. The bleak ending lands as shock rather than satisfaction, and the betrayal's mechanics land harder than its emotion. None of it is fatal, and the experience rewards the patience it demands.

Overall, a genuinely rewarding slow-burn sci-fi horror adventure whose brilliance lives in its documents rather than its monsters, with the PDA-archaeology structure, atmospheric puzzle chains and dense corporate worldbuilding as the achievements. Thin creature presence, disorienting navigation, cryptic dosage clues and a save system that fails when it matters are honest costs, and the pacing rewards anyone who savours every document.
