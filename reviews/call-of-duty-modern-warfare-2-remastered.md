The classic Modern Warfare 2 plot returns with modern polish: a stolen module triggers a Russian invasion of the United States, a task force hunts an infamous terrorist, and a general betrays his own team to claim the glory. The shock beats hit exactly as hard as their reputation promises. The most infamous mission in the series' history remains disturbing but effective, and the choice to participate or not gives the sequence its power; a later comparison to a World War II entry's forced flamethrower objective makes the distinction clear, since choosing matters here. The betrayal lands as one of the medium's great villain turns, genuinely surprising even for players who know the campaign's reputation.

Price's rescue and characterisation remain a highlight. The gulag sequence builds to one of the series' best character reintroductions, and the captain's presence anchors the second half of the campaign. The mission roster reads like a greatest-hits album: snow infiltration with a heartbeat sensor, a snowmobile chase, riot protection with Predator missiles and Javelins, an oil-rig hostage rescue, the White House defence under a timed weapons-release countdown, a safe-house defence that goes loud, and a boat chase and knife fight to close. Stealth-flavoured missions that can go sideways without failing are a consistent favourite.

The betrayal's construction deserves particular attention. The general's motive (claiming credit for the war's end and branding his own soldiers terrorists to bury the evidence) is spelled out in a villain monologue that would be clumsy in lesser hands but here plays as genuinely chilling, and the safe-house sequence that precedes it gives the turn real emotional weight. The build-up across the campaign's middle acts is careful enough that the reveal reads as tragedy rather than twist for its own sake, and the finale's revenge framing feels earned rather than vengeful.

The visual uplift over the 2009 original is substantial. Lighting, textures and character models all benefit, and the remaster presentation makes the set pieces land harder than memory suggests. Remaster-era quality-of-life touches help too: hit markers, red crosshairs on enemies, and no campaign failure for friendly fire, with the game content to call the player a numpty rather than restart the mission. A post-game museum with unlockable mission modifiers is a remaster novelty worth a look.

The campaign is short. At roughly four to five hours, it is the shortest entry in the series by a distance, and the padding-free pacing is both the campaign's virtue and its limitation. The long, verbose title (specifying campaign remastering in full) remains an amusement, and the absence of multiplayer is worth knowing going in: this is purely the campaign, as the title's verbosity warns.

Friction is minor. Dense market and combat environments make enemies hard to track, with too many angles of approach and too many places for hostiles to appear, and smoke and chaos make friendly identification genuinely difficult, producing repeated friendly-fire incidents even with the forgiving failure state. Neither issue breaks the experience, but both are felt in the busier missions.

Overall, this is a tight, four-hour masterpiece of set pieces and betrayals that proves its controversy was earned and its classic status deserved. The shock beats, the captain's rescue and the villain's turn all land, the visual uplift and friendlier failure states modernise the experience without sanding off its character, and the stealth missions that can go loud remain the series' best template. Short, sharp and thoroughly enjoyable.
