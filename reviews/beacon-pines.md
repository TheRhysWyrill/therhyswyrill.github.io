A 1930s-flavoured small town of anthropomorphic animals hides a missing mother, a suspicious fertiliser corporation and a grandmother whose basement doubles as a conspiracy board, all narrated by a book whose pages the player literally writes. The premise lands as cosy Americana with a conspiratorial undertow, and the storybook framing device, where the narrator comments on events as they unfold, gives the whole thing a wickedly literary register. It is a story-first experience, and the writing sustains the tone across every branch.

The word-charm branching system is the whole reason to be here: at Turning Points, a collected charm word slots into the story to redirect it, and the absurdity is half the fun, from tickling a bully unconscious to a rewrite that turns a nuisance character into a mutant. Death endings are met with delight rather than frustration because the Chronicle lets every failure be rewound freely, making bad branches reversible experiments rather than punishments. Compared with earlier branching adventures that offered two paths, the three-or-four-option turns here feel genuinely generous.

The art style is the initial hook and never stops earning its place, a gorgeous storybook aesthetic carried by character animation of flailing arms and a lean move whose only purpose appears to be looking cool. The main trio of young characters carry the story with genuine quirk, and the untrustworthy-adults narrative engine works completely, with every suspicious grown-up tracked across the mystery. The twist-heavy plot stays one step ahead throughout, from a dumpster body-scare through a phone-booth drop to a reveal that flips the whole story into science fiction, and the finale lands emotionally where it matters.

The single true path is the honest limitation: despite the branching, the story funnels to one proper ending, and the good ending requires deliberately exploring the bad ones first. It softens the conceit without denting it, since experiencing every branch is framed as necessary rather than wasted, but players expecting genuinely divergent finales will notice the rails. The Chronicle structure at least makes the funnel feel like an argument about fate rather than a technical shortfall.

Pacing dips in the town-exploration stretches, where fishing, errand-running and light side systems are pleasant but slow, and the diner minigame sequence charms once before padding out on the revisit required for full endings. A silent title screen unsettles on arrival, an odd choice given how much audio personality the rest of the package carries. Occasional wrong-button confusion on the controller mapping causes minor fumbles, the only technical note in a run otherwise free of crashes or gripes.

Roughly six and a half to seven hours cover a near-complete run, every charm collected and every pond fished dry, with no fail states beyond story death, which is free to undo. The collectible-charm loop feeds exploration naturally, letters and side systems reward thoroughness without demanding it, and the length suits a story this tightly plotted. Nothing outstays its welcome, and the confidence of the execution makes even the smallest touches feel considered, from the first Turning Point choice to the final page.

Overall, a charming branching mystery where single charm words reroute the plot at Turning Points and the Chronicle turns death endings into reversible experiments, all wrapped in gorgeous storybook art and a cast of weird, well-drawn kids. The single-true-path funnel and some padded exploration stretches are real but forgivable, and the whole thing plays like a beautiful, clever book that gets to be written.
