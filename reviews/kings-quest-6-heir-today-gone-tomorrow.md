A prince sails three months across the sea after glimpsing a princess in a magic mirror, is shipwrecked on the shores of the Green Isles, and finds her kingdom quietly poisoned: the ferry closed, the islands feuding over stolen treasures, and the princess sequestered for a wedding nobody wants. The vizier's plot gives the fairy tale genuine political intrigue, and the mystery of who turned the islands against each other is constructed well enough to be crackable by the player before the game confirms it. The fairy-tale density is remarkable, weaving Beauty and the Beast, a minotaur prophecy, genies, druids and an actual journey to the underworld into a single island-hopping quest. The romance at the centre of it all is warmer and more mature than anything the earlier chapters attempted, and it grounds the intrigue in something worth fighting for.

The multi-island quest structure is the richest the classic line ever produced. A magic map unlocks travel between the islands, the pawn shop runs a copper-coin barter economy in which a nightingale becomes a flute, a flute a tinder box, a tinder box a paint brush, and the round-robin continues until every item has mattered, and the spell book demands gathered ingredients (swamp ooze, baby's tears, sacred water, black feather) for make-rain and magic-paint spells. The druids' grove, the winged ones' cliff, the minotaur's labyrinth and the pawnbroker's counter each carry their own ecology of items and characters. Interlocking systems this dense had no peer in the catalogue.

The catacombs provide the most cautious stretch of play, their ceiling-gear crusher and minotaur the game's sharpest surviving dangers, and the tension of navigating them rewards diligent saving at every step. The save system itself is unusually generous, offering plenty of characters for save names, a small courtesy that encourages exactly the careful habits the catacombs demand.

The realm of the dead is the game's peak. The L-O-V-E riddle, the Charon crossing and finally the challenge of making Death itself weep, accomplished with the mirror of truth rather than any weapon, close the underworld chapter on a note no combat could match, and the image of death shedding a single gray tear as the mirror cracks remains the production's finest moment. Few adventures of the era, or since, resolved their climax through truth rather than violence.

Death counts sit notably low by the series' historical standards, and the softening is deliberate. Dangers exist but are rarely sprung without warning, the branching design means fewer one-way mistakes, and the result is the least cruel chapter of the classic era. The real barriers have migrated rather than vanished: the cliffs-of-logic letter puzzles are opaque enough that it is genuinely unclear how anyone would derive them from the game alone, closer in spirit to manual-gated puzzles than fair deduction, and the island-treasure deduction chain may still send purists hunting for a guide. Neither barrier is unfair so much as unexplained, and both are the price of a puzzle design this ambitious.

The voice work deserves particular praise. One distinctive actor voices a remarkable number of roles across the islands, and spotting the repetitions becomes its own quiet game, while a familiar singing-skeleton gag rewards series followers. The 1992 introduction holds up surprisingly well for its era, with pseudo-3D staging that impressed then and still reads as handsome now. The icon bar, for all its newness, occasionally obscures which pixel is actually clickable, a small interface friction in an otherwise confident presentation. The wedding infiltration finale ties every system together: the lamp-swap heist executed by the court clown, the false-genie reveal that flips the ceremony, and the branching-route consequences all converge on an ending whose warmth is earned rather than inherited. The romance thread seeded in the previous chapter finally pays off here, and the theatrical staging of the ceremony's undoing is among the most satisfying climaxes the studio ever shipped.

Overall, this is the most confident, least cruel and richest chapter of the classic era, and its high-water-mark reputation is deserved. The barter chain, spell-crafting, branching routes and the make-Death-weep underworld challenge stack more interlocking design than any sibling, the mystery plotting rewards attention, and the finale delivers theatrical payoffs few adventures of any era match. Opacity in the cliffs-of-logic puzzles, the unexplained deduction chains, the icon bar's pixel ambiguity and one agonisingly missed scoring point are the only blemishes on a close-to-perfect fairy-tale adventure.
