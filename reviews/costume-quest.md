The core concept remains the entire appeal: costumes that transform children into what they imagine, played completely straight in combat and entirely earnest in tone. Pretending to be a robot or a vampire and knowing, in the mind's eye, that the transformation is real is exactly the kind of thing childhood Halloween is for, and the game captures it with rare fidelity. Trick-or-treating doubles as the exploration loop, with candy as currency and monsters on a corporate candy heist with quarterly targets. The Halloween-economy satire is wholly its own. As a light-hearted RPG about sibling rescue and imagination, it has no real competitor.

Costumes function as classes, each carrying distinct specials: the robot's missile barrage, Lady Liberty's heal, the ninja's surprise damage, the vampire's full-party bat heal and the pumpkin's half-health area attack. Testing each one is genuinely enjoyable, and the ninja's damage output borders on absurd, re-energising the back half once discovered. Battle stamps work like badges, layering passive effects over the turn structure. Paper Mario-style timing attacks and blocks anchor the combat in familiar rhythm. The costume balance is playful rather than rigorous, which suits the tone.

The Grubbins on Ice DLC adds a monster-revolution setting with a tyrant, a lovesick elder and a Yeti Fest, and its charm matches the base campaign. Its final boss is the sole genuine low point: an RNG-reliant flying phase that desyncs with an unstoppable follow-up attack, leaving blocking impossible and victory hostage to luck. A fixed hit cycle would have kept the phases aligned and the fight fair. Everything else in the DLC lands, including the boss's own comedy framing. One bad fight does not undo ninety pleasant minutes.

Combat depth comes almost entirely from the costume roster rather than the encounter design, and the balance lands on the generous side throughout. The ninja's damage output turns mid-game fights into brief ceremonies, the vampire's late-game party heal erases most of the challenge, and experimenting with which two specials to field is the tactical decision that matters most. Bosses hit hard enough to demand timed blocks, but the difficulty curve stays deliberately gentle from the first suburb to the DLC's ice fields. The leniency suits the material: a game about childhood imagination would be poorly served by a punishing one.

The PC version's auto-advancing cutscene text ruins dialogue pacing, rushing lines past before they can land, a problem not remembered from the console versions. The neighbourhood's lack of a map makes finding the last un-trick-or-treated houses a genuine chore, since each area gates progression behind clearing every single door. XP requirements balloon absurdly between levels, from a few thousand early to six figures late, without any matching difficulty escalation. None of these breaks the experience; all of them are felt. The mapless layout is the worst of the three by a distance.

Two sessions cover roughly four and a half hours for the base campaign plus ninety minutes of DLC, with one hundred per cent completion including every creepy treat card, stamp and hide-and-seek child along the way. The deliberately casual leniency is a feature of the design, not a flaw to be fixed, and the joke density sustains the short runtime. Middling middle-management monsters, a bobbing-for-apples health lobbyist and a Victorian chimney-sweep pager advertisement keep the humour adult-friendly without ever tipping into crudeness. The tone is endearing rather than childish throughout. Few games this size are this consistently funny.

Overall, the costume-transformation concept, the Halloween-economy comedy and the Paper Mario-style combat make this a really good, charming RPG whose only true failures are one RNG-dependent DLC boss and the PC port's auto-advancing text. The mapless neighbourhood hunt for the last doors drags in the middle, and the XP inflation is meaningless padding. Everything else, from the specials to the satire to the seasonal warmth, holds up beautifully on a revisit.
