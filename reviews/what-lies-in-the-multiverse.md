A cartoony multiverse-hopping adventure opens with murderous grandmas, zombie-infested towns and theatre-troupe ambushes, complete chaos played for laughs with total confidence, and What Lies in the Multiverse spends its first hours gleefully proving that tone can be a punchline. Then the pivot arrives, and the comedy gives way to grief and guilt with a weight few games of this register attempt. The game itself warns up front about themes of grief and mental health, and the warning is earned. The final act reframes everything as a meditation on destiny, control and the inability to face one's own mistakes, and the writing carries the pivot without flinching.

Universe-switching is the single core verb, and it does double duty as puzzle mechanic and narrative metaphor. Switching transforms terrain, boxes, water, gravity and enemy threats instantaneously, free-form rather than fixed to special points, and the escalation runs from box pushing to elevator-card mazes to inverted-gravity facility rooms. Interferences, zones where switching locks or forces, act as puzzle modifiers and story signals alike. The worlds escalate cleverly, with a lethal-breath universe making switching itself the hazard, and the mechanic never goes stale across the full nine chapters.

The humour stays sharp throughout the lighter chapters. Fourth-wall-leaning gags, achievement names that riff on other games (an oxygen-themed one in particular) and a zen monastery bit about Wi-Fi without passwords land repeatedly, and the self-awareness extends to a title screen bright enough to blind on the wrong television. Deaths are frequent but cheap, brief QTE chase set-pieces punctuate the chapters, and the platforming serves the story rather than competing with it. Mostly story with platforming and puzzles mixed in is a fair description, and the mix works.

Puzzles occasionally outsmart their author rather than their player. The train box sequence in particular stumps for a long stretch, an obtuseness of arrangement rather than logic, and spotting the misplaced box resolves it with a self-directed shrug. Most of the puzzle design stays pleasant and fair, escalating without cruelty, and the satisfaction of a correctly read multiverse outweighs the few walls. The train chapter and the Omega Orionis stretches, though, outstay their welcome, drawing impatience before their payoffs finally arrive.

The story's structure rewards attention. Blue name colours, a scarf and a photograph seed the clues that let the silent protagonist's identity assemble correctly before the reveal confirms it, and the murder arc, the former assistant and the reframe of the antagonist's motives give the ending genuine weight. A post-credits scene delivers a final gift that lands as a satisfying button on the whole tale. The tonal whiplash between slapstick and grief is deliberate, and the sequencing makes it work.

The art style keeps both registers legible, cartoonish enough for the comedy and clean enough for the heavy beats to land without visual clutter. Roughly six to seven hours cover the nine chapters, a length the pacing mostly justifies apart from the two indulgent stretches. The ending sticks with the kind of resonance that few comedy-first adventures attempt, and the epilogue rewards patience.

Overall, What Lies in the Multiverse is a self-aware multiverse romp that earns its dark ending, with universe-switching doing admirable double duty as puzzle verb and narrative metaphor, and a writing pivot that lands with real force. Occasional puzzle obtuseness and two overlong chapters are honest costs, and the whole adds up to one of the more confident tonal tightrope walks in recent memory.
