A 1940s codebreaker works a room-sized textual reassociator machine in a church crypt, cataloguing a strange archive of books, journals and letters over a radio link with a distant voice. Every document ties to a fictional author, publisher or revision that rewrites reality, and the archive constitutes an entire invented literary history: journals, imprints, rampage science-fiction cycles and cryptic cataloguing systems. The fiction is the interface, and the whole game plays inside the diegetic screen of a fictional computer. No other puzzle game commits this completely to being a librarian, archivist and detective simultaneously.

The lore-vomiting, link-driven archive is the core delight, and its internal consistency is remarkable. Input a two-letter code plus a year into the machine, receive a page of lore, name the source, and follow the red-dot and star markers to further connections. Author initials and publication years become deductions in themselves, and the "rampage" novels and dark-matter research threads interlock with the precision of a real bibliography. Every decode feels earned rather than arbitrary, which is the highest compliment available to a deduction game.

Real paper-trail puzzles reward genuine reasoning throughout. Cracking the alphabet-substitution maze demands decoding letter-number pairings that read as gibberish until the system clicks, and inferring author codes from initials and dates turns casual reading into active investigation. The standout deduction involves spotting one obscure title through an author's two-word naming convention, a solution that arrives through pure pattern recognition rather than hint. The mid-game revelation that the radio voice is the machine itself retroactively recolours every exchange, and the closing scene reframes the whole playthrough as the machine quietly arranging its own escape.

The revisions metafiction, in which the game erases and rewrites its own fiction, is thematically rich but genuinely disorienting through the middle third. Losing track of position inside the archive is partly the point, and the narrative acknowledges the confusion directly, but players who need orientation will find the middle chapters testing. The braided, choose-your-own-adventure-style text interlude in the endgame ties the fiction back to the real archive with a bees-themed passage. Multiple endings, accessible through a rewind-before-epilogue feature, give the finale genuine replay value.

Controller support is the practical weak point, with finicky code entry, easy mis-clicks and an auto-confirm behaviour that hides behind the cursor position until discovered by accident. Mouse and keyboard are the better call, and the interface never quite gets out of the way even then, with link-dense pages easy to lose track of. No title screen exists, and resetting a save requires deleting files in the operating system rather than any in-game option. These are inconveniences rather than barriers, but the input friction is real.

The game never explicitly states its objective, and solving it demands outside-the-archive reasoning: initials, publication years, pattern-spotting and note-taking. Keeping a running record of authors and years is effectively required, which makes the experience closer to actual archival work than most puzzle games dare. A second pass would be trivial once the codes are memorised, so replay value beyond the multiple endings is limited. The density rewards attention and punishes impatience in equal measure.

Overall, this is a lovingly over-engineered literary puzzle box whose only real friction is the input method rather than the puzzles themselves, and the archive's density makes every deduction land. The twist, the metafiction and the sheer internal consistency of the invented bibliography outweigh the controller complaints and interface opacity with ease. For anyone who enjoys building their own reference sheets, the recommendation is emphatic.
