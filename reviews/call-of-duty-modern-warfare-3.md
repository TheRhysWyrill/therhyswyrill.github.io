The conclusion of the original trilogy opens with Russia invading Europe under a manipulative villain's orchestration, and the campaign stitches together Delta Force, SAS and Task Force 141 storylines into one genuinely global war. The globe-trotting structure never repeats itself: sub infiltration and a harbour escape in flooded New York, a presidential plane hijack, a Sierra Leone stealth crawl, a Somali compound raid with remote chopper turrets, Paris gas-mask catacombs, Prague overwatch, a night raid on a castle, collapsing Berlin and a Siberian diamond-mine rescue before a Juggernaut-armoured Dubai assault. Few campaigns in the series pack this much geographic variety into a single war.

The London terror-attack vignette is the campaign's standout shock. A family on holiday, a truck, and a moment of civilian horror that no marketing spoiled: the sequence lands as a genuine gut-punch precisely because it is unexpected in scale and intimacy, and the game commits to the consequence rather than cutting away. It sets the register for everything that follows, a war where the cost reaches people who never picked a side.

The willingness to kill central characters permanently is the trilogy closer's boldest virtue. A beloved soldier dies of wounds after the Prague ambush, a redemption-arc ally dies on the final mission, and the survival of the team is never guaranteed, which gives every firefight genuine stakes. Nothing is worse than character deaths without meaning, and this campaign understands that: the deaths land because they are final, earned and consequences of the story's logic rather than shock for its own sake.

The finale delivers catharsis with total commitment. The rooftop confrontation, a cable, and a look of terror on the villain's face: Makarov's end is visceral, personal and completely deserved, the punctuation the trilogy spent three games earning. The story reaches a proper conclusion, a rarity in a series built on cliffhangers, and the closing credits finally let the war feel finished. Yuri's redemption arc and Price's ruthless focus give the ending its emotional weight.

The permanent deaths recontextualise the whole trilogy on reflection. A soldier's survival in earlier entries always felt protected by plot armour, and this campaign dismantles that comfort deliberately, letting Sandman's team go down in Berlin and refusing rescue-fantasy resolutions elsewhere. The AC-130 and Warhammer fire-support sequences and the UGV minigun and tank-column vehicle interludes give the campaign its mechanical variety between story beats, and the massively spammable Predator missiles reward the sandbox's generosity. The cast stitching (Delta Force, SAS and Task Force 141 sharing one war) pays off in the finale's convergence.

Friction is technical rather than structural. The PC port lacks controller support, the only entry from the series' modern era to do so, forcing a keyboard-and-mouse playthrough that proves serviceable except for the grenade-throw-back interaction bound to middle mouse, an awkward reach in the fights that demand it most. Enemies are often invisible while shooting, singling the player out from cover the game never reveals, and grenade spam adds injury to opacity. Blackout and muffled audio glitches disrupt several firefights, and the mission-select completion percentage penalises skipped intel with numbers nobody asked for. The unskippable credits run long.

Overall, this is the underdog of the original trilogy, and it sticks the landing: permanent, meaningful character deaths, a London vignette that still shocks, a cathartic rooftop finale and a globe-spanning war that concludes properly. Invisible enemies, muffled audio glitches and the keyboard-only PC port are real annoyances, and the unskippable credits test patience, but none of it undermines a courageously lethal, conclusive campaign that earns its ending.
