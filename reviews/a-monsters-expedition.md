There is no plot to speak of: a monster museum curator tours the flooded ruins of England, salvaging human artefacts for outdoor display, and the entire story lives in the exhibit plaques. Each discovered exhibit is a deadpan monster-expert bit reinterpreting mundane objects with total sincerity, from queuing and velvet ropes to a leech-powered economy, and the comedy never dips across 160 exhibits. The tone is warm, silly and distinctly British, and the emergency cheese sandwich's misuse fine confirms the national register immediately. The puzzles exist to pace out the writing, and the writing rewards every solved island. The plaques alone would justify a book.

The core loop is deceptively simple: push trees. Standing logs roll, felled logs become rafts, two logs side by side make a bridge, and falling into water launches a raft ride that can carry an entire region. Mailboxes fast-travel, islands interlock, and the late game layers in dependencies, including one island that required solving all the others first, a reveal that retroactively reorganises the whole map. The rule-set is tiny but the implications sprawl, and limitations like being unable to push while standing on a stump are never spelled out. Learning them by failure is the design working as intended.

The chill-but-devious difficulty curve is the game's structural achievement. It is approachable at first, one of the best pure chill experiences, and then the late-game islands turn deceptively hard while the same verb set stays interesting for hours. Multiple valid routes per puzzle reward experimentation, and unintended raft launches deliver genuine surprise-and-delight. The world opens dramatically on the first long raft launch, and the sheer scale of the interconnected archipelago keeps surprising long after it should have run out. Slow-zoom camera, looping folk-jazz and islands that un-fog as they fall complete the cosy atmosphere.

A few islands are genuinely impenetrable, most notably a small one with one big and one small log whose required approach point is effectively invisible. It is the one blemish on completion, and the reason many will finish well short of the full exhibit count. No searchable failure state exists for the completionist path either, with no area names or descriptions, so looking up specific solutions is functionally impossible. The map-screen framerate collapse recurs whenever zoomed out, nagging constantly on a game light enough to run anywhere. None of it breaks the core, but all of it is friction.

Completionist overload is real. Over 700 islands, several reachable only via rafts launched from distant shores, make 100% a feat rather than a goal, and the respect the feat earns does not make the wall less real. The protected-tree gotcha, where a cool bird nesting in a trunk forbids cutting it down even a little, is a charming red herring rather than a flaw. Unlimited undo and reset and no fail state mean self-directed frustration is the only punishment. The world map reveals remaining exhibits once the main expedition ends. The dev pedigree shows in every design choice.

The design's lineage from a certain snowman puzzler is legible immediately, and scaling that tight rule-set to an open archipelago is the ambitious leap. No other puzzle game marries Sokoban-adjacent mechanics to a full comic-writing engine this cleanly. The bench-sitting, unlimited-coffee pacing gives the whole thing an unhurried rhythm that makes the hard walls fair. Roughly six and a half hours covers the campaign plus a partial cleanup pass, and full completion is a genuinely expert undertaking. The writing rewards every minute of it.

Overall, a genuinely great, deceptively difficult, endlessly funny puzzle world whose tree-pushing loop, raft traversal and dependency islands keep the same tiny verb set interesting for hours. A handful of impenetrable late islands, no searchable way to look up the last few and a map-screen framerate dip are honest costs, and full completion is one hell of a feat, but the deadpan plaques are worth the trip alone.
