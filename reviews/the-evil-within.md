A detective investigating a mass killing at a psychiatric institution is pulled into a machine that links minds, and the nightmare unfolding inside a killer's brain becomes the campaign's stage. The premise allows locations to teleport and rewrite themselves, a psychological logic that pays off early in rain-soaked courtyards and a village sequence that openly channels the survival-horror classic it worships. Journals, tapes and flashbacks unpack the machine-creator's tragedy with surprising patience, and the barn fire, the sister's fate and the betrayals all seed the eventual revelations. Early atmosphere is genuinely strong, even if the dream logic eventually dissolves the coherence it builds.

The first half earns its reputation. Stealth is a genuinely viable option, with hide spots, sneak kills and bottle throws giving every encounter a choice between silence and gunfire, and the Agony Crossbow's craftable bolt types (harpoon, explosive, shock, flash, frost) turn the environment into a toolkit. Resource scarcity defines the pacing: ammunition stays critically low across most of the campaign, with usually one weapon adequately stocked and the rest holding a few rounds each. The scarcity suits the horror register, and the village chapter stands as the campaign's atmospheric peak. Visually the game still looks excellent more than a decade on, and the presentation holds its own against later entries.

The second half nose-dives. Enemies acquire indestructible masks and machine guns, stealth stops working, and encounters degrade into tedious shooting galleries that squander the systems the opening chapters built. Enemies generally refuse to stagger, even with a chunk of their head blown off, and the absence of a stun-and-follow-up option removes the most satisfying loop its inspiration offered. What remains is attrition: more enemies, more masks, more ammunition burned on encounters that no longer reward patience. The tonal drift from horror into blunt combat is real, and the nightmare geography stops compensating for it.

Boss design is the campaign's deepest flaw. Every boss carries an instant-kill grab, easy to be caught regardless of how clearly the wind-up telegraphs, and death-by-cinematic accounts for the overwhelming majority of failures across the campaign. The Amalgam Alpha's swipes cover half the arena, the chainsaw-wielding Sadist and the safe-headed Keeper both kill on contact, and the Laura fight in a cramped arena demands fire in every possible form. Instant kills do not raise difficulty; they convert it into tedium, and the Chapter 9 mansion, where a teleporting stalker randomly spawns to erase exploration, is the design's nadir. The penalty for curiosity is deletion.

Smaller friction accumulates around the margins. The over-the-shoulder camera sits too close, hold-to-sneak defaults to toggle within minutes, and one elevator encounter never telegraphs that its box-headed attackers must actually be killed. Trap disarming doubles as economy and hazard, trading parts for a two-strike death sentence, and the match-based corpse burning feels pointless in a game that rarely asks for backtracking. The dream-within-a-dream story leaves the reality of any event genuinely uncertain, which may be intention but reads as incoherence.

The DLC is the redemption. Assignment and Consequence deliver tight, stealth-focused chapters with creative light-manipulation puzzles and essentially no flaws, and their side-character perspective retroactively explains the base game's coldest mysteries. Executioner trades tension for a first-person power fantasy, tremendous fun until its final phase is ruined by the same insta-kill wall-hands mechanic the main campaign overuses. Together the three expansions add roughly six hours of material, and two of the three outclass the campaign that hosts them.

Overall, this is a very solid and very frustrating experience whose first half justifies the recommendation and whose second half taxes it, carried by scarce resources, viable stealth and visuals that have aged gracefully. Instant-kill boss grabs, non-staggering masked enemies and the teleporting-stalker mansion are genuine design failures, and the story's dream logic sacrifices coherence for ambition. Play it for the strong opening, the atmosphere and the excellent stealth DLC, and the flaws remain survivable.
