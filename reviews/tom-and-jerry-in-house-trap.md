A cartoon cat and mouse wage house-wide warfare across fifteen levels of split-screen slapstick, and the design treats the entire home as a munitions depot. Kitchen appliances become ambush weapons, garden paths turn into choke points, and the duel never leaves the arena of the household. Levels borrow their names from classic episodes, and the tone never strays from Saturday-morning chaos. The licensed curio of its era, this one built something genuinely distinct out of its property.

The trap system carries the whole design, and it is configured to hurt rather than merely inconvenience. Trapped cookers, televisions, vacuums, dynamite and mouse traps all deal real damage, so the correct way to play is to turn every room into a minefield before the opponent walks through it. Hazards affect both sides equally, which gives the duel a symmetric tension reminiscent of twin-screen spy puzzle games from the same generation. Thrown items extend the arsenal, and the loop of baiting, trapping and punishing is consistently satisfying.

Combat rounds alternate with collect-item races: gathering eggs, rousing the sleeping bulldog from the garden, and retrieving lost keepsakes for the cat's sweetheart. Objectives rotate every few levels, and new rooms unlock in batches as progress accumulates, expanding the house from kitchen to basement to attic to locked-off wings. Level structures stay short, measured in minutes, and the whole campaign wraps in under an hour with a bare credits roll at level fifteen. Nothing outstays its welcome.

Melee weapons (broom, bat, shovel, ladle) expose the design's weakest joint, because landing a hit on the opponent is unreliable to the point of impossibility. Swings whiff against the AI with regularity, which pushes every viable strategy toward thrown objects and pre-set traps instead. In an odd way this flaw defines the experience, since the trap loop is where the game excels, but rounds can still slip away purely because a swing refused to connect. Hit registration this loose demands a workaround rather than offering a choice.

The missing ability to put down or swap a held item compounds the problem directly. Once a weapon is in hand, it stays there, and a boss-adjacent round can be lost purely because the wrong object could not be dropped. No disarm button exists anywhere in the control map, and the omission has real consequences rather than being a mere inconvenience. It is the single most puzzling design decision in the package.

Objective-item locations randomise between attempts with no hints of any kind, which turns late-game collection rounds into aimless room-to-room wandering. A spawn this unguided clashes with the otherwise tight structure, and hunting blind through familiar corridors saps the momentum the earlier levels build. The soundtrack, by contrast, deserves unreserved praise: cheerful, memorable, and consistently worth pausing to hear. Audio carries more charm than the visuals manage.

The final boss, Mechanica, is brutally fast and can land six consecutive hits before an escape becomes possible, a genuine wall by any measure. The counter-strategy, spawn-camping at the point where the mechanical mouse emerges, breaks the fight entirely once discovered, converting an apparent difficulty spike into a triviality. That abrupt swing from punishing to exploitable is characteristic of the era's licensed production values. The ending itself is exactly as abrupt, cutting straight to a bare credits sequence and back to the title screen.

Overall, this is a house-sized duel of traps and thrown objects that succeeds precisely where it innovates and stumbles where it relies on convention. The offensive hazard design and cheerful soundtrack carry fifteen short levels, while loose melee, a missing item-drop button and randomised objectives limit the polish. Judged as a short relic of the licensed era, it earns its recommendation with room to spare.
