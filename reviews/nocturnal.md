A soldier of the Order of the Enduring Flame returns home to find the Phoenix's fire failing, the world-devouring Mist creeping in and his order fled, on a mist-shrouded island rendered in gothic detail: white-haired corpses, flesh silhouettes in the fog and fire-lit temples. The worldbuilding arrives through scattered lore pages and prayer altars, covering a creation myth of the Phoenix and the Unseen, a martyrdom and soldier letters, though the fragments run only two or three sentences each. The central twist is signposted early from a mid-game letter, and the finale confirms it with the Mist puppeting a hooded figure with a personal connection. The ending is a quiet gut-punch built on bitter farewells. The atmosphere is the strongest aesthetic note.

Fire-as-everything is the game's cleverest idea: the torch-fed flame meter ignites the blade, burns the Mist away, doubles as the healing resource through a purchased fire-heal ability and serves as the puzzle key for fire-carrying switches. The design weaponises the resource economy in its best puzzles, forcing triage between healing and progression. The parry system handles arrows and daggers with timed attacks, and the slow-mo thrown dagger that returns like a boomerang is genuinely fun to use. A small nine-skill upgrade tree bought with crystals rounds out the progression, with checkpoint altars and light Metroidvania backtracking. The Hollow Knight adjacency is structural rather than derivative.

The Avatar State is the finale's gift: unlimited flame, air dash and full heals turn the final stretch into a god-mode victory lap, first romping through ordinary enemies and then burning down the final rival's Mist barrier. It is a rare piece of mechanical storytelling, with the fire the Phoenix gave finally standing between the player and the Mist at full strength. The power curve's endpoint is earned across the whole run. The resource economy simply evaporating for the finale is the design's best argument. It goes through the whole room, and it is badass.

Trial-and-error room design is the biggest gripe: unseen hazards kill before they teach, and progress frequently requires dying to learn what was coming. Shallow combat compounds it, with one three-hit string and no combo growth across the whole game. The mid-air attack quirk extends jumps into deaths, forcing the habit of landing before attacking. Torches shatter on one hit, making triage between healing and fire-wave attacks a losing proposition most of the time, and invincible-without-fire enemies punish resource mistakes. A lagging camera in dark rooms completes the friction. The ending's misread-as-rescue ambiguity is deliberate but easy to misjudge.

Missable scrolls and lore pages bothered little, though leaving an area too early soft-locked one puzzle secret, a cost the end-game counter makes visible. The three missed scrolls are acknowledged by the counter without shame. The run lasts roughly two hours and fifteen minutes, well under the advertised three, and the brevity cuts both ways: crisp and complete, but too short for the systems to develop. The combat's shallowness is the trade the brevity imposes. The atmosphere earns the sitting regardless.

The auto-climb is an early delight, and the epic title screen sets the mood the game sustains. Zones 6, 8 and 16 hold the missed scrolls, and the end-game counter names them without judgment. The eyeball hazards are the least fair of the die-to-learn placements, and the fire-carrying backtracking puzzles reward the resource economy's best ideas. The final fight's burn-down of the Mist barrier uses every mechanic the game has taught. The sitting is complete at the credits.

Overall, a tight, atmospheric 2D action-platformer whose fire-as-everything resource design, parry-and-dagger combat texture and god-mode Avatar State finale outshine its brevity and its die-to-learn rooms. Trial-and-error hazards, a single three-hit combo, the midair-attack quirk and one-hit torches are honest costs, and the clever single-resource economy is worth the sitting for anyone who likes their platformers moody and brief.
