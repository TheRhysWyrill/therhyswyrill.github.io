A cozy fantasy world of turtles, sloths and murkies, with the city of Constantia built around the doctrine of constancy, where crystal shortages, waking carcass-robots and a swarm of interconnected quests braid into one investigation. Terra Memoria wraps genuinely deep turn-based tactics in a village-sim wrapper, and no other RPG bakes turn-order manipulation into a cozy shell quite this thoroughly. The cast of quest-givers, the turtle-folk civilisation and the in-game poster cameo from the developer's previous title give the world personality without effort. Constancy as a doctrine, a choice renewed day after day rather than a fate, gives the themes surprising weight. The setup promises comfort and delivers systems.

Timeline combat is the engine of joy: damaging enemies or exploiting elemental weaknesses knocks them back along the turn order, and broken shields leave them weak to everything. Strategising to make an enemy fall behind never stops being satisfying, and the one-way shield system adds a defensive rhythm most turn-based games never attempt. Duo transformations compound the depth, with Edson converting single-target skills into multi-target and Opal shifting elements every turn, a pairing that can render enemy resistances irrelevant. Electrocharge becomes a signature ability once unlocked. The system rewards planning over grinding in a way few RPGs manage.

Progression borrows Final Fantasy 15's deferred XP: bank experience from every fight, then cash it in at camps, where the cooking minigame turns recipes into permanent HP. A full recipe book rewards completion, with 67 dishes available and delightful art on every card, and the stat payoff makes every meal genuinely valuable. Shrines (speos trials) unlock paired skills with puzzle conditions like sacrifice or repetition. The XP economy has quirks worth noting: level-30 well enemies pay a fraction of what lower-level golems gave, and carcasses pay triple, a curve that makes no sense at all and leaves the endgame overlevelled. Camps become the loop's heartbeat, and the leveling enthusiasm never fades.

Navigation is the persistent friction. The point-and-click world is dense with quest-givers but short on signposting, and the hunt for who to ask about carcasses ends at a library book nobody points toward, while Clonk's hammer is never found at all. Vague hyena quests ("consider them as such", "walk a mile in whose shoes") complete themselves without explanation, with no indication of why. Mossang's oversized hitbox makes platforming and puzzle navigation awkward, and no numerical health bars exist, only visual ones, forcing guesswork on kill thresholds. Meta's absurd health stat, ending at 666 HP while the others sit near 500, tips party balance without comment.

Stability is the most ironic cost. One mid-battle freeze, repeated hang-ups inside Edson's house near Facto City, and a crash right at the credits force a reload before the final fight just to see the ending. The climax arrives abruptly regardless, with the crystal thief's reveal followed almost immediately by confrontation, leaving the epilogue to carry the resolution. The carcasses' true nature, ancient digger-robots waking because magic cyclically returns, dissolves the "threat" once a fawn re-tunes them with song, a payoff the investigation earns. Jie's villainy is legible early to attentive players, and the thematic landing justifies the setup.

Beyond the RPG core, Begin is a full town-building sandbox layered onto the Redlands, with a fishing collectathon, pins and recipe hunting layered on top. The town-builder rewards noodling with layouts the way the combat rewards noodling with turn order, and both loops coexist without demanding each other. The endgame ramps enemy levels faster than the story beats, though an overlevelled party renders that moot. Old School Musical's DNA shows in the soundtrack and the art's playful rhythm. For a first hybrid of this many systems, the seams hold together remarkably well.

Overall, a delightful systems-forward RPG whose timeline combat, deferred-XP cooking loop and town-building sandbox justify the journey even as the vague quests, unfindable objectives and credits-adjacent crash test patience. The rough edges are navigation and stability, not design, and the constancy theme lands with real weight by the end.
