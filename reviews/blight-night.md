One night in autumn 1986, a small town rises from the dead, and the acts peel back a layered conspiracy: a drilling company strikes a mind-altering chemical, the military weaponises it, a pastor poisons his flock, and command finally orders the town erased from the air. The storytelling is done almost entirely through found notes, radio broadcasts and environmental detail, and the density of that writing is remarkable for any project, let alone a solo one. Dark comedy threads through the horror, from a breaker manual to a belt-buckle murder note, and the tone lands as a blend of zombie survival and 1980s small-town Americana pastiche.

The music and radio broadcast writing are the highlight. Recurring FM segments, records by a fictional country crooner and diegetic radio storytelling give the town a living voice, and the songs are genuinely catchy enough to chase down. The safes, dig spots, carnival token codes and a church raven puzzle form a cohesive optional-puzzle layer woven into the fiction, with a token-to-bar safe-code chain that earns genuine admiration for its construction. Gravestones, sermons and carnival rides all feed the mystery, and the act structure physically reopens the town area by area, with dig spots marked Silent Hill-style for a distinct investigative rhythm.

Combat feedback is satisfying throughout. Ragdoll physics on melee kills floor enemies with shear force that never stops looking great, and the endgame arsenal (a fully upgraded silenced Makarov and a three-star LMG) delivers a genuine power fantasy, turning the finale into a victory lap. Progression runs through skill points, equipment slots for hats, boots and belts, and a pawn shop loop selling junk to fund upgrades. The infected roster (walkers, burnts, spitters, ghouls, drifters) keeps encounters varied across the five-to-six-hour runtime.

The core loop gives the investigation its rhythm. Exploring Golden Springs, looting trunks and bodies, managing a small inventory and selling junk to the pawn shop fund the escalating arsenal, while six acts chain objectives (generator, spark plugs, sewers, water control, carnival pumps) with lamp-post saves and, from Act 4, fast travel. Dark areas demand light sources, and the isometric presentation with map-marking nods to the early survival-horror classics the game clearly studies.

Stamina is the single biggest mechanical complaint. It drains fast, recovers slowly, and the glacial walk speed compounds both, gating sprinting and melee in a way that grates from the first act to the credits. Inventory friction compounds it: a tiny slot count, no item dropping, and chests that refuse to register as looted unless fully emptied. The 1986 setting's internal logic also wobbles, with the town supposedly dead wholesale yet notes, graffiti and survivors abound, an inconsistency the narrative never quite explains.

Bugs and a soft lock are the real blemishes. Collecting the C4 in the military base during Act 3 before triggering the Act 4 carnival blockade bricks the run entirely, a preventable failure with obvious fixes, and one that ruined a prior attempt outright. Elsewhere, a camera lock in the sewers, a safe cursor stuck in normal gameplay, weapons refusing to fire, an ammo counter that fails to update and enemies visibly popping into existence round out a list the game should not have shipped with. Some puzzles are also under-clued, with poorly worded breaker instructions and a founder-statue order that stumped, and one motel safe went permanently unsolved.

Overall, this is an impressively crafted indie Action, RPG with real atmosphere, note-rich storytelling and satisfying combat, and the solo-developer pedigree makes the achievement more remarkable, not less. Punishing stamina, inventory friction and the preventable soft lock are genuine flaws that deserve a patch, and the internal-logic wobbles need attention in the planned continuation. The whole remains a thoroughly enjoyable conspiracy romp worth playing, with a finale that earns its power fantasy.
