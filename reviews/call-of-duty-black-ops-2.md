A dual-timeline cold-war thriller spans the 1980s CIA operations against a rising warlord and a 2025 cyber-war where drones and world grievances become weapons. The antagonist is the campaign's anchor: a tragic figure whose vendetta began with his sister's fate, always two steps ahead, always plausible. The mole mystery, the fake capture, and the Mason family arc all twist in ways a first-time player genuinely cannot predict, and the choices ripple into the ending in ways few campaigns of the era dared. This is the first entry in the series to build a genuinely branchable campaign, and the structure still feels fresh.

Branching paths and player choices make the campaign feel replayable in a way the series never managed before. Character deaths, war outcomes and the finale all depend on player decisions and Strike Force success, and the Yemen kill-the-target dilemma reads as suspiciously wrong in the moment, with the paranoia paying off narratively for anyone who trusts the instinct. The karma dilemma and the final kill-or-capture decision give the ending real consequence. Incentive to replay is not a marketing claim here; it is built into the structure itself.

Strike Force missions are an inventive addition despite their strangeness. RTS-lite squad defence with direct unit control and a helmet feed that works well, they break the corridor-shooter rhythm with genuine tactical texture. The tactical map divides opinion and is skippable for anyone who prefers direct control, and squad commands can be ignored entirely without penalty, but the missions succeed even on their own terms. The wingsuit descent, the flooded Lahore stealth, the horseback defence in Afghanistan and the VTOL chase over Los Angeles round out a varied set-piece roster.

The 2025 setting felt odd even at release. Tech predictions amuse more than impress thirteen years on, and the super-futuristic armoury sits awkwardly against the parts that did not come true, though the dated-future register has its own period charm. Gameplay remains standard series fare, unchanged since the beginning and carried by the campaign's ideas rather than its shooting, which is both the entry's limitation and its honesty. Drone enemies and bullet-spongy foes late in the game provide the only real difficulty spikes.

The story's emotional architecture deserves its own recognition. The antagonist's tragedy is rooted in a sibling's fate, and the writing takes that grief seriously enough that his escalation reads as comprehensible rather than cartoonish, an approach few blockbuster campaigns attempt. The 1980s timeline gives the CIA operations a grounded, era-flavoured register (Angola, Afghanistan, Panama) that contrasts cleanly with the drone-war future, and the cuts between the two eras give the narrative a rhythm the single-timeline campaigns lack. Hudson and Woods carry the older thread with worn conviction, and the family arc across both timelines closes with genuine weight.

Friction is minor but real. A recast voice actor for a returning character takes players out of the experience whenever he speaks, an odd misstep given the character's history. Vehicle handling is awkward throughout, with horse riding compared unfavourably to a different genre's vehicles entirely, and sprint stamina feels needlessly restrictive after the series removed it elsewhere. Grenade aiming arcs confuse despite the indicator, and the end-credits concert cameo is baffling but memorable, a footnote that sums up the campaign's willingness to try things.

Overall, this is the strongest campaign the series has produced for its choices alone, a dual-timeline thriller whose branching paths, tragic antagonist and consequence-driven structure give a linear shooter genuine replay value. The unchanged core shooting, the odd future-setting and the voice-recast misstep are acknowledged but forgiven, and the Strike Force experiments earn their place. Proof that branching paths can work in a campaign built for spectacle.
