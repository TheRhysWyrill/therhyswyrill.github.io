An SRO agent named Sheila chases a mysterious artefact across connected worlds: a research facility, an otherworldly forest, a floating island and a final volcanic confrontation, pursued throughout by a mercenary rival. The story exists purely to string set pieces together, ends unresolved, and never pretends otherwise. Atmosphere arrives entirely from the visuals: ray-traced fire, water reflections, lightning-drenched ruins and hanging corpses set a tone far above the narrative weight. A cheeky bonfire easter egg nods to Dark Souls, demons called Lu-Lu beasts swarm the forest, and a volcano-faced guardian closes the show.

The technical achievement is the headline. Ray-traced reflections, detailed environments and fluid combat all arrive from a single developer, and the impossibility of that sentence never quite wears off. Small tells betray the one-person studio: UI and XP counters reflecting in water surfaces, clumsy character animations, lip-sync a half-second behind every line. None of it matters against the fire and light, because the craft on display is undeniable even where the polish is not. On underpowered hardware the game needs 720p to hold steady, and a console port ironically outperforms the older PC setup.

The combat sandbox carries the whole experience. Sword swipes chain into EMP launches, grapple pulls drag enemies airborne, lightning strikes and a time freeze layer tactical options over FPS gunplay, and access to every power from the start reshapes how each encounter plays. The shotgun deserves special mention: it fires as fast as the trigger can be pulled, evoking the best rapid-pump weapons of the looter-shooter canon and rewarding aggression like nothing else in the arsenal. Skills purchased across runs persist, collectibles (a vase, a jade statue) and costumes pad replay, and a single rotating-symbol puzzle provides the briefest pause between firefights.

Two bosses structure the whole game. The Lord of the Forest opens the boss ledger with a proper arena and fair telegraphs, but the finale misjudges its own strengths: a cramped room, heavy incoming damage and enemies that reflect shots back at the shooter turn the power fantasy against itself. Even fully upgraded, the last fight grinds. The contrast between the fluid open arenas and this final box is the campaign's sharpest design mistake, and it lands one encounter away from the credits.

Stability is the other tax. A speedrun attempt collapses when a grapple into an ammo pickup sends the character plummeting through the world into an endless fall, fully soft-locked; the breakage is more spectator sport than tragedy, but a second playthrough dying to physics is a real cost. The runtime is the deeper issue: 26 to 35 minutes of content wearing a game's price tag, functionally a demo, complete with an unresolved cliffhanger and three playthroughs' worth of replay squeezed out of half an hour of material. Honest framing helps; the label on the store page does not.

Progression persists across runs, which is where the repeat value lives. Unlocked powers carry forward, skins reward completion (including a deliberately goofy school-uniform costume for speedrunning), and the SSS rank offers a genuine skill ceiling for anyone inclined to chase it. One death per playthrough is typical on a first pass, and the whole structure supports a 100% clearance in an afternoon. The loop is thin, but it is looped deliberately, and the density of the combat makes each lap shorter and stranger than the last.

Overall, this is a dazzling half-hour proof of concept from a one-person studio, a gun-sword-power hybrid that plays better than its scope has any right to allow, and an open tech demo priced as a complete game. The ray-traced presentation, the rapid-fire shotgun and the persistent power sandbox earn their repeat visits. Read it as the demo it is, and the craft justifies the price of admission.
