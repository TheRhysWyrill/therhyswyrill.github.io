A mining town twisted into a nightmare version of itself becomes the hunting ground for a girl searching for her missing cousin, and the horror that follows is built from childhood fears rather than adult nightmares. Bethwood's fog-lit streets, school corridors and cable-car heights hide patrols of creatures that read as inner demons given shape: the eyeball imagery that symbolises being watched, the mannequins that channel a fear of dolls, and the spotlight-horned boss that ties humiliation in front of a crowd to concrete threat. The tone stays eerie rather than frightening, closer to torch-lit unease in the Silent Hill: Shattered Memories mould than to gore. diary entries scattered through the world carry the town's backstory, each one the last thoughts of an inhabitant whose life interlocks with the red quartz mined beneath the streets. The premise takes a familiar light-based horror template and rebuilds it around a child's perspective, and that reframing is what carries the whole game.

The flashlight is the entire verb set. Shining its beam on certain creatures renders them vulnerable, turning standard encounters into light-management puzzles, and a charged blast can kill outright when the battery holds. Batteries are the resource that keeps the light alive, scavenged from drawers and vending machines, and the tension of a dying beam mid-patrol gives even simple crossings weight. Stealth is the smarter default: crouching below sightlines, reading patrol routes and using the map to plan routes matters far more than fighting. Monsters track by sight and by sound, so sprinting past a patrol is as dangerous as walking in front of one, and the detection loop rewards patience over aggression throughout.

The school is where the design settles into its rhythm. Corridors loop back on themselves, vents and unlocked windows offer alternate routes, and each classroom hides either a collectible or a creature worth avoiding. The detailed map earns its praise here: every room, vent and shortcut is drawn out, and route planning becomes part of the puzzle rather than a menu chore. Backtracking is freely permitted across the whole map, so missed collectibles never lock behind progress, and earlier areas re-open with new tools in hand. Revisiting the school grounds with the freeze ability changes which paths are viable, giving the re-openable structure real mechanical purpose rather than mere convenience.

The mannequins are the horror's sharpest instrument. Harmless ones line corridors as set dressing, but the enemy versions chase with rotating heads, speak in distorted taunts and refuse to stay down, and they are genuinely unnerving in a way the standard roster never reaches. The fish-head shamblers and the crow-like flyers fill out patrol duty, while the Invisibles, tracked only by their footprints, force a different kind of caution entirely. Four distinct types is a thin roster for a full campaign, and the repetition shows by the final act, but each type is at least mechanically distinct rather than a palette swap. The spotlight boss stands apart, a fleshy searchlight that must be dodged between cover rather than fought head-on.

Environmental puzzles bridge the stealth. Boxes are shoved to block sightlines, water and steam are rerouted through valve puzzles, electrified puddles are frozen with the fire-extinguisher to create safe crossings, and thrown soda cans bait patrols away from doors. The distraction tools are the friction point: cans thrown toward a creature sometimes draw only a step or two of interest before it loses interest entirely, making some routes harder than the design seems to intend. The quirk never blocks progress outright, but it undermines trust in one of the game's core verbs. Battery management and the flash-attack upgrade compensate, giving at least two reliable answers where the cans waver.

The ticket fragments deserve their own mention. Scattered among the diaries and inhabitants, they accumulate across the whole game, and the number gathered changes what the finale offers, giving the collectible hunt a narrative payoff most horror games never attempt. It turns completionism from a checklist into a story variable, and the incentive to sweep every area is real rather than nominal. Brightness defaults dark enough that the first act plays half-blind until corrected, a small but persistent flaw. A scream the character lets out on taking damage sits oddly against the otherwise restrained sound design, loud and strange where everything around it stays quiet.

Overall, this is a child's-eye horror built on flashlight stealth, patient route-planning and a collectible hunt that genuinely matters, roughened by a thin enemy roster and inconsistent distraction tools but carried by strong atmosphere and thoughtful structure. The re-openable map, the sound-and-sight detection loop and the ticket-driven finale give the short campaign more depth than its size suggests. A rescued curio that earns its second life.
