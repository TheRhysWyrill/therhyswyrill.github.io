GYLT delivers a spectacularly atmospheric survival horror experience, driving its emotional campaign forward with a very cool and interesting visual style. This striking aesthetic feels like a brilliant mashup of Alan Wake and Silent Hill: Shattered Memories but cleverly frames its dark world through the eyes of a younger protagonist. The narrative carries excellent thematic depth, masterfully connecting the grotesque monsters to real-world experiences of bullying through hidden diary collectibles while treating you to multiple ending paths, including a highly satisfying secret conclusion where both characters escape together.

The core gameplay loop thrives on rewarding stealth utility and creative puzzle mechanics, keeping the pacing consistently fresh as you navigate a detailed and well-connected map layout. Exploring these eerie corridors relies on crouching, sneaking, and throwing soda cans to distract patrolling threats, all backed by an excellent tracking counter that makes hunting for area collectibles incredibly convenient. This journey gains fantastic tactical depth from your dual-purpose tools, letting you move ladders to reach hidden zones, freeze environmental hazards with a fire extinguisher, or focus your flashlight beam to melt barriers and burn away monsters in satisfying combat encounters.

However, this stylish nightmare still faces a few notable hurdles stemming from aggressive sprinting penalties and terrible stamina limitations. The single-player experience suffers from a severe movement restriction where sprinting makes an absolute insane amount of noise, instantly alerting every hostile creature in the vicinity. This survival friction is compounded by a sluggish stamina bar that drains far too rapidly during intense chases, transforming what should be a swift escape into an incredibly frustrating trial and error exercise.

The user experience introduces additional headaches through unreliable enemy distraction logic and abrupt, jarring audio design choices. You will likely encounter major irritation when dealing with specialized Crow enemies, as they stubbornly lose interest in your thrown distractions almost instantly and ruin your careful stealth positioning. This progression is further disrupted by ridiculous, out-of-nowhere volume spikes from specific eyeball noises alongside a reliance on cheap mannequin jump scares that can feel more annoying than genuinely frightening.

The tactical exploration can occasionally experience minor friction due to vague interactive cues and unpolished puzzle physics. You might notice moments of deep confusion when interactive objects completely lack clear visual markers, easily leaving you stumped on incredibly simple progression points. Finally, your mechanical engagement faces minor irritation when the game arbitrarily strips away your flashlight or extinguisher for narrative reasons, leaving you entirely defenseless alongside janky environmental loops that can occasionally trap you in hazardous damage cycles.

Overall GYLT is a remarkably atmospheric, visually charming, and mechanically creative horror adventure that excels through its meaningful anti-bullying narrative, satisfying flashlight combat mechanics, and detailed map progression tracking. While the dark journey is occasionally dragged down by punishing sprinting noises, erratic enemy AI reactions, jarring audio spikes, and unpolished interaction prompts, the outstanding artistic direction and clever puzzle variety ensure it stands as a highly entertaining and triumphant thriller.
