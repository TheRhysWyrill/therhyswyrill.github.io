A post-apocalyptic Earth emptied of humanity by an adorable army of corrupted toys sets the stage for a cat on a hoverboard with an armless AI companion, hunting undead unicorns and mutated generals, and the premise is played completely straight as a joke. The writing is gleefully crude, mean-spirited in the best way, with running gags about managers, personal confessions and a talking goldfish convinced he is human. Few games commit this hard to a toy-dystopia with this much gore attached. The tone oscillates between bewildering and hilarious, sometimes in the same scene. It oozes style from the first frame and never stops.

The hoverboard is simultaneously mount, sword and fuel gauge, and the entire design flows from that single inspired conceit. Grinding on rails refuels, boost carries combat and traversal alike, and lock-on, executions, grenade shots and shield-and-slam add-ons layer onto the base loop. Score-chasing combat in the character-action mould rewards aggression, with credits looted from ATMs and spent at an in-fiction toy store. Cosmetic cat customisation and a profanity filter upgrade round out the economy's absurdity. Movement itself becomes the fun, which is the loop's greatest achievement.

Levels are genuinely varied: a radioactive factory with a slime chase, an arcade-machine inner world running pinball, light-gun and platform-homage challenges, a flooded museum, a dollhouse castle, a prison island and a surreal final mind-level. A mid-game run of bosses keeps the variety coming, from the manager duo through the goldfish collector, the doll queen and a mutated-bear mind-boss. Enemy design stays fresh where repetition was expected, shifting archetypes continuously. The set-piece escalation rarely repeats itself. Difficulty options arrive with pun names, and the standard tier is well-judged.

The soundtrack is the game's most unexpected delight, pumping and varied from mid-game to the closing credits, punching far above any reasonable expectation for a game of this size. Ranged-enemy spam interrupts combos at the worst moments, and over-boosting causes overshoots on jumps so reliably it becomes a self-inflicted comedy loop. Key-piece collectibles unlock challenge levels but are under-explained, with the post-game hunt easy to skip without regret. Visual strain during executions while a boss laser fills the screen is a recurring annoyance. An early fatal-error crash never recurred, and performance otherwise impresses on modest hardware.

The professor reveal and the serum backstory behind the toy uprising land with more emotional weight than the crude surface promises, and the family-reunion resolution arrives warmly after the surreal memory vignettes of the mind-level. Bewilderment is a recurring state rather than a failure, and the writing seems to intend it. Roughly five and a half hours cover the story comfortably in one sitting, with a post-game stinger teasing more. The tone's commitment earns its strangeness. Whatever confusion remains is the price of the ambition.

The arcade inner world deserves singling out as the campaign's creative peak, folding pinball, light-gun homage and platforming challenges into one toy-box level that justifies the whole concept on its own. ATM-looted credits feeding an in-fiction toy store keeps the economy inside the joke, and the pun-named difficulty tiers extend the writing's commitment into the menu itself. The mutated generals and the manager duo give the boss roster a corporate-satire streak that matches the dystopia. The doll queen and the prison island close the variety loop with escalating confidence.

Overall, this is a stylish, gleefully crude character-action romp whose mount-sword-fuel hoverboard, banger soundtrack and genuinely varied levels outweigh ranged spam, overshoot friction and bewilderment. The execution is loose in places but the attitude never wavers, and the surprise soundtrack alone justifies the visit. A short, wild ride that earns its recommendation on style and momentum alone.
