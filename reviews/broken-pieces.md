A woman trapped alone in the Breton fishing village of St. Exel since an unnamed catastrophe pieces together what happened through her missing partner's journals, recovered tapes and a dead spy's dossiers. The investigation layer is the game's standout: a government cell in the lighthouse tracking a submarine, a cult shipping coffins of sacrificial villagers out to sea, and a state plan to quarantine the entire village, all converging on a finale that recontextualises the opening nightmare. The mystery is dense, legible and genuinely absorbing, told collectible-first with the confidence of a studio that understands environmental storytelling. It is the strongest part of the game by a clear margin.

The season-switching mechanic is the cleverest system here. A magic pebble used at fountains toggles the village between summer and a frozen winter, opening new paths and collapsing marked walls to form stairways and shortcuts. Weather as a puzzle key gives exploration a second dimension, and routes that dead-end in one season open entirely in the other. It is the kind of single idea that carries a whole adventure, and the game builds its geography around it deliberately.

Atmosphere sustains the mystery between revelations. The eerie abandoned village streets, the sea columns on the horizon, surreal memory dreams and the cult's staging inside the church all land, with lit windows in an empty village generating dread that combat never manages. The supernatural is filtered through mundane Breton textures, circuit breakers, fishing lockers, moonstone furniture, and that domestic grounding makes the folk-horror elements hit harder. The village itself is the best character in the cast.

Combat is functional but rough. A pistol-only loadout with a twirl-dodge system falls apart against yellow attacks, which are effectively undodgeable, and the red-attack twirl was literally the trigger for the game's worst technical failures. Enemies absorb damage without much feedback, and encounters devolve into dodge-and-shoot cycles that the design never varies. The game's weakest link, confirmed repeatedly, and the section where all patience drains.

Unreal Engine crashes compound the combat trouble. Three occurred across a full run, at least two triggered by dodging in combat, and the engine's reputation takes the blame rather than any single bug. A crashed attempt ended one session outright before a restart. Stability is the difference between a flawed gem and a frustrating one, and this sits uncomfortably close to the line.

Puzzle guidance wobbles. Several sliding-block puzzle tables and an axe-hidden-in-snow sequence leave the player thoroughly lost, and the crafting system, in which objects left in a chest are consumed nightly to auto-craft high-quality ammunition, is never adequately explained until discovered by accident. The time-pressure day/night loop, deadly after eight in the evening, is padded by long traversal times, repetitive backtracking and no sprint indoors. A permanent-sprint soft-bug late in the run, amusingly, improved the experience.

The final act sequences its puzzles well: four commemorative plates on the church cross, an organ melody, decrypted submarine tapes on a projector, and the closing signal from the lighthouse. A surprisingly hard train-switching arcade minigame and a full-heal fridge pickup round out the systems, both fun and both underexplained. The endgame rides the mystery's momentum to a satisfying close, jank notwithstanding.

Overall, this is a hauntingly atmospheric folk-mystery whose investigation loop, season-switching geography and eerie village staging outshine its janky combat, undodgeable yellow attacks and crash-prone engine. The time-loop twist rewards every journal and tape collected along the way, and the rough edges are survivable for a story this well told. Worth enduring the friction for the mystery.
