Pathogen X sends agent Aiden into a black-site military base overrun by a virus that makes its victims hyperaggressive and mutates them, a rabies-like plague rather than the familiar zombie shamble. The retro-style survival horror framing is deliberate, and the document-driven worldbuilding sells it: Dr. Stein's journals mugged through for dark comedy carry the "it's for science, what could possibly go wrong" register the genre thrives on. Voice acting, strong music and competent visuals all arrive from a genuinely one-person production, which remains the headline. The game punches well above its budget at nearly every turn, and the soundtrack is the loudest reason.

The music repeatedly outperforms expectations. It elevates every encounter, from ambient dread in the corridors to the boss themes, and it carries the experience through stretches where the enemy roster cannot. The soundtrack is the single element praised most consistently, and it earns that billing: where the bestiary runs thin, the audio fills the gap so completely that the shortfall becomes easy to forgive. Few solo projects invest this heavily in composition.

Weapon progression drips steadily from pistol to MP5, shotgun, sniper and assault rifle, each arriving with its own personality and its own problems. The shotgun hits hard but sprays inaccurately, the sniper feels awkward to aim until no-scoping reveals its real strength, and the MP5 emerges as the clear winner against the boss. Keycard gating and a light exploration layer structure the base, and the generous supply loop, with ammo and health handed out constantly, even from bosses who apparently never learn, keeps pressure off the resource management. The economy is almost comically kind.

Loud, slow shamblers make up most of the roster, telegraphing themselves audibly before appearing, which reads as both comfort and structural weakness. Thin enemy variety is the game's biggest criticism, and the sameness of the opposition dulls encounters the music works hard to elevate. The Wraith is the lone standout threat, and the game reserves it for the worst-designed section: a bafflingly harsh timed fetch quest where the creature's damage output combines with unclear objectives to produce the campaign's only real anger. The ten-minute evacuation hold that follows, by contrast, proves far shorter in practice than its countdown advertises.

Controller support is competent but incomplete. Gamepad sensitivity cannot be adjusted, and reloading by holding a face button feels awkward in extended fights. The missing sprint compounds the stiffness, an odd omission in a game built around corridor movement. None of these break the experience, but they are felt most during the timed section, where precision matters most and the controls cooperate least.

The ending earns its charge. The scripted final beat lands with impact, though the death screen that flashes rather than a fade undercuts the moment and drops the pulse unnecessarily. The same one actor voicing both the agent and a dying soldier produces accidental comedy throughout, conversations that sound like a man talking to himself, and the game is better for the charm even where the doubling shows. The finale's infection-themed turn lands as written; the presentation stumble is the only flaw in the execution.

Overall, Pathogen X is a surprisingly accomplished solo-dev horror whose soundtrack makes up for its thin bestiary and whose presentation consistently exceeds its budget. The timed Wraith chase is a genuine misstep and the controls have rough edges, yet the whole remains more than the sum of its polish. For a one-person show, it is very impressive.
