A zombie outbreak on the resort island of Banoi follows four immune survivors through resort, city, jungle and prison, scavenging, escorting and fighting as they go. Dead Island established a niche in 2011 that nothing else occupied: first-person melee looting with dismemberment physics on a tropical resort, and the blend still reads as singular. The tone is pulpy rather than scary, and the B-movie framing (Sam B's opening rap included) commits to what it is. The story tracks an outbreak, a mysterious radio voice and a corporate conspiracy, with the DLC's prison-lab arc supplying the narrative's real teeth. What keeps the hundred-plus hours of content alive is the combat loop underneath.

Melee combat and ragdoll physics are endlessly satisfying, and the fun lives in the drop-kicks, dismemberment and slow-motion kills. Enemies ragdoll across pools and balconies with a physical weight few zombie games manage, and a well-timed kick into furniture remains one of the genre's great simple pleasures. The loop of loot, craft, upgrade and repeat never sours across the full campaign and DLC, propelled by weapon mods (shock, Molotov, paralyzing) that genuinely change encounters. Durability and repair costs add decision weight without becoming punishing. Mid-fight glee at an enemy with more health than expected is the loop working exactly as designed.

Looting and the economy are addictive, with constant rifling through bags and houses amassing wallets and crafting materials at a rate that turns the protagonist very rich, very quickly. Repeatable quests (alcohol, bleach, champagne) pad the loop with low-stakes fetch runs that fund the upgrade spiral. Weapon crafting occupies a large share of the decision-making, and the temptation to hoard rather than upgrade is real, since something better always turns up soon after investing. Rarity tiers and skill trees give four playable characters distinct builds, with one survivor's regeneration perk carrying solo runs. Vehicles and fast travel arrive late enough to feel earned.

The twist ending genuinely surprises, with the reveal that the radio voice guiding the survivors was the hacker Karen rather than Ryder landing hard enough to prompt a full re-evaluation of the story, and the "vaccine" being the virus itself closing the conspiracy neatly. Ryder White's DLC reframes the whole antagonist question in a way few expansions attempt. The recontextualisation elevates a script that would otherwise be pure B-movie. It is the rare zombie-game ending that rewards attention. The prison-lab conspiracy braid holds together better than the campaign's chapter structure suggests.

The friction is consistent and mostly structural. Every escort mission across resort, jungle and prison is slow and frustrating, with protected characters who simply refuse to run. Enemy respawn frequency in dense areas punishes: infinite spawning is fine, but the quarantine zone and prison swarms replace foes faster than they can be cleared. Occasional AI and scripting bugs confuse thugs (some simply will not attack), doors, and quest logic, with a "skipped an event" flag firing incorrectly. Heavy sprint breathing grates from the first hour. A fog-of-war map frustrates completionists who want every corner uncovered, and lemon juice is bizarrely common loot.

Guns are expensive to upgrade and rarely worth using against zombies, doing less damage than a good katana ever will, and the firearms only earn their keep when the DLC forces the issue. The 2011-era quest design shows its age in waypoint placement and objective clarity. Co-op-optional design means solo players can complete everything, though the swarm sections test any solo build's patience. The resort-to-prison chapter arc keeps environments varied even when the objective structure repeats. What the game lacks in polish it returns in physicality, and the physicality is the product.

Overall, a janky but deeply fun first-person zombie looter whose drop-kick ragdoll combat, addictive looting economy and genuinely surprising DLC twist still hold up years later. Slow escorts, punishing respawn frequency and 2011-era quest jank are real costs, but the core melee loop delivers exactly the itch-scratch the genre promises.
