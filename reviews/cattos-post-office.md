A single sunny day in a tiny cat-run town frames the whole game: a postal cat picks up packages at the post office, carries them across town, and unknowingly walks through a community secretly planning his birthday party. The atmosphere is aggressively adorable, with National Belly Rub Day on the tavern calendar, a Windows-XP-hills screensaver gag on a monitor, a cat DJ behind decks, and a scratching-post gift from a British department store. Gentle jokes land constantly, including a sign that does not quite say chicken, and the whole package wears its inspired-by-cats origins openly in the credits concept art, alongside one poster gag aimed at cult gaming oddities.

Physics toys are the real game. Cans can be kicked toward bins, footballs score into goals, pots knock over, trolleys wrestle, and ducks quack into conversion, complete with tiny postman uniforms. Kicking cans and messing around between deliveries sustains more delight than the checklist itself, and the toybox interactivity gives the town a tactile quality most walking-simulator-adjacent releases never attempt, with a protagonist who performs little jumping jacks at every opportunity. Every system exists for silliness rather than progression, and the design is honest about it.

The delivery loop is deliberately simple. Seven packages, a handful of favours, saving bakery bread, retrieving the florist's scissors, locating a lost package, fetching a television remote and delivering a coin to a fountain, chain together with no fail state, no combat and no inventory management. A night-time telescope event and the party close the day, and the linear structure never pretends to be anything more than a gentle checklist between toys. Side quests never materialise, and the design makes no apology for their absence, which is the correct call for a game this size.

The birthday-surprise arc delivers real emotional payoff. The town feigns forgetting, the tension builds, and the party arrives with personalised gifts, a mural painted in a chosen blue that turns out to be a portrait, and a song. The writing flirts with the cute-game-goes-dark trope just enough to raise suspicion before staying resolutely wholesome, and the relief of that refusal is part of the charm. A meow-karaoke finale and a pier send-off close the day on a quiet, contented beat.

Jank sits at the edges, though none of it obstructs. Bins impose arbitrary can limits, kicked cans respawn to clutter the streets again, and the trolleys are so bouncy they seem made of rubber, occasionally trapping the wrestler inside. A layered-audio quirk lets faint strains of other music leak through when the camera angles just so, the telescope vista disappears the moment the day is ready to end, cutting short the view being admired, and the day-night shift also arrives late with little fanfare to mark the transition. None of these quirks break the day; they simply add texture to a package already comfortable with its own rough edges.

The night shift itself deserves note. The telescope event under the stars gives the town a second visual register, and the contrast between the bright afternoon rounds and the quiet evening makes the incoming surprise feel closer. The pacing holds across the roughly ninety minutes because each delivery zone introduces a new toy to fiddle with, so the checklist never outstays its welcome. A gentle day-night rhythm, simple deliveries and constant tactile distraction add up to a structure that knows exactly how small it should be.

Overall, this is a lovely, funny, very short slice of cat-town life whose tactile toybox and wholesome surprise arc make the roughly ninety-minute runtime feel like a palette cleanser rather than a slight. Respawn clutter, rubbery physics and a thin delivery loop are honest limitations, but as a bite-sized hour of unreserved charm the game does exactly what it sets out to do.
