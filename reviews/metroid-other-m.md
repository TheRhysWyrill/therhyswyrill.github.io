Set on the Bottle Ship in the aftermath of a previous mission, Metroid: Other M hands its bounty hunter a federation squad, a hunt for a saboteur and a bioweapons conspiracy, and dresses the whole thing in cinematic Team Ninja presentation. The style is undeniable, with direction that earns credit even where the writing collapses, and the combat is fun, fast and genuinely stylish, the single element praised from the opening hour to the credits. This is a solid action game wearing a Metroid skin, and the skin fits better the less the characters are allowed to speak. The notorious monologues, including the infamous baby discourse, drag every cutscene they touch.

The combat rewards attention throughout. Sense-move dodges, lethal overblast finishers and a concentration system that refills health and missiles mid-fight make the encounters feel expressive, and the difficulty stays moderate, with boss fights offering little wiggle room for the concentration-healing window but never turning cruel. Weapons arrive in the series-typical drip, from missiles and bombs early through Speed Booster, Plasma and Wave Beams, Seeker Missiles, Grapple, Space Jump, Screw Attack, Gravity Suit and finally Power Bombs, and the first reaction to every new tool is that ten more minutes with it earlier would have helped. Item hunting proves fair by series standards, and the map's item markers genuinely aid the hundred-percent chase.

The level design works against the series' DNA. One-way systems close corridors behind progression, locking backtracking out until arbitrary gates open, and the authorisation structure, where a commander must permit each weapon's use, compounds the linearity with narrative-mechanical friction. The map confuses more than it guides outside its item markers, and the residential and bridge sections drag badly under a slow indoor walk speed that no upgrade ever fixes. None of this breaks the loop, but it all pulls against what the genre promises.

The script is the campaign's real problem. Names like the deleter and MB land like a child invented them, the mid-game exposition dump runs marathon length, and a mid-arc beat where the commanding officer shoots the protagonist makes no sense however charitably it is read. The Ian flashback lands as genuine tragedy, and the self-sacrifice ending redeems the arc emotionally, proof that the plotting could work when it stopped explaining itself. Enemy sound design adds accidental comedy, with certain creatures squawking like seagulls in attack and death, and the atmosphere stays serviceable without ever becoming memorable.

A fan de-monologue patch exists that removes most of the worst monologues, and its effect on the experience is hard to overstate, turning constant eye-rolls into occasional ones and letting the stylish action breathe. The third-person missile aiming breaks the game when enabled and is best switched off, while free concentration healing smooths the difficulty in a way the original design arguably needed. Those are the two adjustments that transform the package, and neither requires much effort to apply.

Roughly nine to ten hours cover a hundred-percent completion, ending with a Zero Suit escape sequence and a high-rate ending, and the difficulty never becomes the sticking point that some series entries manage. The cinematic flair, the expressive combat and the fair item placement make the case for the game, and the script makes the case against, with the balance tipping toward the former the moment the monologues are trimmed.

Overall, Metroid: Other M is a stylish, expressive action game whose Team Ninja combat and fair completionist structure earn a recommendation despite one-way corridors, authorisation gates, absurd naming and monologues that drag every scene they touch. As a piece of the series it frustrates; as an action game with the script trimmed to a whisper, it sings.
