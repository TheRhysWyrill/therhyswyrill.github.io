A dystopian visual novel cast as a series of divination sessions: an unseen divinator, a pair of robotic hands, answers clients' questions by drawing runes. Clients include a detective haunted by his partner's disassembly, a hitman planning his hundredth kill, a roboticist with his daughter in stasis, and a disillusioned robot questioning whether life has meaning. News broadcasts between sessions chart a city sliding into riots, suicides and robot disappearances. The tone is bleak, philosophical and deliberately uncomfortable, with an explicit suicide and self-harm content warning at the start. The world's collapse happens entirely through those interstitial bulletins, and few games attempt a complete narrative arc in under an hour with this much thematic weight.

The bleak, fully realised dystopian worldbuilding carries the entire experience: an AI whose suicide triggered a suicide wave, robot rights, riots and corruption. The rune-drawing mechanic is genuinely engaging, with arranging stones in different orders to answer each client's question producing results that feel meaningful to the story. One robot client's recitation of a famous Yeats poem ties the whole narrative together when the AI's remnant surfaces, and the payoff is deliberate and clever. The hitman session's writing has real teeth, and the robot's existence-and-consent monologue lands hard. The quiet, eerie atmosphere is a feature rather than a flaw.

The divination choices matter mainly at the sessions themselves, with each client's answer changing their story thread and ultimately which of two endings is reached. The mass-robot-suicide ending is genuinely sharp, and the true-chaos implication gives the finale real weight. Sessions can be replayed via a flowchart with skip, and the alternate answers are reachable in a single extra run. The structure is tight and the flowchart respects the replay's time. The two endings diverge cleanly rather than muddily.

The true and loop ending structure is interesting in theory, but the alternate playthrough changes only two or three lines of dialogue per session, which makes the second run feel thin. At roughly forty minutes total, the game is far shorter than expected, enjoyable but barely there. Despite the content warning, self-harm as a distinct theme is barely explored beyond suicide itself, a gap between promise and delivery. Puzzles are minimal, and the game is about reading, choosing and absorbing the world rather than playing it. None of this undoes the writing, but all of it caps the value.

The five-conversation structure is the game's boldness: complete strangers asking about their futures, with the world's fate decided in the margins. The detective's grief, the hitman's hundredth kill and the roboticist's stasis daughter each give a different register of loss, and the robotic hands framing keeps the divinator unknowable. The art direction is spare in a way that serves the bleakness. The pacing is relentless in the best sense. The thematic weight outlasts the runtime by a wide margin.

The flowchart replay respects the second run's time even where the branching disappoints, and the spare art direction keeps the focus on text. The tower-storm reading that predicts a client's repeat crime is the mechanic's best moment, proof that rune order can carry genuine narrative consequence. The divinations' answers feed the ending choice cleanly, and the skip function makes the alternate route a ten-minute detour. The suicide contagion theme is handled with the discomfort it demands. The package is small but complete.

Overall, a bleak, under-an-hour sci-fi visual novel that punches well above its runtime on writing and atmosphere, with the dystopian worldbuilding, rune-based divination framing and the AI-remnant payoff as the achievements. Thin branching in the second playthrough, minimal puzzles and a barely-explored self-harm theme beyond suicide are honest costs, and the brevity caps the value without capping the impact.
