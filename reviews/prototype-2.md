A grieving sergeant loses his family to the second outbreak, gets deliberately infected by the first game's protagonist, and spends the campaign eating his way up a paramilitary chain of command to reach him. The consume-and-disguise fantasy returns intact: identities and memories are stolen from consumed soldiers, tanks and helicopters are commandeered mid-fight, and escalation runs from expendable soldier to one-man apocalypse. Sin City-style cutscenes with red and blue highlights give the revenge arc a striking visual identity, and Blackwatch's casual cruelty toward civilians is more evident than in the original, which sharpens the motivation considerably.

Movement is the heart of the game. Gliding and air dashes arrive from the start, and progression layers triple air dashes, enormous jump height and chain-jump training on top, with jumps holdable to keep momentum flowing. By the endgame the traversal is so complete that rooftops stop being obstacles and start being routes. The tendrils earn their billing as the signature weapon, and the late-acquired whip fist is outright broken in the best sense, powerful enough to marvel at every time it lands.

The consume-and-disguise loop infiltrates bases without firing a shot. Consuming a soldier grants his identity, letting the protagonist walk through checkpoints, and the "being watched" suspicion system punishes sloppy stealth with escalating alarms that frustrate often enough to matter. Blacknet missions and mutation collectible sets feed upgrades, and 100% completion is achievable in roughly ten and a half hours, about half the length of a full first-game playthrough, a condensation that surprises more than it offends. Endgame power creeps to near-invincibility, which suits the fantasy even if it drains late-combat tension, and the upgrade points themselves buy into only five tracked categories at a time, a noticeable narrowing from the first game's sprawling web.

The finale stages a back-to-back boss gauntlet, Evolved duo, Goliath, a Juggernaut barricade and the protagonist's creator himself, and forces genuine weapon variety across the sequence. Each fight resists the one-weapon default, and the gauntlet structure keeps escalation feeling earned rather than routine. It is the campaign's most engaged stretch, and the fights reward switching between tendrils, whip fist, claws and hammerfists rather than settling on a favourite.

The Radnet events are the weakest content in the package. Repetitive, bugged and mercifully skippable, they reduce optional challenges to a bowling mini-game and similar filler, and the gliding handling inside them actively feels worse than the main game's. Side content overall is thinner than the first game's density of events and Web of Intrigue depth, and the upgrade system simplifies to five choices per point, a streamlining that trades build variety for accessibility. Sprint handling occasionally fights the player, with turning losing precision at speed.

The story supports the power fantasy where it counts. Black box audio logs detailing the pre-outbreak collapse are the collectibles worth chasing, and Manhattan-as-quarantine-zone worldbuilding treats its civilian cruelty as fact rather than backdrop. The revenge arc lands its beats, and the villainous-but-sympathetic antagonist structure gives the campaign a spine the systems alone would lack. None of the streamlining undermines that spine, even if it leaves the whole package feeling like a tighter, shallower take on its predecessor.

Overall, this is an enjoyable but condensed sequel whose tendrils, broken-good whip fist and escalating movement outweigh bugged Radnet filler, a simplified upgrade tree and side content that cannot match the first game's breadth. A good sequel, a lesser game, and still well worth playing for the traversal alone.
