Arthurian legend retold from the dragons' side of the shield: a pun-laden kingdom called Camelhot, a squire dragon who must win a tournament, a princess promised into an arranged marriage, and a pair of scheming knights with the combined intellect of a wet towel. The Terry Gilliam-designed art gives every scene a storybook grotesquerie that suits the material, and the writing carries a Monty Python-adjacent accent from the first line of dialogue to the last. Nearly every object, character and location exists to land a joke first and function as a puzzle piece second.

The joke density is relentless and, crucially, designed to reward attention. Recurring gags compound across the whole campaign: a chancellor's beard that grows scene by scene, a knight whose cowardice precedes him, an evil laugh he can never finish. Satirical jabs at unions, paperwork, therapy and royalty sit alongside gags aimed squarely at the point-and-click conventions the game belongs to, and the humour never once apologises for being very stupid in the best possible way.

The voice acting is disproportionately excellent for 1996, performed with an energy that borders on mania and blessed with recognisable turns, including one performance of beyond-insane intensity and a voice sharp-eared listeners will place immediately. Dialogue-hoovering is a reward in itself here, which matters because the game openly mocks its own genre: unsolvable problems are waved through with "magical properties" hand-waves, a looping shortcut road lampoons backtracking, and the credits close on a bitter voice actor's meta-rant about the whole business.

The L1/R1 context-sensitive verb switching (look, talk, speak, take) is the one ergonomic stumble. Fumble-prone on a gamepad, it invites speaking to objects meant for taking, and repeated misclicks never quite stop happening. A subtitle display that initially reads as cut-off text turns out to be an X-to-advance onboarding wrinkle, and saving works the old-fashioned way, taking an eon that is annoying but ignorable. None of it ruins the experience; all of it is honest friction.

Progression is chapterised around the tournament deadline, and the puzzles are mostly logical once the gag logic clicks. The inventory amasses glorious absurdity (manure, prunes, a dried sword, popcorn canisters, stilts) to be applied across Camelhot, Castle Grimm, the mines and the Cave of Dilemma, with mine dust for the laundry-beast test as the one easy item to forget. Minigames pepper the run: a catapult range, a dance-off, thumb wrestling, and the Cave of Dilemma's four trials, from shearing a spinning porcupine and lifting a land mass to a rabbit hat trick and conquering a fear of laundry beasts.

The finale belongs to the schemers, whose stolen throne is undone by their own Black Dragon 2, a piece of self-inflicted comeuppance the tone has earned a dozen times over by then. Set pieces escalate accordingly, one hypnosis scene in particular arriving at a volume that demands the neighbours be warned. Throughout, the cumulative design holds: jokes planted early pay off late, and attention is repaid generously.

Overall, a genuinely hilarious, lovingly voiced parody adventure that holds up almost entirely on the strength of its script, with fiddly gamepad verb switching and archaic saving as the only real costs. The pun-laden Camelhot setting, the compounding running gags and a credits sequence that mocks its own genre make this one of the funniest things its era produced, and it has lost none of the bite.
