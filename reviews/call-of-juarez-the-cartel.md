An inter-agency task force of DEA, FBI and LAPD officers hunts the Mendoza cartel after a bombing in Los Angeles, with corruption seeping through every level of law enforcement, and the murder-mystery texture of suspecting which teammate is the mole gives the campaign a personal hook. This is the series' black sheep: modern-day cops instead of outlaws, no duels, and a threadbare western identity carried only by one character's surname, a lineage reveal and a cameo of the Juárez gold. The tonal 180 from the predecessors is the defining fact of the game, and everything else follows from deciding whether that swap interests or offends.

The gunplay is genuinely solid, which rescues the package from its reputation. Weapon handling and sound land with the competence of a good 2011 shooter, revolvers, the Model 29, an Uzi, an S&W 5906, shotguns and assault rifles unlocking by level rather than chapter, and the shooting is mindless in the best sense. Weapons feel somewhat interchangeable, and enemies go down far more easily than the tanky foes of the earlier games, which saps tension from firefights that should build it. The feel is right; the stakes are not.

Native controller support on PC was an unexpected rarity for 2011, and it works properly, a small but genuine point in the port's favour. The mission roster spans burning marijuana fields, a strip-club manhunt, a dockside trafficking raid, armoured-van chases, a rooftop stakeout and a final three-way confrontation, but the structure is repetitive: mostly linear shootouts, driving chases and one club infiltration, with weapons feeling interchangeable across the roster. Pacing stays steady but samey, with several driving-heavy missions padding the middle.

The secret-agenda system is the one mechanic no other entry in the series attempts. Teammates quietly pocket cash and phones behind each other's backs, a co-op betrayal layer with real potential, but it is poorly explained and largely pointless in practice: the special-item hunts give no indication how the money is meant to be found, and the mechanic reads as a half-formed idea rather than a system. Concentration mode returns, though the catchphrases attached to it repeat endlessly and grate by the midpoint.

Technical friction accumulates. One crash mid-freeway-chase, controller-input drops and repeated snagging on level geometry mar the experience, and the WMV cutscenes require a repair job before they play properly on modern setups. Excessive profanity in the dialogue becomes a running joke, approaching House of the Dead territory without quite reaching it. None of these are fatal, but together they date the production well beyond its visual polish.

The finale's moral choice between revenge and redemption branches the epilogue per character, and the three outcomes reward post-completion research, with the redemption path's ending for the veteran detective the best of the set. The mole plot resolves in a way that rewards the suspicion the campaign cultivated, and the grim tone holds even as the plot leans melodramatic. It is more narrative ambition than the game's reputation suggests, executed adequately rather than well.

Judged as a generic modern cover shooter, it is decent and occasionally entertaining; judged as the successor to a western series, it is barely recognisable. Both judgements are true simultaneously, and the game's legacy is the sum of that contradiction. The best defence is that the shooting satisfies and the runtime does not overstay, and the worst is that nothing else about it justifies the name on the box.

Overall, this is a competent, mindless modern shooter whose solid gunplay, native controller support and mole-hunting plot make it a decent game on its own terms, despite repetitive missions, paper-thin enemies, technical friction and a western identity reduced to a surname. The weakest entry in its series and simultaneously better than its reputation, which is as neat a summary of its contradictions as any.
