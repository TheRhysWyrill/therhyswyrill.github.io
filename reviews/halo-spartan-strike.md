A top-down twin-stick take on Halo's sandbox, framed as a combat simulation covering two operations: a classified recreation of the New Mombasa Conduit recovery in 2552 and a Gamma Halo and New Phoenix campaign in 2557. The briefing tone is functional military sci-fi, serviceable connective tissue rather than a selling point. Roughly double the length of its predecessor, it delivers more of the same in the best sense: short missions, tighter aiming feedback that now shows where the cursor is pointed, and an expanded vehicle roster. It is an unambitious sequel that perfects a formula without transcending it. The simulation frame also gives the structure a neat justification: each operation is presented as a training exercise, which lets the campaign hop between eras and locations without connective explanation, and the classified-mission wraparound gives the whole structure a tidy endpoint.

The vehicle sandbox is the heart of the game. Drivable Warthogs allow shooting from the driver's seat, with seat-switching for gunners, and the roster extends through Ghosts, Scorpions, Hornets and even elephant escorts. Being able to fire while controlling the vehicle directly transforms these sections from chore to highlight, and the driving missions rank as the campaign's most memorable content. New Mombasa's city combat with Warthog convoys benefits enormously from the freedom.

Turret-defence segments are the second peak. Anchored to a stationary gun and given waves to melt, the combat loop hits a satisfying rhythm few other sections match; the sheer rate at which enemies evaporate makes these missions the campaign's most consistently enjoyable standoffs. Skulls and score boosters add a replay layer, with Famine and Shield/Armor Deplete available as modifiers, though neither meaningfully boosts the score. Assault ops and medals award per-mission credits, giving the endgame a replay-for-gold structure.

Promethean enemies freshen the roster considerably. Halo 4-era Prometheans and their weapons arrive mid-game and are a welcome change from the Covenant-only focus of the first game, giving encounters a distinct texture in the campaign's back half. Their weapons feel notably different in hand, and fighting them provides the midpoint with its own identity separate from the opening operations. Skirmishes against them across Gamma Halo's jungle and Forerunner structures hold up well, and the enemy variety extends further through waves of Covenant holdouts, animated constructs and shielded elites that keep encounters from blurring together.

The finale is where the goodwill evaporates. New Phoenix's defence mission ends on an endless-swarm difficulty spike paired with a timed Hornet extraction, the only mission that earns genuine resentment. Worse, the Hornet itself can be destroyed by stray fire and fail the mission outright, forcing a cheap-feeling restart. The campaign also peters out rather than ending, mirroring its predecessor's structure and concluding with little ceremony around the classified missions.

Technical presentation is mixed. On SteamOS the game boots at a very low default resolution with blurry UI, 1024x768-era defaults that demand manual correction before the campaign even begins. In-engine visuals are otherwise clean for the format, and the top-down perspective suits the twin-stick combat. Stability is not a concern; the friction lives in defaults and difficulty balance rather than performance. The low resolution also matters less in motion than on the title screen, since the campaign's small-scale firefights read clearly even at modest resolutions once the setting is corrected.

Overall, this is a solid, focused follow-up whose working vehicle sandbox, Promethean mid-game and satisfying turret defences outweigh a weak finale, an unceremonious ending and a low-resolution boot default. As an expansion of the formula rather than a leap, it holds up well.
