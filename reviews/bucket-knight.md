A knight with a bucket on his head hunts the ultimate treasure to buy a house, and the wordless slapstick is told with a straight face throughout. The trophy's king's bounty converts to gold, the home is purchased, and the epilogue reveals a family of bucket-headed children, which is the true ending's joke and it lands. The presentation is deliberately retro, framed like an old CRT television, with chiptune-flavoured music that is simple but effective. NES-style enemy rosters cycle from goblins through skeletons, slimes and bats. The premise is pure silly charm, and the game never pretends otherwise.

The weapon progression is the satisfying core: a revolver, shotgun, AK and finally a minigun that is effectively the last weapon but twice as deadly. Money persists only per-life and buys upgrades at steep prices, with death meaning a level restart. Levels run one to five minutes across roughly twenty-five stages in a few worlds, each with collectible coins and gems, cherries and apples for healing, and keys opening gates. Enemies mostly shoot only forward, which keeps the run-and-gun readable. Saw blades and spike pits insta-kill while cheese and contact damage cost hearts. Eighty minutes covers the whole campaign, and collectibles can be fully swept in the same sitting.

The deadly cheese is the running gag and the nemesis: environmentally placed cheese blocks that damage or kill on touch, forcing genuine respect for what looks like decoration. Shotgun goblins are brutal before upgrades, dealing a heart per contact while taking four-plus hits from early weapons. The power balance flips late once the minigun arrives, and the late-game power fantasy is genuinely satisfying. Difficulty spikes early and eases as the arsenal grows. The design's spite is front-loaded rather than evenly spread.

Jank is the honest cost. Jump inputs sometimes fail to register, which is scary in a precision platformer, and enemies shoot through spikes and platforms while the player cannot. The enemy-count HUD stats are plainly wrong, a coding issue that makes the level-end tallies untrustworthy. Enemies render behind candle flames in a layering oversight, and treasure scattered hard to see compounds the visual clutter. The missing final boss means the last levels are standard gauntlets plus a chase, an anticlimax after the minigun high. None of it is fatal, and the brevity keeps the friction from wearing thin.

The short runtime is the point rather than a flaw, and the game sits comfortably alongside the thirty-minute classics of an earlier era. The one-sitting structure suits a palate-cleanser between big releases, and the premise's silliness is the identity rather than a deficiency. The CRT framing and chiptune score do real work in selling the retro register. The budget pricing matches the scope honestly. The epilogue's bucketed babies are the right final joke.

The unselectable world screen is an early oddity, and the design's spite is concentrated in the first third before the arsenal catches up. Cherries and apples heal at exactly the rate the late levels demand, and the per-life money economy makes every death a genuine cost without ever feeling punitive. The enemy rosters escalate cleanly from goblins to bats, and the forward-only shooting keeps encounters readable. The CRT framing earns its nostalgia honestly. The one-sitting structure is the design's quiet argument for itself.

Overall, a cheap, cheerful one-hour NES-style blast that knows exactly what it is, with a satisfying weapon-progression loop from peashooter revolver to screen-clearing minigun wrapped in charming retro presentation. Dropped jump inputs, enemies that shoot through terrain, wrong level stats and the deadly cheese gauntlets are honest costs, and the silly bucket-headed premise is worth the price of admission on its own.
