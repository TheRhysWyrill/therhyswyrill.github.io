A corporate dystopia where syndicates have replaced nations sounds like a strategy game's premise, yet this 2012 reimagining of a classic series rebuilt that world as a first-person shooter, and the transplant mostly takes. Chip implants define the setting and the combat alike, with data logs, radio broadcasts and propaganda documents building a satire that runs from obituaries to employee-termination reports. Playing as an agent of an amoral corporation is a deliberate moral provocation, and the writing leans into it rather than smoothing it over. The violence is strikingly graphic, enemies routinely coming apart in pieces, and the game offers no comfortable framing for it. Starbreeze's pedigree shows in the gunplay, which carries a weight few contemporaries matched.

The signature system is the DART chip, and it still feels remarkable a decade later. Breach applications let an agent remote-detonate an enemy's implant to force suicide, persuade them to fight as a temporary ally, or backfire their weapons against their own squad. The tactical overlay goes further, highlighting enemies through multiple walls and letting certain weapons fire through cover entirely, because an ocular implant renders concealment meaningless. Chaining persuades and backfires across a firefight turns aggressive cover shooting into a puzzle of priorities. Few shooters before or since have offered anything comparable.

Chip overloads drive the loop, but the upgrade economy undercuts them. The chip tree spreads points across ammo capacity, shields, regeneration and breach boosts, yet only backfire scales meaningfully; suicide and persuade stay static regardless of investment. The result is a build that converges on a single dominant application rather than exploring the full toolkit, a strange imbalance for a system this inventive. Co-op cross-agent applications exist for persuading and forcing suicides on fellow agents, extending the gimmick into multiplayer for those who seek it out.

Kilo's synthetic-memory origin gives the protagonist's amoral service a wedge of ambiguity, and Lily's defection threads a personal undercurrent through the corporate scheming. Neither thread is dwelt upon at length, but both land, and they sharpen the question the writing keeps pressing: whether an agent who overloads strangers' chips for a syndicate can be counted among the good guys. The story never answers, which is the point, and the ambiguity gives the graphic violence its sting.

The rocket-boots swarm-missile boss encounter marks the campaign's lowest point, an opaque fight with no obvious counter-play and repeated deaths before it yields. Boss quality swings hard in both directions: some encounters confuse, while the finale's direct solution is oddly simple once spotted. Level lengths swing too, with some missions ending almost before they begin. Environmental storytelling remains the reliable constant throughout, the corporate documentation darkly comic enough to reward stopping and reading in a game that otherwise rarely slows down. The lottery-winner segment deserves particular mention, a pitch-black satirical vignette that says more about the world than any cutscene.

The twins' immune-state revival in the finale is the sharpest mechanical frustration, with each re-entering an invulnerable state while Merit revives them until the link between the two is worked out. Grenade controls never become intuitive, the usual button mapped elsewhere and the correct input resisting discovery across several missions. Heavy bloom and post-processing wash out early scenes, though the underlying art direction holds up surprisingly well for its age. Upgrades that ignore two of the three breach applications compound the sense of unpolished edges around an excellent core.

Overall, this is a short, stylish and morally grim shooter whose chip-hacking combat survives the years better than almost anything else about it. The breach applications and wall-penetrating overlay remain genuinely distinctive, the satire lands, and the flaws sit mostly at the margins of an excellent core loop. As a reimagining of a strategy classic, it earns its place.
