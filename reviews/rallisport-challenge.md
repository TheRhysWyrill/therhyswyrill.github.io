The discipline mix is the whole appeal: stage rallies, rallycross, hill climbs and ice racing split the campaign so no stretch of the game ever repeats itself, mirroring the structure that made the Dirt series beloved. Safari, Mediterranean, alpine and ice events rotate across a points-based career ladder running from basic through pro, expert and classic tiers to unlimited. The variety kept a single session fresh across nearly five hours. Pure rally would have worn thin; the circuit-style interludes never let it.

Each discipline demands its own technique, and the game teaches them without a single tutorial screen. Ice racing rewards slow, steady throttle discipline over aggression, hill climbs ask for a co-driver's trust in a road too narrow to see, and rallycross punishes the rally habit of leaving space for oncoming traffic. Weight transfer, handbrake use and the difference between gravel and tarmac braking all surface naturally through repetition. The handling model sits comfortably between sim and arcade, demanding commitment without punishing curiosity. Learning four disciplines in one campaign is a generous structure that modern rally games still borrow.

Visually the game is exceptional for 2002, with car models and environments that genuinely do not look like their release year. Period-correct sponsor brands line the trackside, the Michelin man makes a cameo, and the era-appropriate sponsor-heavy dressing doubles as a time capsule. The absurd spoiler-laden hill climb machines, effectively ground-based aircraft, give the whole thing a period charm nothing modern replicates. Fidelity this good is the reason the game is still worth visiting. It is one hell of a visual showcase for its era.

The late-game difficulty spike in the unlimited tier is brutal: every stage begins seconds behind with no realistic way to close the gap, turning confident wins into damage-limitation finishes. The Mediterranean mud-and-rain stages and the stiff-handling Citroën humble earlier expectations, and ice racing rewards slow, steady throttle discipline over aggression. Damage percentages never meaningfully punish, which softens the spike's edges but also removes any stakes. The spike does not block progression, which keeps it an irritation rather than a wall.

Camera options are poor across the board, with blind crests hiding the road ahead and an awkward fisheye view that distorts more than it informs. Crests in particular make blind jumps genuinely unnerving when the landing cannot be seen. Pre-configured cars with minimal tuning strip the garage depth, leaving almost no alteration between events. Roughly twenty tracks form the entire roster, limited but forgivable for the era, and the bonus events unlocked at one hundred thousand points simply recycle existing layouts with nothing new. A minimal musical score rounds out the spartan presentation, one song repeated more than it should be.

Getting an early-2000s PC port running on modern systems is its own difficulty tier, and setup trouble preceded any racing at all in this case. The sequel's console-only fate remains a strange footnote, with the first game on PC and the follow-up stranded on Xbox. Playing on the easiest tier purely for the restart-any-race option is a learned caution from earlier rewind disasters. Career progression flows through unlocking cars and events, and the ladder structure holds up as a template. The old-school design has aged better than its interface.

Overall, the discipline variety and genuinely exceptional visuals make this a charming, great-looking slice of Xbox-era rally well worth a visit, despite the unlimited-tier spike, poor cameras and a small recycled track pool. The finale's harder events spike harder than they should, but nothing blocks the fun of the first four tiers. It is not a bad little game, and the period charm carries it home.
