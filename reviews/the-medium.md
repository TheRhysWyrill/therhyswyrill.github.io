Kraków, 1999: a funeral-home-raised medium follows a mysterious caller to the abandoned Niwa workers' resort to investigate a recurring dream, and The Medium builds its psychological thriller from communist-era Polish history. Party dignitaries, a massacre and government experiments on a gifted child thread through the resort's decay, with a Nazi-era bunker repurposed as a nuclear shelter anchoring the past to the horror. The register is melancholy thriller rather than horror, with a grand total of one jump scare, and the story lands better than the studio's reputation suggests. The family mystery at the core keeps its grip from the first dream to the final reveal. As a slow-burn descent into historical grief, the writing delivers.

The dual-reality presentation impresses from the first cutscene, with two worlds rendered simultaneously side by side rather than toggled. The transitions between realities are genuinely spectacular, and the photoreal character models and environment detail reward stopping mid-game just to marvel. Technically it is a statement of intent, a genuine simultaneous render that no toggle-based predecessor attempted. Whether the statement needed making is another question, and the game itself never answers it. The spectacle is real even where the necessity is not.

The split-reality gimmick is ultimately a flex more than a mechanic. No moment requires both realities side by side, and rendering two environments at once introduces the game's worst technical artifact: strobe-light visual glitches that flay the eyes during dual-reality sections. The mechanic impresses the eyes without ever earning its keep mechanically, a showcase in search of a puzzle that demands it. Cutting to single-reality rendering would likely have eliminated the strobing entirely. Ambition and execution diverge exactly where the hardware bends.

Puzzle design runs a grab-bag of quality. The toy-mirror placement teleport is genuinely clever, with mirror position dictating arrival point in the spirit world, and the film-developing sequence and water-plant valve maze work well. The chase-escape logic baffles, and the running joke of everything missing a handle wears thin fast. Insight highlights interactables, spiritual energy powers the supernatural tools, and skin walls yield to the right charges. Chase sequences bookend the resort, with the pursuing monster delivering tension the puzzle stretches sometimes lack.

The Henry mind-dive is the campaign's highlight, a playable flashback delving into a security officer's memories to break him from the inside. The security service archives and the meat-scandal files give the sequence a documentary texture the resort's hauntings cannot match, and playing as the second character offers a perspective shift the structure needed. The archives' bureaucratic dread lands harder than any monster. More of this and less of the strobing would have balanced the middle act. The mind-dive alone justifies the runtime's slower stretches.

Movement is deliberately sluggish, with a run button that behaves more like a fast walk, an irritation compounded by collectible hunting across seven hours of playtime. Postcards, notes and echoes fill the environments, and a full echo sweep adds hours to the runtime. The pacing holds despite the restriction, and the atmosphere rewards the deliberate tempo. The ending's structural symmetry, the game beginning as it ends, lands with genuine force. The studios' previous titles built to this, and it shows in every frame.

Overall, The Medium is a photoreal psychological thriller whose Polish-historical mystery, mind-dive flashback and dual-reality spectacle deliver an intriguing seven hours, held back by a showcase mechanic that never earns its keep, strobe-light glitches and a restricted run button. Thriller rather than horror is the correct frame, and the writing rewards the slower pace. Worth playing for the story and the spectacle, strobing aside.
