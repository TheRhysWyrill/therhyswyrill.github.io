A hand-illustrated haunted manor in a memorable pink-and-purple pixel palette, sold as horror and delivered as a puzzle box. The Gothic-comedic register announces itself early, from an occult cult pamphlet promising mystic rites followed by coffee and cake to a demonic duck waiting in the endgame. A dead child's spirit serves as a friendly-but-sinister guide who teleports around the grounds, and the toy-soul mythology gives the manor a tone sitting between Coraline and a classic gothic escape room. Atmosphere carries the fright quota more than frights do.

Puzzle variety is the design's engine, with dozens of distinct, hand-crafted brainteasers: matching symbol plates, cooking breakfast for a ghost, a connect-four game played with wine bottles, transcribing sheet music, colour-mixing potions, sliding-block puzzles, ocultist pyramids and a three-riddle test. Each is self-contained, each teaches its own logic, and none relies on the same trick twice. The kitchen order puzzle and the sheet-music transcription stand out for how cleanly their clues integrate into the manor's rooms. It is a dense chain with very little filler between links.

Progression is gated by heart, diamond and skull keys alongside teddy-bear rescues scattered through the manor's large hand-drawn layout (forest clearing, entrance hall, dining room, dungeon, sewers, satellite room, garden and catacombs). The gating gives exploration a clear shape even when individual puzzles are obtuse, and item collection follows classic point-and-click rules of odd objects with hidden purposes. Room-to-room flow stays tight, and the sewers and satellite room add spatial variety the opening floors only hint at. The layout rewards systematic coverage rather than backtracking chaos.

Death arrives cheaply, instantly and with almost no consequence: restarts are immediate, items respawn at checkpoints, and no progress is ever lost. Frequent instant-death traps (toxic goo among them) draw more amusement than dread, and the minimal penalty drains tension from sequences that should hold it. Deaths function as puzzle feedback rather than punishment, which suits the tone even as it undercuts the horror framing. The scariest thing about dying here is how little it matters.

Jump scares are present but rare, and the soundtrack telegraphs every one by going conspicuously quiet. The stall-and-sting pattern becomes readable within the first hour, and the scares land only a handful of times across the whole campaign. What remains instead is dread by atmosphere: dark whimsy, shadowed corridors and a soundtrack that knows when to withhold. Horror branding oversells the game; the puzzle craft underneath it undersells itself.

The story unfolds through notes and letters, with a Paris Orchestra rejection letter addressed to a dead woman standing out as a standout gag, and closes on a garden grave monologue explaining a death, a father's occult sacrifice attempt and a soul scattered into toys. Multiple endings split between freeing the spirit, leaving the malevolent force intact, and confronting it, and the true route sneaks in through unsignposted catacombs after the apparent conclusion. That second route is a pleasant surprise that extends the playtime beyond first expectations. The writing keeps its dark comedy consistent through to the end.

Overall, the puzzle variety, the hand-drawn presentation and the toy-soul mythology make this a stylish, funny, puzzle-rich hidden gem whose jump-scare branding oversells its horror and undersells its cleverness. Consequence-free death and telegraphed scares drain genuine tension, and the true ending's lack of signposting will pass some players by. Approached as a dark-comedy escape room rather than a horror game, it earns its recommendation completely.
