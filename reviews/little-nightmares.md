Aboard the Maw, an undersea vessel of grotesque, gluttonous adults hunting small children, the entire game plays from the perspective of someone roughly a foot tall. Little Nightmares commits to scale as its whole identity: everything is enormous, and both the comedy and the horror flow from being tiny in a hostile adult world. The storytelling is environmental and implicit, wordless throughout, with the hunger arc and the ship's dark hierarchy reconstructed rather than explained. Chanting in the soundtrack puts the nerves on edge, and the Lady's haunting melody lands as the score's peak. Grotesque childhood-nightmare imagery blended with purely physical puzzle-platforming is a combination nothing else quite attempts.

The enemy and world design consistently delights and horrifies in equal measure: the Janitor with arms too long, the twin Chefs in uncanny fat suits and masks, and the Lady herself, each design engineered to be weird, uncanny and unforgettable. The guest hall is absolutely disgusting in the best way, a banquet of gluttony rendered with visible relish. A Looney Tunes piano drop shares space with genuinely disturbing imagery, and the tonal blend works because the horror never blinks. Few creature rosters this size leave such a distinct impression. The designs alone justify the visit.

The DLC deepens the dread across three chapters, following the Runaway Kid's parallel route through the same ship from a different child's perspective, a structural trick few games attempt. A flooded, leech-infested depths chapter and a dark residence of shadow-children expand the Maw's geography while the main campaign never revisits them. The gnome-cooperation puzzles in the coal room are the package's peak, recruiting a little troop of gnomes to haul coal and pull switches with real charm. Piecing together the Runaway Kid's fate across the DLC lands as the narrative payoff. The gimmick of replaying the same vessel rewards players who know its layout.

The loop is stealth, chase escapes and physical puzzle-solving: key hunts, sausage-rope rigging, the grand piano trap, gnome-operated coal furnaces, and the mirror-throw fight against the Lady. Deaths are frequent but checkpoints are close, and dying in every possible way still leaves the game not especially difficult. Collectibles are easy to miss blind but mostly cosmetic. The roughly two-hour main game expands to four and a half with all three DLC chapters, a complete single-evening package. The pacing never sags across the full run.

Depth perception on the balance-beam and pipe-crossing sections is the most repeated complaint, with no second-chance grab once a slip begins, and it is genuinely hard to tell where the character is standing. The controls compound the problem: trigger-to-grab is iffy, climbing is awkward, and unexplained mechanics like lamps that look like checkpoints but are not cost real progress early. The game demands a maximised brightness setting and is punishingly dark in stretches even then. Long end credits with an unintuitive hold-to-skip close the package awkwardly, a small design miss in an otherwise polished presentation.

Two hard crashes during the DLC marred the technical picture, though the Series X instability rather than the game itself drew the blame, and one apparent loading hang after a death added to the suspicion. The hide-and-seek stealth sequences carry genuine tension, especially against the Janitor's roving hands. Sound design earns its praise throughout, from the chanting to the wet acoustics of the depths. Forceful hugs for gnome friends are the comedy's high point. Everything technical is excusable; everything tactile is not, and the grab inputs sit in the latter camp.

Overall, a grotesque, scale-obsessed horror puzzle-platformer whose creature design, gnome-cooperation DLC puzzles and wordless dread justify the full package despite depth-perception platforming, iffy grab controls and punishing darkness. The same-ship DLC structure and the fate its parallel route implies make the whole greater than its parts, and the experience leaves the player eager for the sequel.
