The year is 1793, and the son of a missing occultist arrives at a private island where the era's most powerful men have gathered for a summit, and what begins as a search for a mother becomes something considerably stranger. Washington, Napoleon, a cardinal and a Prussian minister all take seats at the table, and the historical casting gives the conspiracy an immediacy that fictional names would never carry. The murder of a houseguest supplies the initial hook, though the mystery soon grows well beyond a simple whodunit into a centuries-old tale of stolen identities and abducted children. Deductions accumulate through property deeds, family trees and Masonic dating, and the evidence trail rewards close attention.

The conversation system is the design's real innovation. Every character carries defined vulnerabilities and immunities that must be learned through observation and reading, and exploiting a weakness or steering clear of a hardened defence becomes the game's combat in all but name. Confrontations play out as multi-step scenes where each line spent is a resource, and the effort-point economy gives even casual exchanges tactical weight. Learning what breaks a cardinal's composure or what a president will not tolerate is genuinely satisfying, and the system sustains interest across every episode.

Progression reinforces that talking is the whole game. Manuscripts found and read confer permanent skill points at each quest's close, class choice (diplomat, occultist, detective) shapes available talents, and consumables and collectibles layer further options onto encounters. Late-game demonic talents, including possession and mind reading, expand the toolkit in ways that recontextualise earlier conversations, and forging a possessed letter is among the more inventive sequence climaxes the format offers. The Medusa statues, the Masonic-date lock and the crucifixion-theme door give the investigation physical puzzles to punctuate the dialogue.

Visual feedback is where the polish thins. Interactable objects signal through blue dots that blend into the scenery, collectible coins are near-invisible without pixel-peeping, and one floorboard puzzle obscured enough to force a reload. The camera compounds the problem by repeatedly framing ceilings instead of whatever matters, and the manor's geography fights navigation at every turn, its corridors long enough that any return trip drags. None of these flaws break the experience, but all of them tax the patience during a genre that lives on observation.

Failure states lack teeth. Losing a hand to a crypt trap is a dramatic moment that carries no mechanical consequence whatsoever, and the surrounding cast never so much as mentions the injury, which undermines the stakes the narrative works to build. The same weightlessness affects the romance thread, which initiates itself without the player's apparent input and then swerves into an incest reveal that arrives without earned context. Choices matter elsewhere, so the inconsistency is conspicuous.

The plot's divergence from its advertised shape is worth naming. Anyone arriving expecting a grounded murder mystery finds an occult conspiracy instead, and the tonal pivot is genuinely surprising, delivered with confidence even if it abandons the deductive framework the opening suggests. The epilogue assigns fates to each historical guest with playful attention to history, and the demonstration of the body-hopping family's scale across centuries is well seeded through the documents scattered earlier. The ambition outstrips the premise by design.

Overall, this is a dialogue-driven occult conspiracy that far exceeds its murder-mystery premise, carried by a genuinely clever vulnerability system, manuscript-based progression and historical casting that keeps every conversation charged. Hard-to-spot interactables, a ceiling-obsessed camera, consequence-free failure states and a self-initiating romance all blunt the experience. The systems underneath are clever enough to forgive the rough legibility.
