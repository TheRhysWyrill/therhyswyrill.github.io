A chosen onion sent by the self-absorbed King Brock to stop King Sugar Daddy and find the runaway Princess Cauliflower sounds like a joke, and Rhythm Sprout commits to that joke with total confidence. The talking-animal banter carries a Banjo-Kazooie-esque irreverence through full cutscene dialogue, a villain backstory monologue, mock-documentary interludes and level-specific gags, and the tone swings from silly to gloriously unhinged. A Count who wants only to be best friends and watch Gilmore Girls, a Lord of the Rings fan-club bouncer, the Candy Lane incident mockumentary and a K-pop princess finale keep the comedy surprising from start to finish. The writing's self-awareness is the reason the smile never drops.

The soundtrack is uniformly excellent, without a single weak track across the whole campaign, and the song quality never drops even as the genres rotate. Boss vocals recall the best rhythmic villainy of the genre, and combat sequences weave mid-song without interrupting the track, a design elegance worth singling out because the music simply continues where it left off. Three-lane highways colour-code the inputs, with d-pad yellows, face-button pinks and bumper blues, and the dodge notes keep the hands moving without overwhelming the reading.

Forgiving fail-forward design is the mechanical standout. Missing a note carries no negative beyond resetting the combo, so struggling players keep progressing without punishment, and the design respects enjoyment over score-chasing. Star ratings gate progression and unlock X modes, skins, amusing cameo levels and bonus tracks, and the chili-rating difficulty meter telegraphs every level's challenge from one to ten. The structure invites a full clear in roughly three hours, with all thirty levels plus prequel and bonus content falling within a single comfortable sitting.

Difficulty spikes late and hard. The chilies climb to nine and ten, and the Power Peppers finale, stacked with yellow-note passages, drives even experienced rhythm players down to beginner mode for the first time in the campaign, dying repeatedly on the same passage. Trap notes throw timing off more than any other pattern, tightly clustered notes become genuinely unreadable as the speeds rise, and repetitive patterns melt concentration. The prequel Chili Inc levels and bonus tracks push absurdity and difficulty together, drawing exclamations even while every one of them gets cleared.

Small quality-of-life gaps dot the edges. A fixed control scheme cannot be remapped, a friction felt most in the trap-note timing, and a late trapdoor gag catches the same fall twice. Five-minute boss attempts stretch patience on retries. None of these dents the core loop, and the game's self-aware humour extends even to its own shortcuts, with a phone-call framing and hint scams that stay funny on repetition.

The three-hour runtime suits the material, a comedy blast that knows exactly how long its jokes should run, and mastery of every chart would take far longer for score-chasers inclined to pursue it. Every level cleared leaves the impression of a game that likes its own jokes, and that confidence is contagious. Between songs, the story does real work, with the mean king's one genuine moment calling the hero mean, a jerk and a nerd landing as the writing's best beat.

Overall, Rhythm Sprout marries a killer soundtrack to joke-dense storytelling in a rhythm adventure that respects enjoyment over perfection, with fail-forward design and chili-rated difficulty telegraphing doing the heavy lifting. Trap notes, clustered charts and the Power Peppers finale's yellow-note gauntlet sting, but the song quality and comedy carry the whole campaign with room to spare.
