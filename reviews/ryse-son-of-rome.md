A revenge tragedy dressed in Roman armour opens with a legionnaire rising through the ranks as his family is destroyed, narrated by a ghostly centurion recounting his own vengeance from beyond the grave. The framing structure remains the campaign's most distinctive storytelling choice, lending the grim tone a mythic register that the wicker-man sacrifice and gods-as-puppets ending sustain. Nero's rotten Rome comes alive through collectible scrolls and satirical graffiti, and the Empire's brutal march through Britain carries a moral discomfort the writing never pretends away. The tone wavers between grisly and daft, with a wine-statue gag providing brief levity. It is essentially a tech demo with a story, and the story is better than that description suggests.

The execution system is the game's signature pleasure, timing-based finishers that restore health and reward committing to the brutality. Limb-severing kills are the reward loop's entire currency, and few games have ever committed this hard to gore as gratification. The core loop of strike, shield-bash and dodge is deliberately basic, admitting its own shallowness without apology. Combat stays honest about what it is: a delivery mechanism for spectacular violence. Judged as depth, it fails; judged as spectacle, it rarely misses.

Squad-command set-pieces break the melee with genuine variety: archer volleys, scorpio aim, shield-wall advances and siege defence all punctuate the campaign's rhythm. The shield-wall march remains the visual highlight, a formation spectacle that looks as impressive now as it did at launch. The coliseum and the Damocles reveal elevate the final act into the campaign's best stretch, brief but entirely worth the wait. The wicker-man level's oppressive atmosphere is another standout. Scripted spectacle, not mechanical depth, is where the design invests everything.

The visuals have aged remarkably well for 2013, with forest infiltration and marble ruins alike still turning heads more than a decade on. Grainy low-resolution cutscenes sit awkwardly against the in-game fidelity, an inconsistency the engine's showcase ambitions make ironic. The camera drifts on its own and the wall-defence shake disorients at exactly the wrong moments. Combat prompts read fuzzily, and attacks arrive with no visual tell at all, so ambush deaths feel arbitrary rather than earned. The Praetorians' massed shields late-game drag the flow without adding challenge.

Progression is absurdly generous, with XP so inflated that a character threatens to max out before the second level, almost certainly inflated by PC-version DLC bonuses flooding coins and execution unlocks from the start. Collectibles exist but are clumsily signposted, and chronicle comics unlock only on stage completion, a backwards choice that hides its own content. The cage-adjacent soft-lock remembered from the Xbox version never recurred on PC. Roughly four hours cover the campaign, tight enough that the combat's limitations never fully curdle. The pacing stays brisk throughout.

Boudica's rebellion provides the campaign's moral crux, with legions cutting down Britons whose cause the framing story quietly understands, and the discomfort lands as deliberate rather than accidental. Vistas reward exploration with panoramic engine showcases, and the graffiti gag about imperial punishment shows a satirical edge the marketing never promised. The wicker-man sequence remains the atmosphere's peak, oppressive and memorable in equal measure. Even the daft moments feel authored rather than accidental.

Overall, this is a flawed but gorgeous launch-era showcase whose execution system, scripted set-pieces and still-striking visuals outweigh basic combat, absent telegraphs and a bloated XP economy. The Damocles framing and coliseum finale earn genuine affection, and the whole has aged far better than anyone expected. Judge it as cinematic spectacle rather than mechanical depth and it delivers exactly what it was built to show off.
