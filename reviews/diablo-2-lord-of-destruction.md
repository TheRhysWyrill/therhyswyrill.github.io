A dark, gothic descent across five acts, from the corrupted Rogue Monastery through deserts, jungles and hell to the summit of Mount Arreat, Diablo II remains the template for the loot-driven ARPG two decades on. Pre-rendered cinematics carry unusually heavy weight for their era, the atmosphere sets its tone from the opening frames and never relents, and the expansion adds a fittingly bleak final chapter. The world rewards players who know where to look: returning figures from the first game, a familiar zombified blacksmith in Tristram, and a voice-acting cameo from the Legacy of Kain alumni all wait to be spotted. It is a game built on secrets, and it plays that card deliberately.

Skeleton armies define the Necromancer's early game. A mini-army of resurrected corpses clears crowds while the necromancer directs from behind, and the fantasy holds until dungeons start working against it. Mid-campaign, the pivot to a bonemancer, a spellcaster slinging bone spear and bone spirit from a distance, lands as strange in the best way: the same character becomes two entirely different games. Amplify Damage, discovered almost by accident, reveals itself as far stronger than its description suggests, and the mercenary fills gaps the summons cannot. The respec that enables the pivot exists, but only once per difficulty, so the choice carries real weight.

The Horadric Cube anchors an economy of transmutation: gem upgrading, rejuvenation recipes and the Stealth runeword all emerge from experimentation, while gambling offers a lottery with terrible odds and irresistible pull. The Countess provides a reliable rune farm, skill shrines grant temporary boosts discovered rather than documented, and PlugY-style quality-of-life features (auto gold pickup, an expanded stash) sand off the inventory Tetris. The loop of explore-everything, hoard, sell and town-portal home remains the genre's founding rhythm, and almost everything the modern imitators copied is here in its original form.

Runewords and synergies are the systems' dark side. The rune-word combinations that transform ordinary socketed gear into endgame equipment are essentially undocumented in-game, discoverable only through outside knowledge or countless failed experiments, and the synergy system that makes skill investment multiplicatively powerful is similarly opaque. Learning by accident is delightful the first time and punishing the hundredth. No manual explains any of it, and the game offers no apology; the depth is real, but the price of admission is either a veteran friend or a wiki open in another tab.

The Maggot Lair is where summoner fantasy goes to die. Single-file corridors clog instantly with minions that cannot pass one another, filled with enemies that discharge lightning the moment they are struck, and corpses for resurrecting are scarce precisely where the build needs them most. Kurast's sewers sprawl comically large for their content, Act 4 feels rushed out in small areas crammed with enemies to pad the time, and reanimating foes keep rising after death to grind the exploration loop to a halt. Act 5's snow terrain compounds the problem at the map level, hiding both paths and waypoints beneath a white-out that sabotages the completionist habit.

Boss balance skews strange. Diablo lands as a genuine fight, the only one that does; Baal barely registers, and the Mephisto encounter collapses into pathos. The secret Cow Level, unlocked through the covetable Wirt's Leg ritual, delivers murderous cows every bit as ridiculous as its reputation promises and closes a zero-death, everything-cleared run on exactly the right absurdist note. Stamina bars, weapons that break beyond repair at zero durability and an un-disable-able version watermark round out the 2000-era annoyances, none serious enough to dent the core.

Overall, this is an incredibly good game that still holds up two decades later, and the proof is that ten-hour farming detours feel like a privilege rather than a chore. The depth-from-secrecy design, the army-to-bonemancer arc and the pure exploration compulsion it was built to serve all deliver exactly as remembered. The rough edges are annoyances, never dealbreakers, and the foundation underneath them has not aged a day.
