Twenty-four short missions across three national campaigns built the template everything after followed, and the structure still works. Private Martin's pathfinder drop into Normandy anchors the American arc through Sainte-Mère-Église, the manor battery and the château rescue; the British campaign follows the SAS dam raids and ship boarding; and the Soviet arc runs from weaponless Volga landings through Red Square, the Stalingrad sewers and Warsaw to the Reichstag flag. Historical quotes and Order 227's not-one-step-back set a period tone the campaigns earn, and each national section carries a distinct character rather than a reskinned checklist.

The car-convoy escape from the château remains the mission most fondly remembered, and on replay it is still gloriously chaotic: stealing a German car, conducting drive-bys at speed, and a frantic ride to battalion that trades the cover-shooter rhythm for pure momentum. Elsewhere the timed POW camp raid, the Eder Dam sabotage, the truck chase to the airfield and the multi-part Berlin finale keep the roster varied. Captain Price's debut here gives the campaign archaeological value, spotting the origin of the series' most enduring character decades before his later fame.

Getting the game running on modern Linux is a saga in itself. Only Proton 5 works, newer versions wreck the colour palette, and specific launch arguments are needed for gamma and resolution; the tinkering is the most any PC game in recent memory has demanded, but the payoff, a clean 1440×1080 presentation of a 2003 classic, is its own trophy. The compatibility notoriety has become part of the game's identity on modern systems, and the effort is documented well enough that patient players can replicate it.

The era's systems cut both ways. No sprint and no regenerating health read as charming period honesty to some sensibilities and as friction to others; weapon-limited reloads (the M1 Garand famously cannot be topped up mid-clip) demand discipline the game rarely teaches. Med-kit RNG swings between generous and stingy across the campaign in ways that feel deliberate, and the keyboard-and-mouse scheme rewards practice the older controller habits do not transfer to. None of it breaks the game; all of it dates it.

Defence and hold-the-line missions are the campaign's weakest stretch. Sparse med kits, practically useless ally AI, and brutal checkpoints make the Warsaw factory hold and the apartment defences a grind of repeated deaths; it is effectively one soldier against every enemy under the sun while AI squadmates stand in doorways. The Red Square weaponless landing and the sewers test patience similarly. Friendly-fire rules compound the confusion, failing instantly for early missions then going effectively invisible later, an inconsistency that punishes without teaching.

The crouch-walk animation of every soldier remains absurd two decades on, all shuffling elbows and goofy posture, a cosmetic quirk that has aged into affection. Checkpoint placement elsewhere is fair, and the short mission structure means a bad stretch never lasts long. The pacing discipline, punchy missions with distinct objectives and no filler, is arguably the founding game's most influential trait, inherited by everything that followed.

Overall, the foundation of the series is still fun, warts and all: the three-campaign structure, the car-convoy chaos and Price's debut hold up completely, and the Proton 5 compatibility fight is a satisfying trophy once won. The weak ally AI, RNG med kits and unforgiving defence missions are genuine deductions, but as the origin point of a genre-defining franchise, this remains essential archaeology that happens to be a good game.
