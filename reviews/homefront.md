The war comes home. An alternate 2027 finds a collapsed, EMP-struck United States under the occupation of the Greater Korean Republic, and the story follows a downed pilot pulled into the Colorado resistance. The setting does the distinguishing work: the villains are neither the usual catalogue of Nazis nor Russians, the battlefield is suburban America rather than some foreign city, and the whole thing is framed through in-fiction propaganda, from radio broadcasts and Voice of Freedom transmissions to fake news reports. Occupied-America worldbuilding of this documentary flavour was rare in the era, and it still reads convincingly.

The opening bus sequence earns its reputation. A child watches its parents executed in front of it before the title card has finished settling, and the scene anchors everything that follows, staying memorable a decade later. Grim set-pieces keep landing from there: a mass grave ditch, and a white phosphorus strike burning through a parking lot in one of the more disturbing moments the genre managed that year. The pacing is ruthlessly compressed, with the safe haven destroyed only a few missions after its introduction, a structural cruelty that plays like an interactive disaster film.

Gun audio is a genuine strength, carrying real punch through every exchange and rewarding the firepower with weight. Visuals hold up better than expected, and small touches add texture: silent-takedown stealth through the survivalist camp, sniper overwatch during the church defence, and odd NPC dialogue vignettes that exist purely as flavour, including one memorable enquiry about milking a goat. Collectible notes flesh out the occupation's rise, though the supply is thin enough that the world keeps promising more than it delivers.

Goliath, the support drone, is a mid-campaign highlight when it fights alongside the squad, wading through resistance with cheerful indifference. Its later appearance as a chopper-vs-armour escape fares worse: control remapping lands mid-fight, and glancing down to read the countermeasure bar while dodging tanks is a reliable way to die. The set-piece ambition is real; the execution fumbles the handover.

Controls are the campaign's longest-running irritation. Aim-down-sights is toggle-only with no hold option, controller support is effectively absent, with no aim assist and glyph-less button prompts, and the scheme produces repeated mistakes throughout. Absent too is a slide mechanic, felt keenly when hopping in from other shooters, and unclear enemy fire origins occasionally leave deaths feeling arbitrary. Late defence sections spawn enemies endlessly, rendering sniping pointless against waves that never thin, and ammo for specific weapons, the ACR rifle among them, sometimes cannot be replenished at all.

The campaign is strictly linear, cover-based street clearing with squadmates: houses and yards, sentry towers, a helicopter infiltration, a driving convoy chase, and a Golden Gate Bridge finale with an AA-gun capture timer. There is no difficulty selection, and the whole thing runs two to three hours, which was the central criticism at release at full price and remains the honest measure now.

Overall, an exceptionally short but compelling campaign that still lands because its core idea, occupied suburban America rendered through propaganda broadcasts and genuinely grim set-pieces, has aged better than its reputation. The bus opening alone justifies the visit, and the compressed structure plays like a feature rather than a failing when priced accordingly. Tolerate the dated control quirks and the recommendation holds.
