A WWI French bunker, silent after a German bombardment, holds the enlisted dead, a sealed-off Beast and an officer corps that blew the only exit behind them as they fled. The horror arrives through paper: a sabotage tribunal, pagan tunnel texts, a soldier's doomed wager, and a corporal who drank crater water with a strange sweet taste. The atmosphere is diesel, candlelight and heartbeat, dread built from sound rather than sight. The log-driven tragedy turns environmental reading into the game's actual story delivery, and the Lambert theory can be assembled independently from the medical note and the crater timeline. Few horror games deliver their story this way and make it matter.

The full randomisation is what makes the game work. Codes, item placement, traps and even which corpses spawn where are randomised, so memorising codes is impossible and every run is fresh. The trap randomisation is a genuine surprise, going well beyond the expected code shuffling. The design thesis is devious and brilliant: the main threat is sensitive to noise, the world is pitch black except for one flashlight that is itself really loud. Learning to read the Beast's audio language, the heartbeat cue when it is out of its hole, the dust falling from ceilings, the hole-network rumble, becomes the game's core skill. It can be genuinely out-thought rather than merely outrun.

The single connected bunker opens tool by tool along a daisy-chain route: red crank handle to maintenance, wrench to prison, bolt cutters to Arsenal, then dynamite and plunger. The power system trades light for noise decisions, the corpse-burning Molotov loop manages the rats, and the fuel generator ticks down as a run-long clock. The blackout crisis is peak horror, surviving on borrowed time with a single bullet, and the pressure is real until Fuel Storage unlocks. Once it does, the tension evaporates, and the temptation of a no-generator run becomes the real endgame for hard-mode players. The tool-unlock route rewards planning and re-running with increasing efficiency.

Bullets only ever buy seconds. The Beast comes back angrier, and the design makes armed survival a resource decision rather than a solution. Gas grenades are a carried-but-never-loved item with no obvious application against rats and a mutant, and the chapel confessional subverts scripted-event expectations in an odd direction, delivering a dead body and nothing else. The first death to an unseen tripwire in the tunnel approach is a fair lesson, and the cosmetic hole that exists purely to make the player worry is a clever psychological trick. Two deaths across a full run is a reasonable toll for the darkness involved.

The rabbit-doll finale is the game's masterstroke. The toy from the journals becomes the kill mechanism, and returning it to a dying man's arms as his last bit of humanity shines through makes the monster a tragedy. The ending lands with emotional weight that the genre rarely earns, and it retroactively recontextualises the Beast's behaviour throughout. The series-standard door controls, holding and then pushing with the stick, aggravate throughout, though the tension-driven reasoning behind them is fair. The no-music safe room and knife-chain inventory cap are minor quirks. The finale justifies every dark corridor.

The stats screen review, with over a hundred saves and two deaths, and the Shell Shock and Custom Mode hooks give the game real replay legs. No other horror game randomises its codes, the thing players traditionally memorise, and pairs that with an audio-first stalker that rewards genuine learning. It is the rare entry in the series where the player is armed the entire time, yet the armament never solves the problem. Roughly four to six hours is a fair ask for the density, and the seed randomisation means a second run is a different game. The design confidence is total.

Overall, a devious, beautifully systemic bunker crawler whose randomised seeds turn even its deaths into progress, with the Beast's readable audio behaviour and the rabbit-doll finale as the standout achievements. The fuel system flattening once Fuel Storage unlocks, the series door controls and a few carried-but-useless items are honest costs, but the audio-first stalker and the paper-built tragedy make it a genuinely great horror experience.
