A mountaineering expedition to the never-summitted sacred peak Chomolonzo awakens a curse, and a climber ascends the Tibetan-style valley to find his missing brother. Ghosts pour down into the deserted city, and the horror builds through slow-burn dread rather than jump scares: dead birds, rotting markets, endless fog. Found-document storytelling (monks' warnings, dying climbers' logs, villagers' prayers) gives the disaster genuine tragic weight, and the horror leans on Tibetan Buddhist cosmology, bardos, terma and hungry ghosts, rather than generic tropes. Authenticity of this depth is vanishingly rare in the genre. Few games marry real religious cosmology to survival-horror structure.

Symbol-tracing combat is the standout mechanic: drawing ritual glyphs with the mouse to banish ghosts, each victory sealed with a satisfying tracing flourish. The ranged purification system surprises as effectively a ghost shooter rather than the expected melee, with consecrated tools (ice axe, Kartika, Kila, Lagpa, Kolo) and third-eye vision revealing the spirit world. Purifying shrines with incense heals, which quietly makes the Kila the best weapon by reward structure. Melee is nearly useless in real combat, its function reduced to breaking open pots. Swaying ghosts make shots easy to miss, which keeps encounters deliberate rather than twitchy.

Weapon balance is uneven across the arsenal: the Kila supersedes the starting axe, the Lagpa trades healing for instant kills, and the Kolo shotgun disappoints as a generally not-very-good option. Twelve chapters run roughly seven and a half hours across two sittings, with health upgrades from prophecy statues and a large collectible layer of lore notes, Lando Chronicles and smashable pots. Lore actively outranks loot as a hunting priority, and the note stream tells the disaster's story in affecting fragments. The collectible design serves the worldbuilding rather than completion percentage. That choice is the game at its best.

Climbing itself is the connective tissue between encounters, and it carries the mountaineering fantasy better than most games manage. Balance segments ask for measured stick movements along ledges, QTE-style mouse motions punctuate the tougher traverses, and the thin-air aesthetic sells the altitude even where the bugged gauge fails to. The ascent structure gives the campaign a legible shape: villages at the base, the monastery in the middle reaches, the sacred peak reserved for the finale. Each camp marks progress with genuine weight, because the game has made the climb itself the antagonist's territory. Movement is slow, but the slowness is the setting speaking.

Linearity is both comfort and limitation: the path is strictly one-way with repeated can't-go-back moments, yet the flow never strands or confuses. Fixed camera angles evoke classic survival horror but regularly obstruct fights and navigation, and tank-like shifts compound the old-school friction. Sprinting-alerts-ghosts is introduced with no clear rules and barely matters in practice. The vulture and bird-demon fights draw the sharpest complaints, mostly because maneuverability collapses when it is needed most. Classic-camera devotees will forgive most of this; everyone else should temper expectations.

The oxygen-pressure gauge introduced at altitude appears completely bugged on PC, never visibly dropping, and altitude sections lose their intended tension as a result. The final boss concludes confusingly, collapsing and vanishing after the terma box interaction with no clear defeat beat, an ending that feels disjointed even while its thematic reframe of the demons as personal demons lands neatly. The Europe-only port offers little beyond resolution changes and no controller support, though the mouse-driven tracing is far superior to the original's motion controls. Getting an abandoned port running is part of the experience. The obscurity is almost the point.

Overall, the glyph-tracing combat, the deeply researched Tibetan setting and the affecting found-document storytelling make this forgotten curio genuinely worth seeking out, despite fixed-camera friction, swaying enemy hitboxes, a bugged oxygen meter and a muddled final boss. The PC port's mouse aiming is the definitive way to play, and the combat impresses more than any prior expectation suggested. Patience is rewarded with atmosphere few modern horrors attempt, and the recommendation stands on that atmosphere alone.
