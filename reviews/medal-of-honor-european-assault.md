A WWII campaign tours four theatres (Saint Nazaire, North Africa, Stalingrad and the Ardennes) with proper mission-intro narration each time, and the framing does more work than the plot itself, which stays skimmable at best. Era context matters: this was among the first shooters to make aim-down-sights feel standard, and that novelty is part of why it remains fondly remembered. The tone is earnest mid-2000s WWII pastiche, and the mission-based structure with main and optional objectives gives each tour a shape its contemporaries mostly lacked. Gold medals for completing every optional objective reward thoroughness without demanding perfection.

The presentation is the first thing worth addressing. The game runs internally at roughly 20 FPS, and at that speed it is a sluggish crawl; patched to 60 FPS with resolution fixes, it is transformed into something genuinely playable, night and day. That gap is not a small quality-of-life note but the difference between a chore and a good time, and it colours every other judgement here. Ragdoll physics, comic at the higher framerate, add unexpected levity to firefights.

Squad systems give the campaign its distinct tactical flavour. Position and recall orders, a medkit-carrying economy and squad-revival mechanics separate it from its Call of Duty contemporaries, which shipped without any of that infrastructure. Revives and medkits carry over as rewards from completed missions, tightening the loop across the campaign. The trouble is that the squad-command layer never quite earns mastery: the orders system is easy to ignore, and sending squads late in the game mostly amounts to spending them.

Adrenaline is the key tool. The power grants invincibility, unlimited ammunition and faster fire, and it turns the notorious bridge run in Climbing Mamayev Hill from a wall into a solvable set-piece. Without it, several late stretches tip from tense into punishing, because death arrives absurdly fast: three bullets and the mission restarts, harsher still at the higher framerate where reaction windows shrink. Checkpoints simply do not exist, so save states are the only mercy the design offers.

Ammo scarcity is the campaign's most persistent flaw. Russia in particular starves the arsenal, and hunting pickups interrupts firefights that already tilt against the player. Weapon feel compounds it: hit detection is unreliable enough that shots into walls are indistinguishable from hits, recoil sends guns skyward unpredictably, and the Thompson earns its place as the least enjoyable gun in the roster. Broken textures and mush-prone visual bugs dot the older environments.

The final Von Schrader encounter is the low point of the design. It is built on pure enemy spam, wave after wave with no tactical answer beyond attrition, and the fight's own conclusion is confusing enough that victory can arrive without clarity about why. Squad AI is hopeless throughout: allies sit in open enemy fire, ignore cover, and generally need babysitting the game never teaches. Battle of the Bulge missions such as Farmhouse Liberation, with their tank-defence structure and three-bullet fragility, expose every rough edge at once.

What carries the campaign through the collapse is momentum. The first two-thirds sail by on solid mission design and the novelty of the patched framerate, and the difficulty spike arrives only once Russia begins. The four-theatre variety keeps encounters fresh even as the systems underneath creak, and the optional-objective structure means the game rewards players who push beyond the critical path. It is a game whose best qualities are structural rather than technical.

Overall, this is a nostalgic WWII campaign rescued entirely by performance patches, with a squad-tactical identity its contemporaries lacked and an ending stretch that undoes much of the goodwill: starved ammunition, fragile health, dumb allies and an enemy-spam finale. Patch it, tolerate the jank, and the first two-thirds remain a strong argument for why this series was once the genre's standard-bearer.
