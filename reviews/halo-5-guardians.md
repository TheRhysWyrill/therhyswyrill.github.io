Fun shooter, messy story: the split that defines this entry announces itself immediately and never resolves. A manhunt structure sets two fireteams in opposition, and the trio trailing the famous supersoldier arrives with zero introduction, characters nobody has heard of and no reason to care about. The narrative pivot at the centre is tropey, later scrapped entirely by the next game, leaving this campaign a strange fever dream in series terms. Gameplay, fortunately, does not care about any of that, and the combat sandbox carries the whole experience on its back. The strange thing is how well the two halves coexist: the mess never infects the mechanics, and the mechanics never rescue the mess.

The squad-revival system replaces instant death, and it is the campaign's defining mechanic. Downed Spartans revive instead of restarting, which removes all the classic failure frustration, and AI teammates return the favour constantly. The trade-off is dim squadmates who go down themselves at an alarming rate, one teammate in particular dying often enough to become a running joke. As a design, it trades difficulty for flow, and the flow wins.

The modernised sandbox layers sprint, thrusters, ground pound and Spartan charge over smart-linked weapons, and the arsenal rewards experimentation: the Boltshot is surprisingly good, the SAW a solid pickup. Not everything survived the redesign pass. The plasma pistol takes a harsh nerf, the SMG is useless, and iconic designs were changed for the worse, with the shotgun turning ugly and the rocket launcher losing its one-shot identity to a generic look. Coming from the classic control scheme, the new layout fights the player before being grown into.

Fifteen missions split into short chapters, several of which run so briefly that their separate-mission status puzzles, with vehicle set-pieces supplying the peaks: a Mantis sequence, a Kraken assault, a Phantom ride and a Feton flight. The Guardian awakening at Meridian, the collapsing space-elevator escape and the ocean-plate escape as the Titan jumps are genuinely spectacular, and the pre-rendered cutscenes rival CGI films even by the hardware standards of the day. The presentation budget shows in every frame.

The Warden Eternal is fought six-plus times, identically each time, and wears out his welcome badly. The first encounter is a cool fight; the seventh identical one is tedium, and the repetition is the run's biggest structural gripe. Intel collection fares little better: 117 logs play unsubtitled, connect to little, and run a few seconds apiece, telling so little story that the hunt collapses partway for anyone who misses one. Several missions being separated without reason compounds the sense of a campaign cut into arbitrary pieces.

Brutes vanish inexplicably, elites turn on humanity again without explanation, and the cliffhanger resolves nowhere, though the ocean-plate escape at least gives the finale spectacle to lean on. As a time capsule the campaign remains a curiosity: the only entry whose story the series itself later discarded, and the only one built entirely around a two-fireteam chase structure. Judged as pure shooter, it holds up; judged as narrative, it is best ignored, which is precisely how its own successors treated it. The co-op framing at least means the squad system was designed for company from the start, and the campaign plays as well alongside friends as it does with its own AI.

Overall, this is mechanically excellent squad combat and film-grade spectacle wrapped around a tropey, later-discarded story, with repeated identical Warden fights and unsubtitled intel as the friction. Fun if the plot is ignored, and worth playing for the sandbox even with the caveats.
