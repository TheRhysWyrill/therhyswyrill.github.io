Few titles announce their intentions as plainly as this one, and few deliver on the promise so completely. A vegetable farmer refuses to pay his taxes, receives a shovel as taxation, and is dispatched on errands across a cutesy top-down world where every envelope can be torn up and every achievement adds a new charge to the wanted poster. The title tells the whole story: absurd, self-aware, and proudly unserious. What the title does not tell is how much craft hides beneath the nonsense, from puzzle design to a sci-fi undercurrent that gradually reframes the whole cheerful setting.

Joke density is the engine. The game oozes character at every turn: a soil-sword acquisition staged as a direct homage to a certain green-tunic adventure, a full naval copypasta delivered straight-faced, anime cameos, streamer parodies, and running gags embedded in achievements and tax documents. The references never pause long enough to wear out, and the writing commits to each bit completely rather than winking at the audience. Meme-literate players will catch more layers, but the comedy works even where the citations do not.

Puzzles stay plant-based and gentle. Watering cans grow vines over gaps, boom blooms clear obstacles, and portals bend routes through compact dungeons. The difficulty rarely rises above pleasant, with one exception: a graveyard sequence of liar riddles posed by pumpkins whose logic resists parsing, the kind of puzzle that invites brute-force guessing rather than deduction. Everywhere else the environmental design communicates clearly, and combat (light, tool-based, rarely demanding) never interrupts the flow for long.

The Limitless Line train mode arrived after launch as a free roguelike diversion, sixteen cars deep with dozens of enemies per run. It is fun, and it is free, and it is thin: six trinkets to choose from four at a time, a fixed boss roster, and none of the run-to-run variety that makes the genre compelling. One run reveals most of what the mode has to offer, and repeated runs surface no new modifiers to chase. It functions as a bonus rather than a second game.

Underneath the comedy sits genuinely coherent lore. Field logs, a mushroom-cloud mural, radioactive goop, and quiet mentions of human remains foreshadow a backstory that recontextualises the vegetable society without ever turning preachy. The tonal contrast between the sunny town and the extinction narrative gives the short campaign a spine, and the finale leans into its own corniness with an anime-styled showdown, a deified onion, and a credits sequence that includes a full theme song. Absurdity with structure underneath is a rare combination, and it lands here.

Completion runs quick and clean. Hats, heart pieces, and rip-able documents collect smoothly across a two-to-three-hour span, with 100% achievable in a single sitting and the credits arriving before the joke density has a chance to sag. The pacing understands its own scale: nothing outstays its welcome, and the constant stream of gags ensures the runtime feels dense rather than empty. Post-game content adds a little more for those inclined, even if the train mode's depth runs shallow.

Music and presentation match the tone throughout, with bright sprites, expressive character animation, and a soundtrack that pivots between cheerful town themes and surprisingly dramatic boss music. The art style sells every punchline, and small touches (the escalating wanted-poster charges, the rip-able mail) show a development team inventing jokes out of systems rather than pasting them on.

Overall, this is a short, absurd, brilliantly written bite-size adventure that delivers exactly what its title promises, with coherent sci-fi lore hiding under the memes and a credits sequence that commits harder than most full campaigns. The shallow train roguelike and one brute-forced riddle gauntlet are the only real stumbles. Judge it as a joke-dense evening of comedy and it over-delivers.
