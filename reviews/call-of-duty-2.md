Three interleaved Second World War campaigns carry the game: the Soviets from Moscow to Stalingrad, the British across North Africa from El Alamein to Tunisia, and the US Rangers from Pointe du Hoc to the Rhine crossing. Archival-style narration frames the acts, and the period detail holds up across every theatre. Captain Price's classic appearance is a genuine treat, and the Tunisian-accented McGregor gives the British campaign its own flavour. There is barely a story beyond the events of the war itself, and the ending arrives with surprising abruptness, but the structure was never about plot. The template this game set is the reason the genre looks the way it does.

The technical state is the headline surprise for a 2005 release. It runs buttery smooth, hitting absurd frame rates even at the title screen and holding around two hundred in play, and it looks far better than its console versions ever managed. Detailed environments, legible sound design, and an optimisation job that outclasses many far newer titles make the campaign a pleasure to simply occupy. Few games of its vintage reward a fresh playthrough this cleanly. The visuals alone justify the revisit.

Mission variety is the structural strength: tank-driving battles, an armoured-car escape, MG and AA emplacement sequences, smoke-grenade assaults, and marathon defensive holds all punctuate the core cover-based squad shooting. The Stalingrad hold-the-line sequences, the D-Day roadblock retreats and the multi-wave Hill 400 finale each demand different rhythms. Hill 400 in particular is rough, a multi-wave defence that tests every lesson the game has taught. The core loop never stagnates across roughly seven hours. The variety is what keeps the marathon honest.

Checkpointing is generous to a fault, keeping frequent deaths painless and ensuring nothing is ever really lost to a bad encounter. This is a game without regenerating health, and the medkit-plus-checkpoint economy is a big part of why it still works today. Instant-fail friendly fire from squadmates who constantly block the firing line is a design decision that could have been miserable, and the trigger proves inconsistent in practice, occasionally forgiving shots it should punish. Enemies blend badly into snow and ruins, making visual identification harder than it should be. The Thompson's tiny twenty-round magazine is nothing in a firefight.

Grenade spam is the single biggest complaint: enemies are absurdly grenade-happy, hurling volleys at a frequency that would embarrass the highest difficulties of far newer games, and the Hill 400 fight's six-at-once salvos push it to its peak. Grenades cannot be thrown back, compounding the frustration. Enemy pop-in breaks the illusion in open areas, and prone positions on steps occasionally misread as fatal errors. The one mission-breaking bug came when an armoured-car driver AI simply refused to drive, stranding the turret and forcing a level restart. All of it is survivable, and none of it outweighs the strengths.

The multi-national campaign template basically set the genre standard, and the regenerating-health-free, checkpoint-driven structure remains a refreshing contrast to modern conventions. Played with mouse and keyboard, the aiming feels precise in a way controller players of the era never experienced. The defensive missions drag in the middle third, where being stuck in place tests patience more than skill. The final push restores the glow. The game earns its reputation through sheer mechanical confidence.

Overall, a genre-defining classic that still plays beautifully today, with smooth performance, varied tank, escape and defensive missions and forgiving checkpointing outweighing relentless grenade spam, poor enemy visibility and one mission-breaking AI bug. Bring patience for grenades and the three campaigns deliver a genuinely fun, generously structured slice of shooter history.
