A shrine maiden descends into the Underworld to defeat the Queen of a cursed shrine, and the whole campaign unfolds in under an hour. Minimal, charming, and deliberately story-lite: an eccentric priestess turns up repeatedly to trade barbs, a mid-game duel with a princess resolves into mutual respect, and a melancholy true-ending reveal about a cursed soul supplies the emotional weight the runtime cannot. The freeware lineage is obvious and unapologetic, blending Cave Story's item-collecting sensibility with Mega Man's tight platforming, and it is available free with name-your-own-price. Almost nothing here is wasted, because almost nothing here has time to be.

Ability gating is classic and efficient. A seal-throwing charm handles ranged damage, a wave charm throws projectiles through walls, a double jump arrives mid-game, a twinkle pendant grants the dash, and the green leaf upgrades melee for the true-ending route. Faith-statue blessings extend the health pool, and the world scrolls seamlessly between areas so enemies can chase across zone boundaries, a touch that keeps tension alive during traversal. Difficulty comes mostly from environmental damage and the endgame, not from the gating itself, and the loop respects the map-completion instinct without padding.

The bite-sized length is the whole appeal. The true ending plus all hearts lands in roughly 43 minutes on a first pass, and a second run for the standard ending takes under 20, because once the routes are known the game blasts past in a genuine speedrun flow. Both endings and full item completion fit inside 90 minutes, and the density-per-minute is the feature most metroidvanias abandon. The pacing is aggressive in the best sense: no room to overstay, and every ability unlocked is immediately used.

The final boss spikes hard in speed and damage. Late-fight phases accelerate dramatically and deal roughly three and a half hearts per hit, a wall entirely out of proportion with the rest of the campaign, and environmental damage already keeps the pressure on without the boss escalating further. The green leaf, the true-ending melee upgrade, arrives so late that the first run's final fight is harder for no gain, and the dash cannot be angled downward, only left or right, an odd limitation that costs a few jumps. None of it dents the fun, but all of it is real friction.

Enemy design is the thinnest layer. Foes are one-note, and some carry health pools bloated relative to the toolset, with the starting leaf chipping away at brutes that simply refuse to fall until the upgrades arrive. Combat depth is sparse; the appeal is traversal and route mastery rather than fighting. The map's final three tiles remain unreachable at 97%, and working out that they equal a theoretical 100% requires deduction the game never offers; the hidden developer room rewards those who dig, but completionists get no signposting toward it.

The structure rewards replays properly. The two endings branch meaningfully, the second run at speed feels like a different game, and the freeware price makes the 90-minute total an easy recommendation on value alone. As the ancestor of a beloved series it also carries the connective tissue that later entries build on, with characters whose recurrence rewards the full timeline. The hidden developer room and the theoretical-100% map give the dedicated something extra beyond the standard completion push.

Overall, this is a free, ultra-condensed metroidvania that respects time more aggressively than anything else in its genre, packing two endings, full item completion and a genuine speedrun-worthy second lap into roughly 90 minutes. The seamless scrolling world, the efficient ability gating and the charm of its minimal cast carry it past the spiked final boss and the late-arriving green leaf. Bite-sized, but a game nonetheless, and a complete one.
