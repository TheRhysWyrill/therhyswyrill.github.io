Stranger of Paradise: Final Fantasy Origin wears its inheritance openly, mashing Nioh's gear-and-job systems onto the story beats of the series' first entry, and the mash works better than any premise this goofy has a right to. The story plays as a prequel that sets up the original rather than retelling it, and the reveal that the party occupies the villain's seat recontextualises the entire campaign with delighted shock. Fists are the protagonist's through-line; Jack really does just punch everything to death. What begins as an experiment in expectation ends as one of the more mechanically satisfying action-RPGs of its year, deeper than its presentation ever advertises.

The job system is the game's defining strength, far deeper than its surface suggests. Basic jobs gate advanced ones, advanced gate expert, expert gate master, a tree structure echoing Final Fantasy Tactics that rewards committing progress into specific lines before branching. The freedom for builds is enormous without ever feeling overwhelming, and the tree opens gradually enough that curiosity rather than confusion drives exploration. A build journey from pugilist through sword fighter, knight and Paladin to Tyrant shows the range available to any persistent loadout.

Break-gauge executions drive the combat loop, and the numbers back their centrality: the overwhelming majority of enemies fall to executions rather than conventional kills. The loop never tires, and Holy Fang sustain makes the Paladin build genuinely satisfying, activating before an execution and reclaiming a chunk of health on the kill. Equipment level rather than character level determines power, Nioh-style, and job affinity from gear drives customisation, making every drop a build decision rather than mere stat noise. Difficulty modes, absent from its inspiration, make this the more forgiving entry of the family.

Loot volume is the double-edged sword. The sheer amount of shite picked up borders on chore territory, with menu friction and dismantling management consuming real time between missions, though auto-dismantle softens the worst of it. Rarity colour-coding compounds the confusion, with yellow, green, purple and red tiers so off from genre convention that reading a drop takes conscious effort. Equipment churn is part of the genre's contract, but the presentation of it here fights the player.

Boss difficulty swings wildly. The Master Tonberry side mission stomps any unprepared party at nearly any level, while later story bosses fall with barely a scratch, and the two-phase Tiamat fight lands somewhere between. Level design is small and linear by design, though the shortcuts scattered through levels rarely earn their keep, existing rather than functioning. The mission structure repeats openly, each main mission paired with a side mission that runs the same ground in reverse.

Party AI ignores potions entirely, forcing constant micromanagement to keep companions alive, and auto-scrolling story text moves faster than it can be read, burying the Lufenian worldbuilding that rewards the chase through Fool's Missives and logs. The lore is genuinely intriguing for those willing to hunt it, lore-heavy in the way that respects rather than punishes attention. These small failures sit alongside a campaign that otherwise respects the player's build choices.

Overall, Stranger of Paradise: Final Fantasy Origin is a surprisingly deep action-RPG that honours its source material by revealing the villain's seat has been occupied all along. The job tree's depth and break-gauge combat carry it past loot clutter, uneven difficulty and linear missions. Judge it as its own game rather than as a curiosity, and it earns its place.
