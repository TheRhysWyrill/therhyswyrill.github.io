Twenty-four cups across the base game and the Booster Course Pass make this the biggest kart racer ever assembled, with hundreds of tracks, two credit sequences and cross-generational remixes that reward long memories, and no story gets in the way of any of it. Coconut Mall and Toad's Turnpike return faithfully, the SNES Rainbow Road is left untouched exactly as it played decades ago, and the sheer volume of content is the reason this entry sits at the top of its line. Nothing else in the series comes close on content, and the Grand Prix structure gives the whole catalogue a natural completion loop.

The soundtrack carries real personality, and the Tour arrangements rank above the originals they adapt. Mermaid Byways and Singapore Speedway hum along beautifully, Big Blue borrows F-Zero's energy to great effect, and the music regularly syncs with the racing itself, hitting its beat precisely as a cannon launch fires. The audio design is a genuine strength rather than background filler, and it sustains interest across a full completion run.

Item chaos defines the experience, roughly thirty per cent skill and seventy per cent items by honest estimation. Blue shells, lightning bolts and boos arrive relentlessly at 150cc, typically paired with a red shell in a familiar combo, and the distribution leans rigged from the front: leading racers draw nothing defensive while midfield positions pull triple bananas, a pattern that outstrips even the community's own frequency data. A desire to customise the difficulty, 150cc speed with 100cc itemisation, points at a design lever the game refuses to offer. Miracle plays balance the ledger occasionally, with horn-spamming a blue shell into nothing standing as the run's unrepeatable high.

Solo play holds up as its own complete mode rather than a practice lap for multiplayer. The Grand Prix structure gives every cup a natural beginning and end, rubber-band pacing keeps nearly every race competitive into the final corner, and a perfect sixty-point cup arrives as a genuinely earned reward rather than a participation trophy. Roughly six hours covers all twenty-four cups at a steady pace, and the run never repeats itself, because the track order cycles remixes, originals and Tour additions in a rotation that stays fresh. Even with no human opposition, the AI's personality supplies rivalry, and the campaign closes feeling complete rather than padded.

Character mechanics reward experimentation over meta-chasing. Racer weight genuinely affects top speed, a detail easy to miss for years, and builds built for fun rather than leaderboard efficiency stay competitive enough to matter, with the optimal setups deliberately ignored in favour of whatever drives well. Monster-truck wheels on a sneaker or a landship hold their own, bikes remain a preference rather than a requirement, and rubber-banding rivals, an ever-present dinosaur at the front, a spooky king and a dog with cup-winning pace, supply personality the track list alone could not. A perfect sixty-point cup arrives as its own reward for a run where everything clicks.

Presentation quirks trim the polish. Highlight reels after a strong race show only one track, costing replays of the best saves, and newly unlocked karts auto-equip without asking, disorienting a preferred setup mid-campaign. Lightning strikes distort the music at the worst moments, and the odd meme coin winks at internet culture without derailing anything. These are small irritations in a package this complete, but the highlight-reel limitation in particular squanders the game's own best moments.

Overall, the enormous track catalogue, the superb Tour arrangements and the personality of the rubber-banding roster make this the definitive content-rich kart racer, and the item chaos is both its signature thrill and its biggest flaw. Unskippable reels and forced auto-equip are minor costs, and even in solo the whole thing stays very, very fun.
