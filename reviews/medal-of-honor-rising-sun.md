The Pacific-war campaign follows Marine Joseph Griffin from Pearl Harbor through Guadalcanal, Singapore, Burma and finally a Japanese carrier, chasing the stolen Yamashita gold and his prisoner-of-war brother Donnie. Interstitial letters from home and historical narration give the campaign an emotional warmth few shooters of its era attempted, and the historical framing holds up better than the mechanics. The Pearl Harbor opening remains a standout set piece twenty years on, with a ship going up in flames in a moment that never fades from memory. The brother-in-arms POW thread and the Golden Lily gold-heist plot give the war a personal and a conspiratorial register at once.

Nine missions across four to five hours deliver genuinely varied structures: an on-rails Pearl Harbour defence, jungle night raids, a Singapore stealth-infiltration sequence with a uniform disguise, train-bridge sabotage and a multi-stage carrier sabotage finale with vents, fuel valves, a demolition charge and an escape. Chef enemies who open with throwing knives and the disguise section are era-specific oddities no other Medal of Honour game attempts. Health relies on medkits and canteen drops, with save radios scattered manually across the levels. The mission design's variety is the campaign's backbone.

The Singapore stealth is the campaign's most distinctive hour, trading firefights for uniform-based infiltration and a quieter tension the rest of the game does not attempt. The carrier finale multiplies objectives across vents and fuel valves before a demolition-charge escape, and the multi-stage design gives the ending genuine structure. Canteen pickups restore health in bursts that keep the run survivable through the tougher stretches. The pacing between stealth, sabotage and open combat is confident for its era. The campaign never lets a single rhythm dominate.

Enemies that appear behind the player with no explanation are easily the most annoying thing in the game, and even with several AI companions in tow the ambushes never stop. Bayonet-wielding enemies who rush for an instant kill are a constant threat, and late-war enemy weapons melt health absurdly fast, with the final stage's guns able to destroy the player in no time flat. One enemy with an LMG in particular kicks the run squarely. The linear level design is far tighter than the open-ended sandbox half-remembered from childhood, with a single way to go and nothing else.

Input latency hampered aim throughout, degrading accuracy in ways vertical sync never quite mitigated, and reload and control quirks fight the whole run, with Square muscle memory and a blocked medkit pickup requiring an awkward side approach. Save points are sparse enough that progress nearly slipped, and the short campaign is a fair reminder of how brief older shooters were. The stealth disguise and the jungle raids hold up better than the enemy placement. None of the friction is fatal, and much of it is era-typical. The nostalgia carries the rest.

The campaign's interstitial warmth and its Pearl Harbour set piece carry the emotional weight, and the hidden film canisters and medals give completionists a reason to sweep the levels. The jungle night raids supply the campaign's atmosphere, and the train-bridge sabotage is a mid-run highlight. The sibling thread ends unresolved in this game alone, though its continuation elsewhere closes the loop for those who research it. The nostalgia trip is the point, and the point lands. The game is a fun, historically flavoured blast from the past.

Overall, a nostalgic Pacific-war romp whose Pearl Harbour opening, Singapore stealth and carrier-sabotage finale hold up as highlights, with the letters-from-home framing and the sibling mystery giving the campaign warmth. Enemies that spawn behind the player, one-kill bayonet charges, shredding late-war weapons and latency-degraded aim are honest costs, but the four-to-five-hour run delivers a fun, historically flavoured blast from the past for anyone chasing the era.
