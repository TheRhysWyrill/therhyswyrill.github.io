A solemn Tier 1 and AFO Neptune operation in Afghanistan's Shahikot Valley opens with the Ode of Remembrance and closes with a military funeral voiceover, and the tonal gravity holds throughout. This reboot trades the series' WWII pageantry for modern special-operations austerity, and the gritty, serious tone sets it apart from every other entry in the franchise. The hostage-execution shock beat lands, the finale hits hard, and the radio-chatter texture, with whoever is talking indicated by a fluttering icon, keeps the comms alive. The night stealth sections' quiet ambience leaves the player uneasy in exactly the right way. The brush-off general who orders the SEALs back to Bagram is a genuinely dislikeable presence, which is the point.

The austere tactical texture is the core identity: ammo scarcity, buddy ammo requests and realistic pacing make firefights tense rather than power-fantasy, and death arrives fast from a single burst. The multi-squad switching between Neptune, Wolfpack and the Rangers gives the campaign its structure. Highlights include the covert night infiltration, which plays like a first-person Splinter Cell, downward peeking, the DCS and AC-130-style airstrike sequence and the foggy ridge escape. The slow-mo door-breach sequences are lukewarm, but the pacing around them is lean. Roughly four and a half hours covers the full campaign, and the value-for-length holds.

The sniping and optics are genuinely useful, with enemy highlighting that reads clearly at range. The airstrike sequence is the standout set piece, and the foggy ridge escape earns its tension honestly. The score is generally strong, with one questionable cue on the Apache gunship level the only blemish. Buddy-resupply logistics, where allies only replenish the equipped primary, give the shooting a resource discipline most modern shooters lack. The respect-the-fallen framing is a deliberate contrast with the series' earlier pageantry. The set-piece variety holds from the first night mission to the funeral voiceover.

The weapon-switch penalty is the stingiest design choice: not every enemy drops a gun, and switching means losing access to allied resupply, which dissuades experimentation purely because of the ammo economy. Ammo requests and reloading share a button, causing constant misfires in the heat of firefights. Hitscan misses and flaky aim assist on non-sniper weapons made regular gunfights frustrating, with snapping that walks onto targets rather than helping. Grenade spam lands off-screen, and pop-in spawns by the bunker pile bodies visibly. One death came from the sergeant's own frag bouncing back. The on-rails helicopter crashes that strip the player to a knife are a recurring low.

The broken companion AI model that froze mid-animation turned a bug into an accidental highlight, with the mole decoy working incredibly well for a whole section. Invisible enemies in the ridge escape taxed the eyes more than the skills. The aim assist off is the right default for this game, since the assist snaps awkwardly. The cutscene-heavy pacing occasionally reads as watching a movie rather than playing a game. None of the friction is fatal, and the pacing's lean confidence carries the campaign. The finale's emotional weight justifies the jank.

The opening Ode of Remembrance and the closing military funeral voiceover bookend the campaign with a solemnity the series had never attempted, and Rabbit's arc gives the finale its personal stakes. The score holds up across the valley and the ridge, with the Apache gunship cue the only odd note. The value for the length is hard to argue with, and the lean pacing never wastes a mission. The austere identity is the reboot's real achievement. The respect-the-fallen framing lands.

Overall, a short, sombre reboot whose grounded tension, night-infiltration and airstrike set-pieces and lean pacing outweigh its stingy weapon systems, hit-detection misses and visible pop-in. The respect-the-fallen framing and the austere tactical texture give it an identity the series had lost, and the four-and-a-half-hour campaign respects the time it asks for.
