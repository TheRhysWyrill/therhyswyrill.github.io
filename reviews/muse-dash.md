A side-scrolling rhythm game in which cute anime heroines batter oncoming enemies to the beat, Muse Dash fuses note-matching with a lighthearted runner presentation that most rhythm games never attempt. Air and ground notes arrive in time with the music, enemies double as obstacles to dodge, and collecting them feeds the song rather than distracting from it. There is no story to speak of, and the absence is deliberate: the atmosphere is pure colour, with bosses that dance along to their own tracks and even the pummelled minions grinning through it. The tongue-in-cheek lewdness of some unlockable outfits stays exactly as serious as everything else on screen, which is to say not at all.

The soundtrack does the heavy lifting. Spread across the base game's 42 songs and the expansive DLC packs, the tracklist spans J-pop, electronic and vocal material of such consistency that nearly every song lands, a hit rate few rhythm games approach. Charts are authored to serve each song rather than a generic difficulty ladder, so even mid-tier tracks feel tailored. When the music is this strong, the two-button tapping underneath it barely registers as work; it simply becomes a way of playing along.

Fever multipliers reward uninterrupted combo chains, and the scoring layer extends into a small loadout system: playable characters and elfin companions trade score bonuses, healing and score penalties against each other. Bunny Girl Rin and Joker favour raw score, the dragon elfin earns its reputation as the best all-rounder, and Little Devil's HP drain makes her a punishment unto herself. Optimising these pairings gives score-chasers a layer of build variety that most note-highway games lack entirely, and personal-best hunting on the leaderboards sustains interest long after the song list is memorised.

The difficulty curve spikes hard from level 7 upward. Charts at level 8 and beyond on Master demand a precision that low-level play never prepares anyone for, and the Hard-to-Master jump in particular is brutal. The saving grace is that the chaos is the point: high-level charts throw so much oncoming pattern at the screen that failure still feels like spectacle, and the gap between passing and mastering a song gives every track a second life. It is a demanding ceiling behind a gentle floor, with little in between to ease the transition.

Value is the other headline. The base game costs about the price of a coffee, ships with those 42 songs, and remains entirely worthwhile on its own; the DLC packs cost many times more but roughly triple or quadruple the available content, a trade that is easy to recommend once the base game has hooked anyone. Unlockable illustrations and character outfits add a collectible layer on top, the kind of froth that should not matter and quietly does. A dedicated streaming mode builds playlists out of owned songs for continuous play.

Controls deserve one honest warning: the top-tier charts are built for keyboard, with rapid alternating two-key patterns that controllers handle poorly. The intended technique, hitting two keys in quick succession, is unintuitive enough that mashing a single key is a common adaptation, at the cost of numb fingers and a ceiling on fast passages. Switching from controller to keyboard is the single biggest improvement available, and hard songs are simply not designed around gamepads. It is the one area where the game's cross-platform ubiquity outruns its design.

Overall, this is a cheap, joyful and deceptively deep rhythm game whose soundtrack alone justifies the asking price, wrapped in a presentation that treats every chart as a whimsical stage rather than a static note highway. The character and elfin loadout variety, the leaderboard chase and the collectible layer keep it compelling well past the first dozen hours. Accept the difficulty spike and the keyboard requirement as the price of admission and it earns a permanent spot in the rotation.
