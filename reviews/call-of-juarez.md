A hunted drifter wrongly accused of murdering his mother and stepfather crosses paths with a gunslinging preacher convinced of his guilt, and the western revenge tale runs from both ends at once. Call of Juarez alternates Billy Candle's stealth-hunting chapters with Reverend Ray's guns-blazing ones, and the contrast is the game's defining strength: sneaking through brush with a bow and whip one chapter, blasting through town shootouts with dual revolvers and a recitable Bible the next. The irony tracking gives the story its spine, as Ray's certainty of delivering God's justice curdles into the realisation he has been an instrument of error. A spirit-guided interlude and a riddle-and-graves hunt for the Juárez gold flesh out the middle. The final Billy-versus-Juárez confrontation lands with genuine weight, and the closing declaration that the hunted boy is done running completes a satisfying arc.

The duel quick-draw system recurs at chapter climaxes, driven by mouse movement rather than buttons, and it works: the notorious launch-era input bug never materialised, with every draw registering cleanly. The three-bullet Juárez finale demands several attempts and earns its difficulty honestly. Between duels, Ray's chapters deliver the best gunplay, with concentration slow-mo, a Bible that can literally be wielded at enemies, and mine, train and fort set pieces that escalate well. Fistfights break the rhythm periodically. Dev wanted-poster easter eggs dot the levels, the kind of self-deprecating character most studios no longer bother with.

Whip and lasso traversal is the most persistent frustration, finicky and momentum-less on cliff climbs throughout. The eagle-feather climbing gauntlet and mine-cart dodging sections test patience in the same register. Stealth chapters fare better as design than execution, since the rabbit-hunting and stealth-hunting passages are genuinely the hardest part of the game, but the detection logic wobbles between unfair and blind: sometimes enemies spot an unseen figure with no explanation, sometimes they stare straight through one. Neither extreme builds trust in the system. Trial-and-error climbing compounds the problem on the Billy side.

Weapon durability forces constant scrounging, as guns rust and eventually explode, a novel mechanic that rewards swapping rather than hoarding. Health comes from bottle pickups rather than regeneration on the PC version, which sharpens every firefight until the pickups dry up: the train bridge chapter turns otherwise strong gunfights into a grind of near-death scrabbling because the bottles simply stop appearing. The difficulty curve is otherwise fair, and frequent deaths read as expected for a game of its vintage rather than as poor balancing. Quick-saving fully freezes the game when used, an oddity worth knowing about beforehand. Concentration mode still makes the mine and train ambushes the highlight of the run.

The PC port carries the age most visibly in its controls: no controller support, no hold-to-crouch, an odd key-to-zoom scheme, and inconsistent sprint behaviour that some characters and levels withhold entirely. The keyboard layout defies intuition in places, and movement never settles into comfort. Enemy body-twist dodge reactions make targets deliberately harder to hit, a clever-but-annoying series signature that reads as artificial difficulty here. Instant-death falls punish the climbing sections harshly given the whip's imprecision. None of it breaks the game, but all of it demands tolerance.

Story delivery remains the quiet achievement, told through alternating viewpoints with sermons, moral irony and a preacher who wipes out an entire household's men without hesitation while calling himself holy. The Calm Water interlude gives the pursuit a spiritual breather, and the wanted-poster easter eggs reward attention. Billy's arc from nobody to someone with a name carries genuine emotional payoff by the finale. The chapter structure stays varied across its roughly thirteen episodes, rarely repeating a set piece back to back. It is a script with more personality than its era's average shooter attempted.

Overall, a janky but genuinely distinctive western whose two-hero storytelling, mouse-driven duels and sermon-fuelled gunplay more than carry its age. The whip traversal, inconsistent detection and PC control quirks demand patience, yet the character and craft on display make the tolerance worthwhile.
