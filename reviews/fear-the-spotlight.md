Two students break into a shuttered school after dark with a spirit board and a plan, and what follows is a masterclass in dread built from almost nothing. The low-poly presentation, far from being nostalgia dressing, does genuine atmospheric work: distant silhouettes resolve into nothing, and featureless figures with two beady eyes vanish the instant they are noticed. Silence itself becomes a tool, with stretches stripped of ambience proving more unnerving than any score. This is horror earned through suggestion, glimpsed at the edge of the draw distance, and it outperforms most full-budget scares in the genre.

The dual-chapter structure is the sharpest design decision. The same séance night replays from the second student's perspective, each run armed with different abilities and haunted by different presences, and small scenes recontextualise what the other character experienced. Lockpicking opens routes that were barriers the first time through, and the second chapter trades school corridors for a house sequence haunted by a grief-shaped stalker. Replaying one night twice never feels repetitive, because the second pass is less a repeat than an excavation of what the first pass concealed.

Sunnyside High itself is the third character. The dungeon-like interconnected layout rewards curiosity, with incident reports and scattered notes stitching the 1991 fire and the school's closure into something pieced together rather than told. Locked-door tinkering, fuse boxes, power diverters and tarot-card matching give the exploration a tactile rhythm, while spirit-board rituals anchor the séance premise. The Spotlight-headed stalker telegraphs facing with a literal spotlight beam, turning hide-and-seek into a readable duel of angles, and his per-room spawning on noise keeps encounters tense without ever feeling unfair.

Generous checkpoints deserve specific credit: being caught costs progress rather than lives, which keeps the tension pointed at atmosphere instead of punishment. The stalker of the second chapter is scarier still, spawning per room on noise rather than roaming, and a smartphone-as-flashlight chapter wrings surprising unease from its narrow cone of light. Hide-and-seek sequences throughout remain tension-forward, and none of the scares overstay.

The retro puzzles occasionally work against the flow. Fuse-panel and frequency gates dressed with Omega and tilde dials, and a baffling "400" multiplier, complicate what are ultimately simple doors, and the fussy presentation demands more patience than the solutions merit. A control niggle persists across the whole game, with the sprint input ducking instead and never inviting a remap, which proves mildly comical during chase sequences. Neither issue derails the experience, but both interrupt momentum at moments built for dread.

Technical state is the real caveat. The PC build crashes every five to ten minutes beneath a permanent version watermark, and its graphics options are sparse even when it behaves, which pushes the Switch port from curiosity to necessity. The Switch version runs stable but spartan, and the compromise is visible. A two-person team shipping this much craft is remarkable; shipping it in a state this fragile is the cost of that scale.

Overall, this is a confident, atmospheric piece of 90s-styled survival horror whose suggestion-driven scares, dual-perspective structure and earnest character work outweigh its fussy puzzles and fragile PC build. The low-poly visuals genuinely amplify the horror rather than merely imitating it, and the interconnected school rewards every detour. The technical caveats sting, yet the craft underneath them stands untouched.
