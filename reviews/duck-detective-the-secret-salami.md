A freshly divorced, broke duck detective takes a cut-rate case at a rundown bus company, and the stolen lunch that opens the file unravels into salami smuggling across a border, blackmail, embezzlement and a kidnapping hidden in a luggage compartment. The escalation is the game's cleverest structural trick: what begins as the pettiest imaginable crime compounds into a genuinely sharp conspiracy with a frame-job at its centre, and the multi-party finale keeps raising stakes long after the theft premise has expired. Deadpan narration keeps the noir register intact throughout, from comforting wafts of cheap white toast to the detective's own grim self-assessment, and the comedy never undercuts the plotting underneath.

Voice acting is the engine of the whole production. The gruff duck narrator deadpans over absurd animal-office shenanigans, and every suspect arrives fully performed, which transforms a tidy script into something far funnier than text alone could carry. The cast earns individual affection: a true-crime-obsessed colleague, a sci-fi devotee, and a lovestruck penguin each get room to land their running gags, and naming every employee's species and job doubles as an early puzzle in itself, introducing the office one desk at a time.

Magnifying-glass clue hunting gives the investigation its visual gimmick. Sweeping the lens over characters and objects reveals hidden details, including webbed nubs gaining digits under close inspection, and the reveal animation stays charming across the full case. Clue words harvested from suspects and scenes feed deduction boards, and assembling the boards delivers the game's best moments, with the finale's confrontation scene playing the assembled evidence for maximum comedic payoff as the accused comes apart in spectacular fashion.

The bus-company setting does more work than its one-room premise suggests. The depot's timetable board, the luggage compartments and the border-crossing paperwork each feed directly into the case's later stages, so the environment teaches the conspiracy before the conspiracy announces itself. The office's employee roster doubles as a lesson in the game's comedy-of-species logic, and knowing who does what becomes genuinely useful when the deduction boards ask for it. Even the building's shabbiness is characterisation: a failing company cutting every corner is exactly where salami smuggling would take root. Few ninety-minute mysteries make their location this load-bearing.

The deduction gameplay is approachable but real. Detective mode offers hints on wrong answers, keeping the flow smooth for newcomers, and one late-game spike requires re-treading evidence across the office before the final board fills correctly. The finale asks for a culprit guess before confirmation, and a wrong call costs time without punishment beyond the retry, a light frustration in an otherwise smooth structure. An arrest judgement sequence closes the case, and a familiar landlady's cameo sets up the sequel with a sitcom flourish that lands just as well as the mystery it follows.

Re-examining evidence is the one real friction. Several deductions depend on details from items that can no longer be re-inspected once examined, forcing memory or guesswork where a review would resolve the question, and the limitation bites precisely during the late-game deduction spike. The full case wraps in barely ninety minutes, and the short runtime is the sole caveat on an otherwise complete package, leaving hunger for the next case rather than fatigue with this one, and a sequel queued for the same evening.

Overall, a tiny, fully voiced duck-noir gem punches well above its runtime, combining deadpan prose, a memorable cast and an impressively constructed conspiracy escalation into ninety minutes that feel dense rather than thin. Limited evidence re-examination and the culprit-guess finale are small costs, and the whole package lands as both funnier and more competent than its cosy-mystery competition.
