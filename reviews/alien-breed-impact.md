The origin of the reboot trilogy opens with the Leopold colliding with a ghost ship, the chief engineer crawling from the wreck to meet a synthetic lieutenant, and five levels of reactor shutdowns, medical rescues and turbine restarts standing between the survivor and the mainframe. The atmosphere here is the trilogy's purest: fire, gas and darkness without the apocalypse choreography the finale later added, and the corridors mint every trope the series would repeat (Intex vending tannoy, Attention-all-crew announcements, breaker detours). As a first chapter it establishes everything; as a standalone it is a complete, compact campaign.

Combat is the foundation the sequels built on, and it holds. The assault rifle's damage upgrade, the Ion Spike's oversized rounds, and a flamethrower that outperforms every expectation give the arsenal genuine character, and the upgrade economy via Intex shops rewards looting every corpse. One quirk the sequels removed deserves attention: a single mod per weapon, with buying a second erasing the first, makes build commitments permanent and forces early decisions to matter for the whole campaign.

The lore engine is fully formed from day one. Conrad's tragic marital history, the synthetic lieutenant's controversial manufacturing file, and a colleague's criminal past all arrive through logs that later volumes expand rather than invent, and the enemy ecology (healers, ranged quill-spitters, bony-shield holders, lava-burst larvae) gets readable files that explain why the trilogy's bestiary never needed rework. The connective tissue is all here; the sequels merely grow it.

Friction concentrates on the camera, the trilogy's worst implementation. Eight-direction cycling demands repeated presses to turn, and the minimap is chained to the camera's facing, which makes orientation genuinely difficult; the map refuses to rotate, rooms loop back on themselves, and a mid-game stretch of proper lostness is the trilogy's longest. These are the flaws the sequels quietly fixed, and they show their age most here.

Enemy variety is the roster's thin spot. Basic aliens and chargers carry most encounters, with screamers rare enough to be noteworthy, and the swarm spikes dump more enemies than the ammunition economy comfortably covers, occasionally exceeding what any loadout can clear. The finale undercuts itself further: the Moss Creature, the trilogy's first boss, dies fast to upgraded hardware, its head exploding almost before the fight begins. A scoring screen that grades completion time with a blunt Too long verdict adds accidental comedy to the close.

Sparse auto-saves make manual saving matter, and an achievement-bait item-free run collapses the moment a swarm arrives, a challenge only the disciplined should attempt. Human corpse labelling invites over-reading the fiction for synthetic hints that never arrive, and the pacing of level one, which alone runs nearly an hour, front-loads the campaign before the later levels tighten. The cliffhanger ending sets up the sequel honestly, with the ships beginning their fall as the credits approach. None of the rough edges obscures the design underneath, which is more complete than most first entries manage.

Overall, this is a satisfying foundation best experienced first, in trilogy order as intended: the combat feel, shop economy and log-driven lore were fully formed from the start, and the camera and map flaws the sequels corrected read here as history rather than innovation. Play it after its successors and the downgrade is obvious; play it as the origin it is, and the tropes minted in these corridors repay the whole trilogy. As a compact four-hour campaign at veteran difficulty it neither outstays its welcome nor oversells its ambition.
