Sisyphus rolls his boulder not as punishment but as weaponry, down hills guarded by cows, elephants and explosives, against Hades, Leonidas, Justinian, Vlad the Impaler, Charlemagne, a war pope and Napoleon in turn. Each era stages its boss as a giant animated painting with a weak spot to exploit, and cutscenes lean fully into absurdist Monty Python-style humour. The story exists purely as a vehicle for gags, and the tone commits to that without apology.

The hybrid structure is the design's novelty. Gold buys defences, cows, elephants, mammoths, bombs, TNT, towers and fans, planted along the opponent's downhill route, before rolling the boulder down the enemy side to smash their gate first. Both sides race and sabotage simultaneously, and the resulting push-and-shove produces genuinely close finishes where reaching the gate first snowballs into a building advantage that usually decides the level.

The mammoth defensive unit anchors the winning strategy. Placed near jump-landing spots, it damages the enemy boulder and knocks it off course with a reliability no other obstacle matches, and once discovered it becomes the backbone of every subsequent plan. Learning to front-load it near landing zones transforms the mid-game from survival into deliberate control of the opponent's run.

Boss fights interrupt the racing flow as weak-point duels staged inside famous artworks, and the range of references runs surprisingly wide, taking in even a shooter homage from the era of demons. Painter-based spirits of the age escalate steadily toward a final Saturn encounter whose platforming reads as awkward until attack baiting becomes clear, after which the fight settles into something manageable and satisfying.

Course layouts split between clever and confounding. Some stages route beautifully around the mechanics while others turn opaque, sending the boulder on scenic detours with little indication of the intended line. The witch's-Sabbath wind levels expose the sharpest imbalance: fan traps blow the player's boulder off the edge while apparently ignoring the enemy AI entirely, an asymmetry that reads as unfair rather than challenging.

The Porto water stage is the campaign's low point. Awkward routing combines with a water-damage gimmick and padded length to produce the single most miserable stretch, and the stage stands out precisely because the surrounding campaign keeps its obstacles fair. Controller handling compounds the trouble on tight courses: precision jumps feel awkward, braking is inadequate, and slowdown creeps in when the boulder needs fine control most.

Keys gate one stage and unlock cosmetic boulders and playable characters, though their purpose is signposted poorly enough to cause early confusion, and falling off the course carries no penalty, which flattens the stakes of the platforming side entirely. Overall, the genre mash-up, art-history comedy and mammoth-led strategy deliver something genuinely unlike anything else, and the imbalance, one hated stage and imprecise handling are costs worth paying for a one-of-a-kind concept executed with real personality.
