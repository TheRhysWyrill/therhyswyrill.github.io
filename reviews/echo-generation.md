Maple Town, 1983: siblings and their animal companions work through a summer of missing fathers, cornfield aliens, serial-killer scaremongering and small-town oddballs, a coming-of-age sci-fi pastiche in the Earthbound-meets-Stranger-Things register. The vignette comedy carries the tone: a doll-collecting would-be world conqueror, government agents with no weapons, conspiracy cops, a frog quiz and a principal with a very concerning basement. VHS culture, bubble-tea shops and Highway 31 period dressing give it a flavour nothing else on the shelf quite matches, and the atmosphere stays whimsical even as the plot sharpens.

The timed combat is the core identity, borrowed openly from Paper Mario: action commands for strikes and blocks, per-battle SP, status effects and a level-up choice between health, strength and skill points. The growth arc is the satisfaction engine, from hitting for three damage early to one-shotting trash with specials late, a curve the player actively tracks and enjoys climbing. Learning that SP refills between battles is a quiet systems breakthrough that changes how everything is played, and the combat stays engaging from the raccoon-grind opening to the two-attempt final squid.

The voxel-and-pixel hybrid presentation is a genuine studio signature, identifiable on sight by anyone who has played the developer's earlier work, and the music earns unprompted praise throughout, particularly the spaceship VIP-lounge and boombox tracks. Recruited companions, a cat, a robot, a dog, an alien crew and a jackalope, each bring comic-book-granted skill attacks, and the enemy roster runs from rats and raccoons to werewolves and a junkyard Mech.

Friction concentrates in systems opacity: the enemy respawn rules, some sleep-gated, some timed, some permanent, are never explained and complicate XP grinding plans throughout. Companions all join at level 1 with tiny health pools, a design choice that benches most of them and over-relies on the cat, and the timed-attack minigames swing between too forgiving and too erratic, with the flying puck's random trajectory a persistent annoyance. Dying respawns the party at half health in the area rather than the health they entered with, a rule that nearly soft-locks the junkyard attempt and reads as plain punitive.

The early game is brutally front-loaded: three damage per hit against enemies that hit hard, raccoons as the only viable grind, and later levels demanding hundreds of XP for minimal gain. The junkyard Mech boss is a genuine wall, killing outright on a first attempt with half the party dead, and a mid-game werewolf reminds the game can still bite. A school jump scare startles despite the light tone, and the poison status effect ticks for a single point of damage, comically useless in practice.

Roughly six hours cover the whole story in a single sitting, an under-ten-hour RPG that values personality over systemic depth, and the late-game reveal about the missing father's five-year absence gives the finale genuine sci-fi weight with a time mismatch and a prisoner-ship reveal that land well. The final fight takes two attempts and the credits roll on a game that never overstays. Item-fetch quests for bubble-tea flavours, dolls, bus passes and generator fuel blend with light environmental puzzles built on typewriter symbols, C4 and robot lasers, and the FST portal-test lore threads the missing-father mystery into the cornfield conspiracies without overreaching. The film-within-a-game about an alien skin-eater is exactly the kind of gag the vignette structure exists for.

Overall, a short, charming Paper Mario-flavoured homage whose timed combat, studio-signature voxel-pixel art and comedy writing trade systemic depth for personality and mostly win the trade, with opaque respawn rules, level-1 companions and a punishing early grind as the honest costs. An under-ten-hour RPG is a rare and welcome thing, and this one earns its brevity with genuine warmth.
