Halo's sandbox translated into top-down twin-stick form is a stranger experiment than it sounds, and this game commits to it fully. Six operations of roughly thirty missions alternate between two Spartans: one on the moon X50, the other on Drathus 5 during the invasion. Each mission runs three to five minutes, framed by briefings narrated as UNSC historical records, and the loop is pure twin-stick discipline: move with one stick, shoot with the other, grenades on trigger. The result is a bite-sized campaign that stays consistently fun from start to finish and leaves the impression of a concept deserving more than one outing.

The Covenant arsenal adapts surprisingly well to the perspective shift, an area where the genre experiment could easily have thinned out. The plasma pistol, needler, shotgun and energy sword all transfer into top-down play with readable silhouettes and distinct behaviours, and energy sword Elites plus Grunt suicide units give encounters recognisable pressure. Vehicle hijacking is the standout: Wraiths, Ghosts and Scorpions can be seized instantly and turned on their owners, and tank escort interludes add a slower, heavier rhythm between firefights. Instantly killing a hijacked vehicle's occupants never stops being satisfying.

Defence missions are the highlight of the mission variety. Holding a position while waves pour in doubles as an efficient medal and score farm, and turret segments give those stands a fixed vantage. The XP shop deepens the loop: medals and time bonuses fund score boosters, damage upgrades, sentry drones, seeker missiles and camo, and by the finale the accumulated boosts meaningfully change how the Spartan plays. Bronze, silver and gold score medals gate a light replay incentive, though a single clean pass through each mission is a complete experience in itself.

XP Shop investment exposes one of the design's rougher edges: boosters carry no persistence and must be re-selected before every mission, a small tax repeated dozens of times across the campaign. Aiming is the larger thorn. Without auto-lock, tracking enemies from the top-down view demands constant correction, and the problem worsens on turrets and vehicles where the reticle drifts across Covenant lines. One late defend mission fails outright from a mistimed X press near a turret, an input ambiguity that costs a mission rather than a life.

Operation Frostbite closes its arc with structure, and the sixth operation that follows feels bolted on. It ends abruptly and without resolution, a noticeably weaker note than the Frostbite finale, giving the impression of a chapter appended late. The Forerunner reveal inside it does shift the mission flow from exploration to extraction, a genuine change of pace, but the ending itself lacks the closure the earlier operation earned.

Presentation holds the format together. The top-down rendering keeps Halo's visual language legible at distance, briefings carry the military-record tone convincingly, and the short mission structure makes the game trivially easy to pick up and put down. Nothing about the presentation dazzles, but nothing obstructs either; the frame serves the combat rather than competing with it.

Overall, this is a pleasant genre detour that translates its source sandbox into twin-stick form with genuine care, carried by hijackable vehicles, an XP shop that rewards medal chasing and defence missions that double as score farms. Imprecise aiming, non-persistent boosters and an abrupt final operation are real flaws, but they sit on top of a consistently fun core rather than undermining it.
