Sevastopol is a corporate ghost town mid-decomposition: a Seegson decommission, a collapsed economy, a black market and an AI called Apollo that has decided the humans are the contamination. Amanda Ripley's search for her mother via the Nostromo flight recorder gives the decay a personal stake, and the horror register is tension rather than fright, a game that keeps the nerves on edge for fifteen hours without relying on shock. The atmosphere is 1979 rendered in engine: CRT green, smoke, analogue machinery and a score that ratchets precisely when it should. It is a love letter to the source films that earns the phrase.

The stalker AI is the entire identity, and it remains the genre's benchmark. Vent-drool tells, sightline logic, a persistent memory of habits learned, and encounters where survival feels earned rather than granted: the director system lets one monster hunt for the whole campaign without becoming fair-fight furniture. Every player builds a relationship with the enemy rather than a route-memorisation exercise, and the improbable survivals it produces become the stories told afterwards. Nothing else of its era gives an antagonist this intelligence, and nothing since has surpassed it.

The Working Joes deserve their own iconography. The blank face, the unmoving mouth, the polite menace and the corporate devotion render them more unsettling than any number of monsters, and their Seegson lore is read in full because the worldbuilding rewards it. Threats rotate with distinct grammars (humans, androids, the alien, late-game duplicates), and firearms exist purely for humans, a futility baked in that teaches the correct hierarchy of fear. Motion tracker pings warn and betray in equal measure, and rewiring stations shuffle power between doors, cameras and air purification to direct the chaos.

The flamethrower is the balance's one concession. It trivialises encounters when used, one blast scattering the alien, and its scarcity reshapes play into deliberate hoarding of a resource too valuable to spend. Whether it is a godsend or a design flaw is genuinely ambiguous, and the ambivalence is the point: the game knows its monster is unbeatable and hands over one answer at a price. The scripting problem is less defensible. The medbay ping-pong patrol, where the alien bounces between two rooms and refuses the vents, cuts against the systemic freedom that makes everything else sing, and scripted sequences are the only moments that betray the game's own rules.

The final gauntlet is the worst decision in the package. No save points, constant environmental fire damage and a three-second autosave margin combine into legitimate torture at the exact moment goodwill should be peaking. One genuinely inexplicable death, killed at the requisition-android puzzle with no footsteps or warning, stands out precisely because the game is otherwise scrupulously fair. The DLC value proposition is thin even at sale prices, with roughly twenty minutes of content per story pack, though playing the original crew is enjoyable enough to justify the outlay at a discount.

The worldbuilding hangs together completely, which is rarer than it should be. Seegson corporate history, decommission notices, Julia Jones' Colonial Times report and the Ransom/Lingard corruption thread explain the station's decay organically, and reading everything is rewarded rather than indulgent. Late-game tool unlocks (plasma torch, ion torch, gas mask) quietly reopen earlier areas, and the station looping back on itself gives the campaign a sense of place. Crafting splits cleanly between medkits, noisemakers and the alien-frightening arsenal.

Production values border on the cinematic, with cutscenes that play as film rather than interstitial, and the lineage it seeded is visible in a decade of successors whose stealth systems owe it explicit debts. Minor control friction (intermittent stick refusals in menus, a D-pad-only map, a wobbly spacewalk camera) draws grumbles without heat. The one-hit-kill enemy demands patience, but the fairness of the system makes every death legible, with a single exception.

Overall, this is a masterwork of systemic horror whose stalker AI, period-faithful worldbuilding and sustained tension hold up completely, betrayed only by rare scripted AI stretches, a no-save finale gauntlet and thin DLC value. The rare scripted shortcuts are the only thing that betrays its own rules, and everything else remains the standard by which the genre's hide-and-seek horror is measured.
