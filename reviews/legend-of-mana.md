Legend of Mana makes the player an urban-planner god, rebuilding a mythic world one artifact at a time: place an item on the map, watch a land bloom, and explore whatever story it carries. There is no single plot, only a mosaic of self-contained events, small folk-tale romances, tragedies and comedies populated by recurring characters. Tone swings from slapstick to surprisingly sombre, sometimes within the same hour: conspiracy-brained cults and puffing-penguin pirates share space with a Jumi knight's self-loathing and a kidnapped mailboy's melancholy. The structure treats its quests as anthology episodes rather than chapters, and no other RPG has copied the layout since. It is a very special game, and kind of one of a kind in how it is laid out.

The soundtrack deserves its reputation, carried by Yoko Shimomura's compositions from the opening moments to the final credits. Boss themes rank among the best the era produced, and the intro song alone justifies the remaster's existence. The pixel art matches it: this is arguably one of the most beautiful games of the PS1 generation, and the retouched location backgrounds glow on modern hardware. Character and enemy sprites were left untouched, though, creating a mild mismatch between polished backdrops and original foreground art. The visual presentation otherwise earns every bit of its praise.

The event system is both the fascination and the frustration. Events trigger on opaque conditions, some are timed, some can be failed outright, and land-shape gating means certain artifacts must be placed in specific spots to reach everything at all. Playing without a guide risks missing content permanently, and the timed, failable fairy event can send an unknowing participant wandering the wrong area for forty minutes. A starting tile chosen poorly can quietly lock progress. The vision is admirable; the communication of that vision is not.

The Cactus diary companion becomes a genuine delight, a sentient houseplant whose diary entries grow steadily sassier as events accumulate. Checking in after every event turns the hub into a ritual, and the companion's commentary is some of the game's best writing. The systems around the hub work as optional toys rather than mandatory loops: a farming Orchard, a monster-catching Corral, weapon, armour and instrument forging from materials, and a Golem-crafting lab worth admiring even unused. The Corral's monster catching is iffy in practice, and the Orchard is modest. Everything is voluntary, which is the design's quiet strength.

Combat is the legacy weak point. Attacks only strike left or right, hit registration is unreliable at the best of times, and enemies can stunlock the protagonist into helplessness. Boss fights late in the game spam special attacks that render them briefly invincible, a cheap-feeling pattern that punishes patience rather than skill. The wide ability pool unlocked later softens the grind, and the SP-based special moves add variety once available. Still, landing a clean hit remains a royal pain throughout, and the combat never escapes its PS1-era stiffness.

The remaster adds a save-anywhere feature that replaces proper save states and gets used compulsively, a quality-of-life change with real consequences for how freely events can be attempted. No way to exit areas quickly makes the multi-area wisdom-tour event a slog, an odd gap for a game built around hopping between lands. The ~67-event structure stretches across roughly twenty hours at a comfortable pace, and the final Jumi arc lands as the emotional peak the anthology format earns. Switch handles the game well where the PC version's Linux compatibility fails.

Overall, an anthology RPG whose artifact-based world-building and Shimomura soundtrack remain genuinely one of a kind, with each placed tile generating its own short story. The obtuse event triggers, two-directional combat and land-shape gating demand a guide's help, yet the mosaic format, the sassy Cactus diaries and the music make the friction worthwhile.
