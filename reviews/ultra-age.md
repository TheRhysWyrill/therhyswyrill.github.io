Ultra Age casts a clone agent with days to live into a post-apocalyptic chase for a Frontier vessel, trailed by a turncoat companion and padded out with revelations about time travel and clone harvesting. The seven-day lifespan countdown frames the whole campaign, ticking in the background while corridors and mazes unfold. Atmosphere sits firmly in serviceable B-movie science fiction: dialogue functions as connective tissue, and the delivery never rises above the material. What the game lacks in narrative polish it returns in mechanical ideas, and the combat system is where the money went. It is a linear character-action brawler that runs roughly four to five hours to credits, and its best ideas arrive early.

Weapon durability drives everything. Blades degrade as they connect, and the four-weapon loadout (steel blade, katana, claymore, lightning blade, with gun and charge variants in reserve) switches on the fly, each class carrying unique attacks. Switching is not cosmetic: enemy effectiveness varies by weapon, and the arsenal rewards building around all four rather than settling on a favourite. Combat has depth that the early hours hide, and the system keeps paying out until the final encounter.

The break blade attack is the twist that elevates the loop. Deliberately shattering a weapon deals a large damage spike and grants invincibility frames, turning durability from a resource tax into an offensive panic button. That combination carried the final boss, where the i-frames matter more than the damage. No comparable action game merges durability and offence quite this way, and the design earns its identity from the merge. Managing the break economy across four blades becomes the real skill ceiling.

Enemy-name colours telegraph weapon effectiveness at a glance, a smart system the game never teaches, which means the legibility only clicks deep into the campaign. Death-rewind healing compounds the confusion early: dying restores full health, and crystal-energy healing goes unexplained until later, so the opening hours read as punishing before they read as generous. Time-shift respawning lets day and night cycles repopulate crystals, keeping supplies flowing. Progression is flush, with modules and power-gear upgrades routinely maxed by the end.

Structure leans on repetition. Corridor-plus-maze levels hinge on frequent key-card fetches and backtracking, and the desert stretch compounds the tedium with wide open running space and little content. The padding is structural rather than occasional, and it dilutes an otherwise tight runtime. Level design works best in the underground facility, where the maze layouts serve the combat instead of interrupting it.

The pre-finale gauntlet is the campaign's lowest point. Multi-phase boss fights arrive with no checkpoints between phases, so a late-phase mistake resets the whole sequence, and the pacing turns exhausting rather than climactic. The camera struggles in crowds, and movement controls misfire repeatedly: arc turns swing wide, ledge grabs refuse to catch, and sprint rides the left stick in awkward company. None of these are fatal alone, but stacked on the gauntlet they wear the goodwill the combat earns.

Technical problems close the story. On Linux, an unskippable broken cutscene soft-locks the game roughly ten minutes before the credits, and finishing requires routing around it on a Windows drive. For a game that otherwise runs well, a hard stop that late is a kick in the teeth, and it colours the closing stretch regardless of how good the final boss is. The bug is known, specific and reproducible, which at least makes it routable.

Overall, Ultra Age is a blind-pick surprise whose durability-break combat loop and generous upgrade systems deserved a better-polished frame. Fetch-gate structure, uncheckpointed boss phases and the Linux soft-lock all drag at the margins, yet the mechanical core holds. Judge it as a B-movie brawler with one genuinely distinct idea and it lands.
