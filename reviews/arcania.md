A pastoral-revenge frame opens the story: a shepherd, a murdered village, paladins to hunt, before the plot swerves into faction politics of succession intrigue, mage orders and a city resistance, and finally a cosmic-scale ending that arrives incoherent and abrupt. The writing is consistently the worst part, disjointed and awkward in a prologue that only improves afterwards, and the tone whiplash between revenge thriller and war epic never fully resolves. None of that stops the faction quest chains from engaging throughout, and the sheer contrarian pleasure of defending a game this maligned is its own reward: the hate it absorbed says more about a marketing campaign that sold it as a franchise heir than about the product itself.

The treasure hunts are the game's real spine: five ecosystems of statues, ancient relics, skulls, graves and rune shards turn a roughly twenty-hour run into a completionist audit, with counts checked at every area's end and the totals never once drifting. Crafting orbits the rewards, and the hunt culminates in the Enos's-blessing helmet, a payoff so dominant it makes the final stretch feel godlike and turns the last hours into a victory lap. The collectible layer outlasts the story entirely, and the long-game structure gives every detour purpose, from monastery cellars to grave rows to the statue-cliff paths where the shard hunts live.

The three-route fortress infiltration is a design highlight: melee, ranged and magic-flavoured entry plans offered simultaneously, with nothing route-exclusive, so all three can be sampled in a single assault. Dual-outcome quests split the same mission into two resolutions and cannot be failed, removing the genre's usual anxiety, and the misfiring quest trigger that occasionally breaks one of them is the exception that proves the rule. Zone-by-zone with a linear gate structure, the world reads as technically open, and the dungeon craft of the old mine and the monastery stands out explicitly.

Combat is fun-if-shallow: lock-on, flurries, might-blows and two-handed fire maces carry the loop, with difficulty mild throughout, broken only by a shadow beast, a troll and the finale's spawn waves. Butterfly-marked foraging, no encumbrance and lootable kitchens give the world a lived-in, rainy-England commodity detail that grounds the whole thing, and the day-night cycle and rolling storms give even a provisioning errand some weather to walk through. The XP curve balloons to five digits as enemies keep pace, an economy worth auditing but never a problem, and crafting around treasure rewards keeps every hunt's payout mechanical rather than purely cosmetic.

Friction accumulates in the details. Indoor movement slows to a crawl for no reason, the most-repeated complaint across the whole run. Teleporters connect to each other rather than to a picked destination, stranding travel plans repeatedly, and a rune substitutes for a real sprint. The magic system is shallow, three bolts and runes with no build identity, a mage playthrough would be sorely disappointing. Enemies block indefinitely, some undead cannot be looted, and character models reuse faces so often it becomes a running joke.

An ancient-relic registration bug permanently spoils one treasure quest at the Thorniara gate, verified and unfixable, though the lost reward stings less than it might. The port needs tinkering on Linux: a controller-UI cheat menu re-enabled at every boot, a green-face fix, and a cutscene patch that rescues the finale's opening, a tinkering session whose eventual triumph earns its own satisfaction. The finale itself is a reskin boss, spawn-heavy and beaten by stun-locking, followed by an ending that cuts off mid-breath. The Zyra quest trigger, misfiring in its own corner of the world, rounds out a bug list that is short but memorable.

Overall, a flawed, shallow Action, RPG that never pretended to be anything more, delivering exactly the mindless fun it promised: five collectible ecosystems, un-failable quests, a satisfying power curve and a world worth poking at. Awkward writing, an incoherent ending, indoor slowdown and port jank are real costs, and a mismatched marketing history explains most of the hate this one absorbed.
