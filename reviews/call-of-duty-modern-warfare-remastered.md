Few campaigns carry a reputation as heavy as this one, and the remaster's first gift is proving the reputation deserved. A two-front narrative shuttles between an SAS unit hunting a Russian ultranationalist through the Middle East and a USMC invasion of a fictional Gulf state, and the beats still land: the Al-Fulani execution scene, the nuclear detonation that rewrites the story's stakes, and a closing news report that recontextualises everything. The tone is punchy and self-assured, the soap-opera character dynamics give the missions personality, and the pacing alternates fronts without letting either go stale. It codified the modern military shooter, and playing it now reveals how much of the genre's vocabulary was written here.

The visual overhaul is the other headline. The detail jump over the 2007 original is startling given only eight years separate them: night-and-day texture work, volumetric smoke, laser-sight night raids glowing through fog, and set dressing that makes Chernobyl's ghost town and the sinking cargo ship feel newly tangible. This was always a blockbuster campaign; the remaster is the first version that looks like one, and the upgrade repeatedly rewards attention to corners the original engine could not render.

All Ghillied Up is the campaign's high point, and it holds. A flashback to Pripyat pairs a young lieutenant with his captain for a stealth crawl through enemy patrols in the exclusion zone, and the design holds tension in ways the series rarely attempts: crawling through grass as tanks roll past, timing shots to thunder, and resisting the temptation of a single, optional long-range kill whose consequences ripple forward into One Shot, One Kill. The paired escape across the collapsing town remains one of the best-constructed sequences in the franchise.

The AC-130 gunship mission is the other signature, and it is spectacular if fiddly. Thermal imaging flattens the battlefield into silhouettes while the crew banters, and seconds of sustained fire level compounds in a way no ground-level perspective could deliver. Elsewhere, variety stays high: tank defence on the highway, checkpoint holds, the cargo ship boarding, and the blackout urban fight. The mission roster rarely repeats itself, which is precisely why the set pieces hit as hard as they do.

Friction concentrates on the squadmates. Companions constantly shove the player around in close-quarters corridors, block doorways, and absorb bullets meant for their target, and the problem recurs across nearly every mission with allies. Friendly-fire mission failures follow, some from tight squad AI clustering into grenade radii, some from stray shots in the chaos. Enemy behaviour quirks compound the impression: allies missing point-blank shots, enemies surviving absurd punishment, and strays ambushing from angles the scripting does not telegraph.

One Shot, One Kill's ending deserves its own accounting. Carrying a wounded captain to the extraction while swarmed on all sides, with a full mission replay on failure, tests patience in ways the preceding stealth did not, and a seconds-too-slow attempt that ends the gauntlet is a bitter note on which to close an otherwise superb pairing. Generous ammunition drops (a hundred sniper rounds, endless grenades) occasionally break immersion, and the Mile High Club epilogue confuses more than it thrills. None of it undoes the campaign; the extraction gauntlet simply sits in memory alongside the ghillie crawl rather than behind it.

Overall, the classic genuinely deserves its reputation, warts and all, and this remaster is the right way to experience it: the Pripyat stealth pairing, the AC-130 and the nuclear beat remain genre-defining, and the visual overhaul finally presents the campaign as the blockbuster it always was. Obstructive squadmate AI, friendly-fire failures and a punishing extraction finale are real irritants, but the mission craft underneath them has aged better than almost anything else in the franchise.
