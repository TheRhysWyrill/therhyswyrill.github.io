A masked ball runs on a schedule of murder. Every day at midnight the mansion's guests die at the hands of the staff, and the task is to learn the whole routine well enough to break it, guest by guest. The premise fuses Majora's Mask's loops with Clue-style whodunnit dressing, scored to a baroque-jazz soundtrack and staged with chibi charm, and the fusion is genuinely unlike anything else in the genre.

The mansion operates as a living timetable. Deaths and sounds foreshadow events before they are understood: a gunshot at four, shattering glass at five, and every hour of the day carries its own choreography. Learning each guest's daily circuit becomes the real puzzle, and hearing the tragedy approaching through walls gives the loop a running undercurrent of dread. Knowledge is the only resource, and the game rewards keeping a mental map of who will be where, and when.

Interventions form the core loop. Invitations gathered from corpses and clues read from the environment feed into engineering each rescue before midnight, and a failed day resets to the ball's opening with everything re-runnable. Guests saved grant their masks as abilities, and the roster is delightfully strange: clock control, super-hearing, a glass-shattering voice, X-ray sight, lockpicking, architectural memory and mirror fast-travel. Progression arrives as capability, and each new mask unlocks previously unreachable corners of both the mansion and the schedule.

Collectibles pad the loop generously. Fifty-two playing cards, guest dossiers and location lore scatter through the day, and full completion plus a secret ending fits comfortably into a handful of hours. Difficulty stays gentle throughout, closer to scheduling chess than action, and the pacing respects that: no combat interrupts the routine, and the threat is always the clock rather than a health bar.

The loop's meaninglessness is its sharpest structural question. Averted deaths vanish at midnight with everything else, and comparisons with Majora's Mask throw the difference into relief: there the loops accumulated weight, here each reset erases it. The game's answer arrives late, and it recontextualises the whole mansion in a way that makes the erasure deliberate rather than careless. Even on a repeat visit, that landing carries genuine emotional weight.

Presentation carries the morbid material into charm. Chibi character animations, jaunty arrangements and sharp comic writing keep the murders light without trivialising them, and the title-screen music alone justifies the soundtrack's reputation. The tone walks a narrow line between macabre and endearing, and it never slips to either side. Character work deserves particular credit: each guest and staff member reads as a distinct silhouette, both visually and behaviourally.

Technical stability on PC deserves a caveat. Via Proton, attempts can crash within minutes, and only a custom Proton build rather than the default provides a stable session. Day resets also punish forgotten save clocks, and losing an hour of schedule knowledge to a missed save stings more than any puzzle. Neither fault sits with the design itself, but both shape the practical experience of playing it.

Overall, this remains a scheduling-puzzle gem whose time-loop structure, baroque-jazz score and chibi presentation deliver completely, and whose final act reframes every reset with real emotional force. The loop's erasures, the Proton fragility and the occasional lost save are small prices for a structure this clever, and the mansion's daily routine rewards every hour spent learning it.
