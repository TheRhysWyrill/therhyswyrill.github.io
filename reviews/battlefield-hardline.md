A Miami crime drama following a detective through a drug war, corruption and a three-year time skip into fugitive revenge, told across ten episodes with previously-on recaps like a premium television show. The writing and voice acting carry a genuinely show-like quality, side characters land as memorably awkward or absurd, and an anime-waffle money-laundering subplot pushes the tone into self-aware corniness that works in its favour. Every character betrays the protagonist eventually, an arc that turns sardonic by the end, but the twists, one predicted from episode two and one genuinely not, keep the procedural engine turning.

The cops-and-criminals premise is the breath of fresh air: after four war-game entries, scanning for evidence, arresting for bonuses, badge-freezing and shell-casing distractions make this a genuinely different genre wearing a familiar shell. The stealth system, built on awareness cones in the Far Cry 3 mould, is serviceable and defensible against the criticism it received, with the choice to go loud always available. Case-file hunting becomes a self-imposed meta-goal, unlocking weapons and gadgets, and stretches a six-to-seven-hour campaign past the seven-hour mark for completionists.

Enemy design is a welcome contrast to the previous entry: fast time-to-kill on both sides with no spongey damage sponging, making firefights snappy and fair. Car handling earns repeated praise, buttery-smooth across the whole roster, and the episode variety is remarkable: swamp airboat hunts, a prison-bus escape played as the criminal, a mansion party infiltration, an abandoned-car flee, an airport tank duel and a skyscraper assault via water tank. Few campaigns of any stripe pack this much episodic range into seven hours.

Friction concentrates in the takedown and freeze systems, the single biggest mechanical complaint: melee takedowns sometimes simply fail to execute, and the freeze zeroes the wrong target, catching the man behind rather than the man in front. AI companions get spotted and blow stealth, bodies vanish, and bullets clip through fences, jank that occasionally undermines the otherwise solid stealth loops. The evidence-distance meter behaves erratically, an alligator quick-time event ships with no visible prompts at all, and arrest animations feel sluggish next to the fast combat.

Low level caps arrive early at fifteen, cutscenes cannot be skipped for evidence clean-up replays, one objective is missable behind a scripted ambush, and a single persistent lens flare survives from the previous entry's excess. None of these breaks the campaign, but together they mark the polish gaps of a spin-off developed outside the main studio. The prologue's corny register, at least, is self-aware rather than accidental, and the scanner-and-warrant framework gives each episode a procedural rhythm that the betrayal plot plays against surprisingly well. Shell-casing distractions reward patient observation, and the previously-on recaps, however gimmicky, genuinely help a campaign revisited across multiple sittings.

The Miami setting gives the whole thing a distinct identity: sun-bleached crime procedural rather than muddy trench warfare, with the episodic TV pacing, cliffhangers included, making the campaign feel like a season of television. The finale's offer reframes the entire corruption arc, and the ending lands with genuine weight for a game this frequently dismissed.

Overall, a corny, confident cops-and-criminals spin-off whose TV-cop-show writing, Far Cry-style stealth, case-file hunting and remarkable episode variety outweigh unreliable takedowns, missing UI prompts and scattered AI jank. It is the most distinctive entry in its series, a police procedural wearing a wargame's uniform, and the change from the norm is precisely the point.
