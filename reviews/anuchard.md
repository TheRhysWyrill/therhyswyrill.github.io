A cosy action-RPG about a Bell wielder reviving petrified villagers and restoring a fallen floating utopia, whose twee surface hides something far darker and cleverer: a class divide over whose statues get preserved, a city lab's Ascension programme, a dungeon-as-death-cult structure and a meta finale that interrogates the genre's own chosen-one mythology. The tonal swing from warm village life to body-recycling dystopia is a genuinely rare feat for a ten-hour indie, and the ending presents a philosophical fork between eternity and freedom that rewards actual thought rather than reflex.

The control identity is deliberately odd and eventually brilliant: heavy attack and dodge share a button, so damaging enemies means dashing into them. What starts as the biggest early annoyance becomes the combat's signature, a positional system where approach angles matter and the dodge doubles as the attack. It is two buttons and a gimmick, but the gimmick is mastered rather than tolerated, and basic encounters stay fun across the whole run because the positional identity keeps them fresh.

The music is excellent across every area and every dungeon, consistently one of the best elements from start to finish. The Memento revival ritual structures each chapter, find a memento, delve the dungeon, strike the bell, perform the ritual, and makes every dungeon a rescue mission rather than a loot run. Five Guardians across five dungeon regions pace the campaign, and the village cast, from a farmer's cabbage to a hen to a twins' statue project, gives the side quests small-scale warmth. The bookshelf memento collection doubles as a soft achievements system, each completed quest adding a token to the room.

Progression runs on two-ingredient cooking buffs, material-driven village upgrades and per-dungeon collectibles including materials, obelisks and hidden spirits. The cooking system invites experimentation but the two-ingredient cap keeps combinations limited, a mixed blessing that constrains more than it frustrates. Roughly ten hours cover completion plus 100% material hunts.

Friction accumulates in the details. No map means navigation relies on memory and landmarks, and one infamous wall in the gold dungeon is visible but unreachable with no legible route, the worst navigation offender in the game. The upgrade economy punishes a single missed material with a full dungeon replay, a completionism tax that grows old by the second occurrence. Movement speed offers no sprint, and the dodge does nothing for traversal, a small but persistent wish across the whole run.

One-way trip hazards and portal ambiguity in the late dungeons occasionally dump the player where they did not intend to be, and the final Guardian fight lands anticlimactically brief after the buildup. Neither issue undoes the campaign, but both read as missed opportunities in an otherwise confident structure. The village's small size, at least, keeps side-quest navigation painless by design, and the puppet-show intro sets the tone with a wit the writing sustains throughout. The fishing-Net letter quest deserves particular mention as a small quest that lands an emotional beat larger games would envy, and the Gold Pyramid's animal cast broadens the world's character roster in ways the early hours never advertise. Small touches like these give the ten hours a texture most compact adventures never manage.

Overall, a compact action-RPG whose bell-wielding rescue loop, positional dodge-attack combat and excellent soundtrack carry a story that turns far darker and more meta than its pastel art suggests. No-map navigation, no sprint and a completionism economy that charges full dungeon replays for missed materials are the honest costs, and the ten-hour span punches well above its weight in writing.
