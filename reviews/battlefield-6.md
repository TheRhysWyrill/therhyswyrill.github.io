A near-future confrontation between NATO and the PAX Armada unfolds across nine missions, framed as an interrogation that unspools the story almost entirely in flashbacks. A political-conspiracy layer runs beneath the firefights, with CIA involvement, tangled personal histories and a presidential-rescue set piece, and the campaign closes on a deliberate cliffhanger that points at unfinished business. The writing leans on recognisable genre beats, the foreshadowing is heavy enough that most twists telegraph themselves well in advance, and the darker missions, a Cairo night raid and a dam sequence, give the drama weight the genre usually skims.

Destructibility is the identity. Levelling buildings, rocketing a structure and watching it collapse into rubble, remains the through-line from the series' high-water marks, and Gibraltar supplies the best demolition playground of the campaign. The visual presentation keeps pace, with the locales reading as genuinely pretty under fire and smoke, and the spectacle of mass collapse never wears off across the whole run.

Mission variety is broad. A tank push with slightly goofy boost mechanics, night-vision stealth in Cairo, drone-bombing runs in Tajikistan, a subway chase, drone swarms over Prospect Park and a multi-silo finale give the campaign a rhythm that rarely repeats itself. The Tajikistan stretch is the standout, its unlimited-recharge bomber drones turning the mission into a power fantasy, though the same generosity borders on trivial and the challenge evaporates once the arsenal never runs dry. The night raid ranks a close second for its restraint.

Gunplay earns consistent praise across every mission. Bolt-action rifles carry believable weight, a belt-fed machine gun swings with real heft, and the moment-to-moment shooting sits among the best in the series. Death comes fast, with squishy players punished for exposure in classic campaign fashion, and the pacing holds across roughly five and a half hours without feeling rushed.

Readability is the persistent friction. Smoke-heavy battles routinely obscure enemies entirely, and multiple missions descend into firing at shapes in the haze, an accessibility failure that spans the whole campaign rather than a single set piece. Health regeneration coexisting with heal items makes little mechanical sense, and the flashback structure compounds confusion, jumping from a mountain operation straight to Egypt with no connective tissue and leaving the mission order feeling arbitrary rather than authored.

Squad drama lands unevenly. Predicting nearly every story beat, from a squadmate's death to a superior's betrayal, speaks to heavy-handed foreshadowing, yet the investment holds because the ensemble is given room to breathe, and an engineer, a support gunner and the rest of the unit divide their duties clearly enough to keep the drama grounded. Role clarity does not extend to everyone: one squad member's class is never established despite the others being explained, and physics jank, bodies stuck in walls, cameras yanked by explosions, thrown weapons vanishing mid-air, and one nonsensical three-inch fall death, surfaces often enough to break immersion. A Franchise Leadership credits roll and bloodied gun decals appearing on unfired weapons round out the small oddities.

Overall, the destructible spectacle, the varied mission roster and the heaviest gunplay in the series make this a campaign well above the genre's recent baseline, and the drone-bombing stretch alone justifies the time. Smoke-choked readability, jarring flashback jumps and physics jank are real blemishes on an experience that deserves its defence against the consensus.
