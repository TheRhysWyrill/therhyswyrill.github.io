A deadpan-sinister kindergarten where every adult is a sociopath and every child a threat is the stage, with the missing-student mystery as the dark spine of the whole plot. The writing is the star: the lunch lady's secret-ingredient slop, a small boy's existential nugget-metaphysics, a classmate's psychosexual power games and the janitor's mop-violence backstory. The town-wide cruelty lands as genuinely funny rather than off-putting, and the finale, an apocalyptic release of twenty-five talismans that kills the entire cast and openly advertises its own sequel, is a fittingly absurd capstone. A secret true-form cave rewards the deepest exploration. The pitch-black humour is the engine, and it never stalls.

The quest-web design is the structural achievement: interlocking character missions with dependency chains, repeated Monday resets and an in-game hint system that unlocks as play proceeds. One character's flower is needed for another's quest, a hall pass opens the janitor's closet, and the dependency graph slowly maps itself through the unlockable hints page. Each character's full quest unlocks a reusable special item, feeding back into the web. The schoolyard-to-show-and-tell daily schedule gives every loop a legible rhythm. Fifteen-plus in-game days pass on a full route, and the structure stays compelling throughout.

Apples ration actions per room, and dialogue choices and items chain across the day in a Groundhog-Day loop that turns route optimisation into the core puzzle. Deaths are frequent, cheap and funny, part of the loop rather than a punishment. Full completion covers all twenty-five Monstermon cards, all five friendship nuggets, the true-form cave and the apocalypse ending across roughly three and a half to four hours. The card hunt gives the back half its structure. The apples-as-action-economy is a distinctive twist on the daily-reset format. The 100% run is a satisfying arc.

Some quest steps are opaque without trial and error: a dialogue option that turns the lunch lady hostile, and a hold-to-lift cubby interaction pressed fruitlessly. Brute-force repetition is by design but tests patience, since restarting full days to tweak one late step in a chain stings, and forgetting to report back after poisoning the bugs cost more than one run. The tutorial's info-dump bombards, and the voice recorder bought early went unused in a first route. None of it breaks the loop, and the failures are usually funny enough to forgive. The route planning is the game's real tax.

The meta-ending, literally selling the sequel mid-apocalypse, is the last joke in a package built from them, and it lands. The art's flat, cheerful style contrasts the cruelty perfectly. Short Switch sessions suit the daily-reset structure well. The humour stays pitch-black without ever feeling mean-spirited toward the player. The dependency chains reward the planning impulse the game cultivates.

Three save files invite multiple routes from the start, and the red-sky finale sequence lands as the game's biggest spectacle. The lunch lady's slop percentage is the writing's best single gag, and the missing student's trail gives the darkness a spine that pays off. The morning-show-and-tell cadence keeps each Monday legible even on the fifteenth reset. The contrast between cheerful presentation and systemic cruelty is the joke's delivery mechanism. Failure states are punchlines rather than penalties.

Overall, a short, sharp, gleefully awful good time whose interlocking quest web, deadpan-sinister dialogue and Monstermon collectible layer reward full completion, ending by literally selling its sequel. Brute-force day restarts when a late chain step goes wrong, a couple of opaque interactions and a bombarding tutorial are honest costs, and the pitch-black writing is worth every restart.
