Martha is Dead sets a 1944 Tuscany psychological drama about twin sisters, identity and war against German occupation and partisan conflict, and it is less a horror game than its marketing suggests. The opening hour is the spookiest stretch, with the woods sequences and the White Lady legend doing the unsettling work before the game reveals itself as a photography-driven drama. Quiet dread and heavy themes carry the atmosphere rather than scares, and the most unsettling thing about the campaign is simply how silent it is. Grim material arrives steadily, body horror included, and the narrative pivots somewhere unexpected into territory most games would not touch.

The dark-room photography simulation is the game's defining mechanic and a genuinely deep one. Framing, focus, aperture and exposure play out on a period Rolleicord before prints move to the dark room for enlarger alignment, timed developer immersion and drying, a real-world-accurate process the game narrates while simplifying it. The depth overwhelms at first and satisfies by the end. No other game makes film development itself the mechanic; where spirit-photography forebears used the camera as a weapon, this camera is a fully simulated craft.

The two-way Morse telegraph puzzle is the highlight. Composing messages word by word, then decoding replies by walking a dot-and-dash tree, the system is initially bewildering and becomes the most satisfying puzzle in the campaign once it clicks. Period-accurate hardware interactions push the same identity further: rotary telephone dialling and telegraph keys both feel like operating actual machinery rather than pressing buttons. Infrared threat photos expose hidden messages, and tarot divinations provide chapter connective tissue with the diaries deepening the family history.

The White Lady legend weaves folklore through the woods, though riddle objectives can be hard to read against the environment. The stone hunt and bridge riddle both suffer opacity spikes, and the infrared icon is easy to miss entirely. The terrible bicycle compounds traversal complaints: awkward to ride, barely faster than running, and seemingly present only to justify the map's scale. Constant treks back to the house to develop film stretch the pacing between chapters.

Performance is the campaign's most frightening element on Linux. DX12 via Proton craters to around ten frames per second whenever the camera is raised, and the switch to DX11 mid-campaign transforms the experience into something that genuinely holds sixty. A boot crash from missing redistributables requires manual installation before the game even starts. On Windows none of this applies, but the DX11 recommendation stands regardless.

The narrative deals its heaviest hand late, with disturbing interactive scenes that earned the game its censorship history on console, where they play as cutscenes. The identity questions the story raises resolve into ambiguity that reads as strength rather than evasion, and the self-harm-awareness pivot lands with a seriousness few games attempt. The doll-scene sequence in particular reframes earlier sympathy into doubt. Ambiguity here is deliberate, and the game trusts its audience with it. Whether the story's central questions resolve neatly matters less than how seriously the framing treats them, and the confidence of that treatment elevates the whole campaign.

Overall, Martha is Dead is less a horror game than a gorgeous, grim, photography-driven psychological drama whose best puzzles and most disturbing scenes both land harder than expected. The dark-room simulation and Morse telegraph carry it past performance trouble, traversal complaints and riddle opacity. Play it with DX11 enabled and the horror expectations checked at the door.
