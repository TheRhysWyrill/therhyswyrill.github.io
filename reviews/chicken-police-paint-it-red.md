Clawville is a rain-slicked, black-and-white city where animal heads sit on human bodies, and a former member of a legendary detective duo, 121 days from retirement, is dragged back into the game by a blackmail case tied to a gangster's empire. Segregated species districts, hardboiled voiceover and a Chandler epigraph set the tone before the first scene loads. The premise sounds like a joke and plays like a noir film. That tension defines everything the game does well. Few titles commit this completely to both the pastiche and the puns, and the confidence shows in every frame.

Presentation does the heaviest lifting. Monochrome visuals with selective colour accents evoke classic detective cinema, while painted character portraits give each suspect a distinct, poster-ready face. The voice cast delivers line after line of gravel and smoke with total conviction, and the mix of jazz-tinged music with distant traffic and street noise sustains the mood between scenes. Even the profanity filter is characterful, swapping harsh language for a well-timed "cluck".

Lore runs deep for a story of this shape. A codex fills with more than thirty entries covering the city-state's history, its species politics and the cases that broke the legendary partnership, and optional collectibles and personal-info entries reward thorough scene inspection, each discovered item peeling back another layer of Clawville's segregated-species society. The writing leans into hardboiled convention knowingly, stacking genre furniture without collapsing into parody, and the Chandler epigraph signals early that the pastiche is a labour of love rather than a punchline.

The detective meter anchors the questioning system, tracking rapport as impressions are read from suspects' faces and used to unlock new dialogue lines. Reading an interviewee's nervous glance before choosing an approach gives interrogations a genuine rhythm of observation and pressure. Scene types, split between main, limited and closed, gate exploration transparently, making it easy to plan a complete sweep before moving the case forward. It is a well-considered structure for a dialogue-driven investigation, and reading the room correctly under time pressure delivers a satisfaction most adventure games never attempt.

Beyond the interviews, standard point-and-click work fills the gaps. Walking Dead-style scene interaction keeps fingers busy between conversations, inventory-based puzzles stay solvable without moon logic, and a secret ending plus a post-credits gallery of film-poster gags reward full completion. The Marty reunion scene stands out as a warm mid-case highlight, and the police-station interludes give the investigation a home base with real personality. Reaching the secret ending requires full attention to the questioning system rather than busywork, which keeps the completionist layer meaningful instead of mechanical.

Friction exists, mostly at the margins. The animal-head-on-human-body designs take real adjusting to, and dialogue scenes occasionally frame characters from angles that flatter no one, with front-facing close-ups exposing how small the heads sit on the shoulders. The interface is not always self-explanatory, particularly around pausing and map controls. Worst of all, the optional shooting-range minigames suffer slow aiming, heavy input delay and unreachable back-row targets, making them skippable by design and best treated as a curiosity rather than a challenge.

Overall, this is a fully voiced animal-noir gem whose presentation, writing and interrogation design come together far more convincingly than its quirky surface suggests. The voice work alone justifies the price of admission, and the worldbuilding underneath the jokes is surprisingly rich. Clumsy shooting minigames and an awkward camera barely dent an otherwise confident, complete package.
