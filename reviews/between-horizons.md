Stella inherits her father's post as chief of security aboard the generation ship Zephyr, thirty-three years into a century-plus voyage to Eurus D, and inherits its problems too: an attack, a dissident movement demanding democracy, and eventually a hack diverting the ship toward a secret second mission. Between Horizons marries the closed-society generation-ship premise to a proper clue-and-deduction detective structure, closer to a serious mystery than a walking simulator. The themes of inherited mission, generational duty and democracy aboard a sealed society give the cases stakes beyond puzzle-solving, and the dissident subplot's arguments land with real rhetorical force. Grief, duty and agency in a closed society are handled with more care than the compact runtime suggests. The final act's faster-second-ship reveal and the vote dilemma deliver genuine emotional investment.

The 2.5D art style is the constant refrain, praised a dozen times over: the Zephyr's corridors, the soothing menu music, the whole aesthetic makes a case for more games in this register. Scanning for clues is surprisingly fun, interviewing the ship's roughly 1,300-person population builds the case files, and the PDA evidence board turns collected facts into deductions. Cases are submitted permanently, with no undo, and the game hammers that finality home. Puzzle variety holds well: data-terminal irregularities, paper-recycling arithmetic, keypad deduction and alibi cross-checking. The structure rewards thoroughness, and 121 clues are collectable in a full sweep.

The branching evidence-driven structure is the engine: assign an evidence item as each case's solution, permanently, and the accuracy shapes which of many endings the campaign delivers. Cracking every case and unlocking the best ending tier is achievable in a tight single sitting, and the autosave-only design fits the permanent-verdict philosophy. Optional conversations deepen the ship's society, though some dead-end without offering anything. The nonlinearity occasionally leaves the next step unclear. When the system works, the deduction feels earned.

Friction accumulates at the edges. The PC keyboard and controller bindings are visibly broken, with tutorial prompts reading Unbound, forcing a mid-prologue switch from controller to keyboard; it is a dealbreaker-level flaw that the game's other qualities somehow survive. Navigation around the ring-shaped ship is confusing, with locked areas, easy wrong turns and circles walked while hunting missing information. The final hacker identification is the deduction most under-clued, solvable by process of elimination with no concrete evidence to back the answer, a conclusion the game should have clued properly. Earlier cases fare better, but the late-game cluing dips exactly where confidence matters most.

The detective structure earns its comparison to contemporaries in the deduction genre, and the permanent verdicts distinguish it from every game that lets players re-guess. Autosave-only fits the design's commitment. The epilogue's Ethics Committee framing closes the moral questions the cases raise, and the monologue about the opening attack's consequence gives the ending weight. The music is surprisingly soothing throughout, an odd fit for a murder investigation that works. The single-sitting length is a strength of pacing rather than a flaw of scope.

What holds the package together is confidence: the art commits to its palette, the writing commits to its politics, and the structure commits to its finality. Every case matters because none can be revisited, and the branching endings honour that. The navigation friction and the binding bugs are real costs, but neither touches the deduction core. The under-clued final deduction is the design's one genuine miss in an otherwise fair difficulty curve.

Overall, a beautifully presented, tightly written sci-fi detective story whose 2.5D aesthetic, permanent case verdicts and closed-society politics deliver genuine emotional investment across a compact single sitting. The broken PC bindings, ring-ship navigation confusion and one under-clued final deduction are real costs, but the deduction core and the art make it an easy recommendation for narrative-mystery fans.
