Amalur remains one of the strangest experiments of its era: MMO-grade breadth fused to single-player action combat this fluid, wrapped in a fate-weaving premise that still feels like the setting's signature idea. A protagonist whose death broke the loom of destiny walks a high-fantasy world at war between mortals and the immortal Tuatha, and the fateless-one conceit (a hero with no destiny) gives even routine quests thematic weight. The visual leap from the 2012 original is stark in itself, running far beyond what the last generation ever managed, and the remaster's core promise, making a beloved oddity playable again, is honoured.

Combat is the crown jewel, and it holds up against most modern peers. Nine weapon families span shackrams, fay blades, staffs, sceptres and great swords, each with distinct move sets, and mixing them freely (daggers one moment, spellcasting the next) makes build experimentation effortless. The Reckoning Mode slow-motion finisher delivers the fate conceit mechanically, and the shackrams in particular remain genuinely unusual: no other game offers anything quite like them. Across a full completionist run, the combat never stops being the reason to continue.

The remaster's fixes address the original's real complaints. Region level-caps no longer scale content to the entry level, so backtracking no longer trivialises earlier zones, and the enemy-name colour system makes sense once decoded. The stash, junk and salvage systems are dramatically improved, and blacksmithing, sagecraft and alchemy reward deep engagement; progression is generous to a fault, with skills, trainers, books and respec abuse allowing near-total skill maximisation. Three DLC campaigns (Teeth of Naros, Legend of Dead Kel with its Gravehal Keep building, and the Fatesworn endgame) plus a glut of faction quests (House of Valor arena, Scholia Arcana, Travelers, House of Sorrows) pad the world generously.

The Fatesworn endgame is the expansion's genuine goal, and the chaos-rift structure gives it shape: portals seeded through the world, a chaos-versus-order confrontation with the god Telogrus, and the fate-sworn armour set as a tangible target. The narrative lands on a surprisingly poignant note about fate and free will, and the farewell that follows the final confrontation is quiet and melancholy rather than triumphant. As an arc for a decade-old world, it closes better than anyone expected.

The Fate Sworn armour quest, though, is the centrepiece's fatal flaw. Five separate large dungeons each funnel into an embedded, enemy-crammed chaos rift, and the switch-hunting, door-opening, horde-clearing loop is worse than the Oblivion Gates it invites comparison to: time-wasting design in a way the base game never quite stoops to. The DLC campaign is also shorter than community consensus suggests, an odd pairing of tedium and brevity, and the endgame recycles the same boguet, spite and troll roster across all four regions and the chaos content with very few new enemies.

Older cracks remain visible elsewhere. The story and lore are plentiful but skimmable, delivered through books, murals and long NPC monologues that reward patience the quest pacing does not earn. The inventory economy is the persistent friction: a 190-capacity ceiling, quest items that refuse to be junked, and constant selling trips interrupt exploration. Post-game XP inflation is wild, with former thousand-point enemies suddenly paying fivefold, and NPC pathing plus companion AI remain dated: companions cannot swim or jump, follow poorly, and occasionally stand idle mid-fight.

Overall, this is a hugely generous, mechanically excellent action-RPG whose combat still outclasses most of its modern peers, and the remaster genuinely fixes the original's worst habits. The Fate Sworn armour quest's tedium, the recycled enemy roster and the inventory friction are real deductions, but ninety hours of everything the world offers confirm the affection: the quest design is pure 2012 filler, and the combat still justifies every hour of it.
