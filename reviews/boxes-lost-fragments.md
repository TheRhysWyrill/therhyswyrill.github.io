A mysterious letter-writer hires a master thief for a series of odd jobs, each one a job extracting fragments from ornate puzzle boxes across a gothic-Victorian facility. Collectible notes from the client reveal a creator who built an artificial being powered by a dark-energy heart, then ripped the heart out and imprisoned her when her curiosity turned dangerous. What begins as a comfortable inventor mystery accumulates an AI-ethics sting, and the final reveal recasts the entire campaign. Every box, it turns out, was a piece of a prison break.

The diorama structure paces the whole campaign beautifully. Five themed chapters (Initium, Nexus, Cognito, Fractus and Aurora) each hold multiple boxes whose extracted fragments feed a central mechanism, giving every chapter a clear sense of progress and destination. Themes rotate from a clockwork workshop through industrial machinery, spy safes and a temple to a sarcophagus. The hub structure keeps momentum high without ever feeling like a menu, and the fragments make the next floor feel earned rather than merely unlocked.

Puzzle variety is the game's standout quality, and the boxes themselves are the stars. A Monument Valley-style mechanical ladybug plays with perspective, a model ship actually fires its cannons, a train box steams through its own window, a temple stages red-light-green-light with its own statues, and one cannon is genuinely fired. Symbol alignment, colour sequencing, music cylinders, mazes and spot-the-difference puzzles fill out the roster. Each box is its own invention, and the roster never repeats a trick.

Difficulty sits on the low side, with most boxes yielding within minutes and the campaign clearly simpler than the dense-realist standard set by the genre's leading series. The trade is deliberate: bright, toy-like dioramas replace tactile griminess, and the pace keeps delight ahead of challenge. Sliding-block puzzles supply the only real teeth, and they are easy to irreversibly ruin because no reset exists; recovering an original starting position is exceptionally difficult. One circle puzzle aside, everything else falls to patient observation rather than logic-brute force.

Mouse control occasionally fights the player on precision rotations, and losing grip of a turning mechanism mid-adjustment costs more patience than any puzzle does. A 2.5 GB VRAM draw raised eyebrows at boot for a game of this scope, and the campaign ends without credits, briefly obscuring whether the finale had actually arrived. None of these quirks outlast the moment. The presentation itself is consistently handsome, and the boxes photograph like exhibits.

The whole campaign resolves comfortably within a single evening of roughly three and a half hours, with occasional stuck moments confined to the sliding puzzles. Fragment extraction unlocks each chapter's floor, and the final box completes the escape the notes have been hinting at throughout. The twist is well constructed, and the player's unwitting role as lockpick for a godlike artificial mind retroactively colours every earlier box. Few puzzle games stick a narrative landing this cleanly.

Overall, the sheer variety and charm of the puzzle boxes carry a relaxed, gorgeous campaign whose only real frustrations are unresettable sliding puzzles and a difficulty ceiling set deliberately low. The Aurora twist, the themed chapters and the toy-box presentation outweigh the mouse friction and missing credits with ease. Trading challenge for delight is a gamble, and this one mostly gets away with it.
