Moonbury Island distrusts the capital, and the capital sends a chemist anyway. The premise inverts the usual strangers-in-a-small-town formula: residents hate outsiders after a past chemical accident blighted their land, and the trust meter opens firmly hostile, earned back one cured patient at a time. The overarching arc of restoring the aloe plant and clearing the chemists' old name lands warmly, and the writing mixes slice-of-life with genuinely affecting threads, from Garrett's acceptance of his wheelchair to Hannah's creative self-doubt and Nova's grief for her late husband. The tone stays cosy throughout, and the cosiness is the point.

The forage-brew-cure loop is the game's engine. Stamina-limited sickle, axe and hammer gather materials by day, light encounters against bears, blobs and generals guard the rich patches, and the gathered ingredients feed a block-fitting brewing minigame that plays like a chemical Tetris, filling cauldron blocks with the right extracts. Diagnosis runs on rhythm and dodge minigames, and each cure pays out gold and trust. The loop clicks early and stays satisfying across a long campaign, with early unlocks such as fast travel smoothing the daily rhythm.

Moonbury itself is the real star. Around twenty-five residents keep daily schedules, and learning when and where each one is reachable becomes a substantial share of the playtime, tracked obsessively across the whole town. Friendship questlines carry surprising emotional weight, with the in-game book's maxim that no potion can cure what happiness cannot serving as the story's thesis. The dog deserves special mention, from its endearing walk to its endless pettability, and it anchors the campaign's gentlest moments. Romancing nearly every eligible character happens almost incidentally through the friendship system, though confessions unlock little beyond dialogue.

Badge upgrades gate new areas in sequence, from the Meadow Range to the Glade to the Barren Wasteland, each unlocking a fresh plague-outbreak wave of patients. Starter tools feel underpowered at first, sitting at minimal durability, and tool and cauldron upgrades cost far more than the town's economy pays out, making income feel tight throughout. The daily material spawn caps, two razor fins or three big trees per area per day, artificially stretch the late-game fetch quests, and several quests gate behind one-more-day respawns that test patience in the final stretch.

Fishing is the economy's sorest wound. The activity itself is mechanically fine and passes pleasant hours, but the experience payout per fish sits insultingly low, turning an optional pastime into exceptionally tedious work whenever progression demands it. Two side quests, A Joyful Tradition and A Promise to Keep, refuse to trigger for days despite meeting their stated conditions, and two cooking recipes, the omelette and ginseng soup, appear unobtainable even by community consensus. The starting house, a dump with a tatty sofa, at least gives the renovation economy something to fix.

Completion sits at a realistic ninety-two percent after a long, unhurried campaign, with the journal of fish, enemies and recipes rewarding deliberate hunting over house upgrades. The late-game pacing shifts noticeably once every quest starts demanding a return visit the next day, and the final fishing-based marriage quest, compared favourably to a certain open-world fishing marathon, closes the arc on a high. The grind is a feature for those playing to inhabit Moonbury rather than to optimise it.

Overall, Potion Permit is a warm, well-written chemist life-sim whose clinical forage-brew-cure loop, block-puzzle brewing and full town of scheduled, individually written residents make the slow burn genuinely rewarding. Tight money, pitiful fishing XP and multi-day quest gating are honest costs, and the town's charm pays them back with interest.
