A tiny salesman arrives in a Yorkshire town for a mayoral meeting and never reaches it, dragged instead into helping every eccentric resident with problems that escalate from mundane to unspeakable. The town of Barnsworth is a lovingly rendered, thinly-veiled caricature of Barnsley, all working-class pub and chippy culture, with jokes about gravy tax and morning pints anchoring the hyper-local satire. No narrative exists beyond the task-to-task chain, with the mayor perpetually in a meeting and the credits arriving only when the meeting finally does. The comedy commits completely to Northern English absurdism. It is a sketch show disguised as an adventure, and the disguise is half the joke.

Smacking is the entire verb set, with jumping the only other action, and the design wrings astonishing mileage from that poverty of options. Every solved task lubricates the next tiny chain of errands through the connected town, from lubricating a stuck arm to squeezing a lawnmower to navigating chip-shop queues. Fall damage simply does not exist, so experimentation is consequence-free and the world rewards poking absolutely everything. A character's early lament about loving puzzles while noting there are none around here lands as deliberate meta-commentary on the whole structure. The triviality is the point, embraced rather than hidden.

The running gags accumulate magnificently: a mole with a wife and an appetite for worms, a sausage-dropping glory hole, a stiff fish that defies description, all building toward a chaotic musical finale where a deranged family cooks the mole into a soup. The milk quest stands as the weirdest peak in a game full of them. Each solved errand escalates the absurdism rather than repeating it, and the pacing never lets a gag overstay its welcome. The comedy is relentless without becoming exhausting. Very few games sustain this joke density across an entire runtime.

Authentic Northern English dialect drives the writing, presented with dialect subtitles that preserve the local voice faithfully. The game charms most with those subtitles on, though the obscurity is real: even native Northerners will occasionally lose a line, and the title screen's FAF for options baffles beyond reason. The register is swingale-and-flat-cap absurdism delivered with total sincerity. For those raised on it, the dialect is the warmth; for those outside it, subtitles soften the loss. Either way the voice work carries the comedy's soul.

The scale of the tiny protagonist shifts constantly, visually disorienting without ever truly bothering, and the two-verb gameplay remains paper-thin throughout. Puzzles are absent by design, difficulty is absent by design, and challenge-seekers will find nothing here. The hot tub and cellar sequences provide physical comedy peaks that justify the simplicity. Around two and a half hours cover the whole experience, brief enough that the thin mechanics never curdle. The flat-colour art keeps every gag legible and charming.

The pub, the chip shop, the cellar and the hot tub each host their own self-contained comedy sketches, giving the connected town the structure of an anthology threaded together by errands. The crossword, the soup and the sausage each serve as props for gags that escalate the surrealism one notch further than expected. The finale's musical crescendo lands as genuinely stunning, deranged and delightful in equal measure. Every sequence understands exactly how far to push before pulling back.

Overall, this is a riotously funny love letter to Northern England whose relentless absurdism, loving dialect and perfect comic pacing outweigh paper-thin jump-and-slap gameplay entirely. The milk quest, the mole and the musical finale deliver peaks that justify the whole, and the brevity ensures nothing outstays its welcome. A very British game, and a very good one at exactly the size it chose to be.
