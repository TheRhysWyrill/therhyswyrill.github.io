A raccoon private eye working the walled city of Veiltown takes what looks like a routine infidelity case, and the premise unravels into a conspiracy involving a nightlife heiress's club, disappearances in West End and an ape ruling class that literally consumes the lower orders. The class-warfare allegory is not subtext here; it is the text, delivered through satirical environmental writing and moody pixel-art districts that rank among the medium's most distinctive cityscapes. Cannibalism as class metaphor sets a tone few games dare. The Dorton Tuna fun-facts machine and grotesque corporate obituaries deepen the satire without breaking the noir register. The city itself is the game's finest character.

The document-analysis deduction scene at the science minister's apartment is the investigative gameplay's high-water mark, cross-referencing stolen papers with a contact to deduce a buyer's identity. It is the one sequence where the detective premise delivers genuine deduction rather than dialogue routing, and it deserves to be the template for more of the game. Dialogue and inventory puzzles follow the standard adventure grammar competently. Stealth sequences appear occasionally, functional but shallow and occasionally buggy-feeling, cleared without much engagement. The mechanical layer supports the story without ever competing with it.

The pole-cipher keypad puzzle gates the Bite's basement, a prolonged stall that resolves into delight once its logic clicks and a retroactive appreciation for what it was attempting. The terminal password rewards environmental observation, and quest-pathing generally guides through clues rather than markers. Mislabeled quest expectations and invisible interact prompts create mild friction without ever blocking progress. The puzzle layer is light, uneven in its signposting, and at its best genuinely clever. More consistency would have elevated the whole.

Act three abandons the detective frame entirely, mutating the story into body-horror science fiction with a surreal self-confrontation sequence that reframes everything before it. The swerve is genuinely surprising, executed with conviction, and while it may divide taste it is a boldness the genre rarely attempts. The early beats suggest a comfort-zone noir before pulling the rug. The transformation imagery lands with Persona-adjacent weight. Whatever the player expects going in, the finale refuses to deliver it politely.

The investigation itself is thin for a game wearing a detective's coat, with real sleuthing confined largely to the opening mark-hunt before the plot takes over. The sparse, well-placed soundtrack impresses, particularly the diegetic songs that surface only at key moments rather than droning beneath every scene. Roughly three hours across two sittings cover both ending branches, with the second runthrough mostly a straight replay to the alternate close. The brevity suits the focused structure even where the mechanical depth disappoints. Achievements reward a single complete pass.

Clarissa Bloodworth's club anchors the case's social geography, the ape ruling class embodies the allegory's power structure without a word of exposition, and the disappearances threading through West End give the city a genuine sense of menace. The worldbuilding rewards attention paid to its margins, where the political subtext lives in shop signs, streetie symbols and background chatter rather than speeches. Two ending branches close the story with meaningfully different registers. The writing never wastes a frame of its runtime.

Overall, this is a short, atmospheric anthropomorphic noir that over-delivers on tone, worldbuilding and audacity even as it under-delivers on detective mechanics. Veiltown's districts, the document-analysis scene and a genuinely surprising finale justify the visit, while the shallow stealth and thin investigation remain honest limitations rather than betrayals. A distinctive, committed piece of political allegory that looks and sounds like nothing else in its genre.
