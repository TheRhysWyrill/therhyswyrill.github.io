Earth is succumbing to desertification, and the lunar microwave power transmitter that once fed it went silent during the 2054 blackout; an unnamed astronaut is dispatched to find out why. That premise builds an entire mystery around planetary energy infrastructure, with the MPT, helium-3 mining and fusion reactors forming the mechanical and narrative spine rather than set dressing. The investigation unfolds across Pearson Station, the Copernicus Moonhub, abandoned outposts and the Tombaugh reactor, piecing together an evacuation, an authoritarian plan and a conspiracy of stolen resources through audio logs, holograms and database entries. It is a slow burn that rewards patience, closer in tone to silent sci-fi film than to game.

Atmosphere is the game's strongest suit. Silent corridors, flickering emergency strips and the oppressive quiet of a dead colony lit only in strips generate unease that no jump scare could match. The wordless protagonist suits the setting perfectly, and the diegetic lore delivery never breaks the frame. Exploring darkened rooms carries tension precisely because nothing is there; the loneliness is the point, and it is executed with discipline.

The set-pieces break up the puzzle-solving with genuine spectacle. The rocket launch plays out in real time against a NASA-style checklist, an unforgettable sequence that makes the process itself the drama, while the zero-gravity spacewalks that follow revel in their own weightlessness. The drone piloting through service pipes, lunar rover driving and monorail rides fill the connective tissue, and oxygen management in vacuum sections adds light tension without ever punishing. The final reactor sequence combines hazard avoidance with a timed dish alignment for a proper climax.

Movement is the game's weakest mechanical element. Outside zero-G, the astronaut glides as if on ice, momentum carrying past intended stops and making precision manoeuvring harder than it needs to be. In fairness, the design never demands tight control; nothing in the campaign requires reflexes, and the quirk settles into tolerable strangeness rather than active frustration after the first hour. It is a flaw the rest of the game absorbs.

Open-rover exploration tempts and then fences itself off. Invisible walls and an out-of-range warning ring the drivable surface, blocking sightlines to distant golden domes that exist only as bait, and the restriction reads as artificial in a game otherwise committed to believable spaces. The environments are linear by design, chaptered by facility, and the few moments where curiosity exceeds the boundary underline how much more fun a truly open lunar surface would have been. Puzzle signposting wobbles too: the timed monorail track-switching inputs are not obvious until failure teaches them, and interactable-object ambiguity occasionally slows progress without ever seriously blocking it.

The lore rewards attention at every level. Reading every log and database entry assembles the Lunar Council's evacuation, the authoritarian "Outward" plan and the personal fates threaded through it, and the conspiracy of stolen helium-3 develops real grip as the pieces connect. The slow-burn structure trusts the player to care about paperwork and transmissions, and the trust pays off. Few puzzle adventures make infrastructure itself the story.

Overall, this is a quietly gripping sci-fi mystery that rewards patience and curiosity far more than reflexes, its atmospheric dead-colony isolation, real-time launch sequence and infrastructure-as-narrative design outweighing floaty movement, invisible rover boundaries and under-signposted timed sections. The wordless protagonist and diegetic lore deliver a tone few games attempt. A haunting, satisfying opener to a series worth following.
