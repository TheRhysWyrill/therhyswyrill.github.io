A linear sniper fantasy with no open zones and no contracts, built entirely around cinematic overwatch set-pieces where a single shot at extreme distance is the payoff. The campaign jumps between the Philippines, a Bosnia flashback to Sarajevo and the Tibetan border, and the mission structure is cooler and better built than its predecessor in every respect that matters. Sarajevo delivers the most atmospheric stretch, a deserted, hostile cityscape rendered in muted dread, and a night-vision mission stands among the series' best. The narrative shares nothing with the first game, making the sequel connection one of name alone, and a mentor's betrayal lands with recognisable familiarity for anyone who has played the genre before.

The red-dot aim assist is what makes the shooting fun at all. Without it, most long-range attempts would sail wide, and with it the game's signature moments, a bullet-cam chasing a round across more than a thousand metres, become consistently achievable. Slow-motion multi-kills from overwatch positions deliver the set-pieces the genre promises, and the heartbeat, breath and minimap interface improvements over the first game keep the necessary information visible without cluttering the scope.

Long-distance sequences rank as the best content in the series. Insane-distance shots, lined double-kills where one round passes through two bodies, and wind and bullet-drop compensation give the sniping genuine mechanical texture, and the bullet-cam on long-range kills remains the franchise's signature flourish. Enemy patrols now react to discovered bodies, a small but meaningful step up in simulation, and spotter callouts guide each overwatch setup with competent direction.

Detection remains the series' stubborn flaw. One missed shot by a millimetre and every enemy knows exactly where the shooter lies, a one-strike system that punishes honest ballistics more than error. The AI is bizarre in both directions, detecting silently-fired rifles from absurd, acoustically impossible range while sprinting right past a prone shooter unnoticed, and the double-kill system is inconsistent about when bullets pass through two bodies, sometimes cleaving cleanly and sometimes stopping dead. Lined shots deserve better rules than the game provides.

Stability failures compound the frustration. Two crashes on a Linux and Proton setup contradicted the platform database's platinum rating, and one early session lost its save entirely to a crash, an unforgivable cost in a campaign short enough to finish in a single long sitting. The game runs roughly ninety minutes shorter than its predecessor, and under-explained collectibles add little, with one remembered cache and no indication where the others hide.

Small annoyances accumulate at the margins. Sniper-tagged hand-shots that wound instead of kill, a cluttered boot-up advertisement sequence before the menu, and a villain twist that genre veterans will call before it arrives all register without derailing anything. The ending, however, deserves explicit credit: where the first game cut to black with no denouement, this one assembles its finale properly, and the structural improvement typifies the sequel's overall step up.

Overall, the red-dot-assisted long-range sniping, the cooler mission structure and a genuinely assembled ending make this the strongest cinematic overwatch package in the line to date, even with the broken detection system and Proton instability. It is short, occasionally unstable, and worth playing for the thousand-metre kills alone.
