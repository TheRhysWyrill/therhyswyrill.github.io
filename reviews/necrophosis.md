A wanderer crosses a dying realm of dead gods in Necrophosis, a first-person puzzle game assembled almost entirely from corpses. The curse of Necrophosis presides over everything, and the mythology arrives through cryptic inscriptions carved into tongues, tablets and slabs, supplemented by terse exchanges with deities still trapped in the scenery. Nothing here is played for comedy, yet the overwrought inscription text lands somewhere between morbid and absurd, and the game's deadpan delivery gives the whole thing a peculiar charge. Visually it owes an obvious debt to HR Giger, all clustered orifices and sinewy architecture, and the art direction carries the production. The audio runs the same direction: every surface squelches, and the soundscape is deliberately repellent.

That commitment to the grotesque is the game's defining trait. Environmental puzzles revolve around organs, orifices and infestations, and the body horror is pervasive enough that trypophobia-adjacent imagery appears constantly. The tone never winks, which makes the absurdity funnier: a lore tablet can instruct the reader to look up toward the sky while the sky is the only thing in view, and the game offers no comment. Atmosphere emerges from this contrast between absolute earnestness and material that borders on self-parody.

Possession drives the puzzle language. Collected brains, eyeballs and limbs attach to inert bodies and objects, letting a fresh arm reach what the old one could not and letting detached parts scout ahead. The signature moment is piloting a flesh mech assembled from whatever organs the world has supplied, and the mechanic scales all the way down to a voice-mask that grants control of distant lanky figures. It is a strange, generous idea, and the game keeps finding new containers to stuff it into.

The lantern gates every area and must be fed with souls, which turns the trade-chain progression into the game's spine. Hearts, tears, coins, masks, crowns and brains all circulate as currency for one another, with heavy inventory juggling as the cost of doing business. A carried burden slows movement to a walk, so there is no sprint and no combat, and the pacing never leaves the deliberate register. Coin logic is charmingly opaque at first, arriving with no explanation of why anything wants them.

Encounters lean on pattern and physicality rather than combat. A whack-a-mole soul-eater demands quick routing of escaping souls, a maggot infestation must be cleared through a Simon-says sequence, and eyeball-in-socket puzzles treat the eyeballs as frustratingly brittle resources. A bone-spider stealth interlude breaks the rhythm midway, and the closing sequence scales the possession mechanic up to a colossal vessel for a ragdoll-driven finale. Each beat has a distinct mechanical identity, which keeps the chain of trades from feeling repetitive.

Difficulty is wildly uneven. The second area stalls badly: one interactable is effectively invisible until the camera is on top of it, coloured yellow rather than the white flash used everywhere else, and the third-soul hunt invites walking the same corridor dozens of times before the elbow-sized answer is spotted. Later areas then fall with almost no resistance, with two consecutive sections offering barely any opposition. The contrast makes the weakly signposted stretch feel less like design and more like an oversight.

Smaller oddities accumulate. No controller support is a strange omission for a game built on slow, simple inputs, and the omission is felt most during the inventory-heavy trade sections where mouse precision buys nothing. Against that, the scale of the achievement is remarkable: two people built this, and the art, audio and mechanical variety all read like a much larger team's output.

Overall, Necrophosis is a short, striking curio whose flesh-scape art direction and possess-anything puzzle language leave a mark well beyond its size. The signposting collapse in the second area and the missing controller support are real irritations, but neither undoes the craft on display. Judge it as a macabre mechanical toy box rather than a puzzle game with consistent difficulty, and it earns its keep.
