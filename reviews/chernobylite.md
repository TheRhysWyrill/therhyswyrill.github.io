A sci-fi-tinged Exclusion Zone built on real disaster history opens with documentary-style text about the evacuation and fallout, and the dread-soaked Pripyat atmosphere delivers repeatedly across the whole campaign. This is primarily horror in the vein of the developers' earlier thriller pedigree, and the atmosphere is the core strength: genuinely creepy moments land across every session, the rain-grey zone feels persistently hostile, and the Russian voiceover track fits the setting far better than the higher-pitched English alternative. The genre blend is a genuine oddity rather than a STALKER clone, folding base building, companion morale, sci-fi portals and RPG progression into one survival shell.

The survival and crafting systems are refreshingly restrained: braced for a resource-tracking burden, players find only five components to track, mechanical, electronics, flammables, herbs and mushrooms, making the loop approachable without being shallow. XP flows from everything, looting, events and clues alike, and the Fallout-4-like base grid hosts companion assignments and daily planning. Captures by NAR soldiers funnel into a prison escape rather than a game over, a forgiving structure that keeps momentum alive.

The psyche system is the design's quiet genius: combat is mechanically mediocre but deliberately discouraged, since every kill costs psyche, and stealth-first exploration becomes the rational strategy rather than a restriction. The heist finale, with squad assignments and companion stakes, evokes a celebrated RPG set-piece and converts steady enjoyment into outright delight, with per-character epilogue wrap-ups rewarding the choices made along the way. Re-running missions to flip companion outcomes and secure the best ending is worth the time, and the branching structure gives the twenty-hour campaign genuine replay pull.

The late-game railgun is the balance's undoing: with self-regenerating ammunition it trivialises encounters, trades one point of damage for never carrying ammo, and turns the tense scavenger fantasy into an overpowered killing spree that kills the horror's tension. The dodge mapping compounds the combat's weakness, clicking the left stick to evade while also using it to move, an unreliable control that fails to register during the fights the game already discourages.

Stability stumbles are real: crashes hit twice in a session, though the saving system redeems them, always keeping a save so no more than ten minutes of progress is ever lost. Smaller friction includes a resource name that grates as a wording nitpick and subtitles with no drop shadow that become hard to read over bright backgrounds. Neither touches the core loop, but both are the kind of polish gaps that remind of a small studio's reach. The chernobylite crystal itself threads through the fiction neatly, powering both the base's upgrades and the portal mysteries, and the companion roster's morale interplay with the daily schedule gives even quiet days weight. Free-roaming scavenging runs between missions keep the zone's dread at the foreground rather than letting the base simulation swallow the horror.

Roughly twenty hours cover every quest, investigation and the best heist outcome, with the daily base-planning rhythm, one personal mission plus companion assignments, giving the campaign a satisfying cadence. The documentary grounding keeps the fantastical elements anchored, and the finale's twist reframes the antagonist in a way that lands as coherent rather than contrived.

Overall, an atmospheric, forgiving sci-fi survival RPG whose dread-soaked atmosphere, restrained five-component crafting and Mass Effect-style heist finale carry it past mediocre combat, an overpowered late-game railgun and occasional crashes. It is a genuine genre oddity that rewards stealth-first exploration and deliberate choice-chasing, and the zone is worth twenty hours of anyone's time.
