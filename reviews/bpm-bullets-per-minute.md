A valkyrie descends through Norse-styled dungeons, shooting dwarves, spiders and champions to a heavy-metal soundtrack, and every action in the game answers to the beat. Reloads sync to the music, dodges snap on the downbeat, enemies open fire in time, and the result is a shooter that plays less like a gunfight and more like an instrument. There is no story to speak of and none is missed: atmosphere is entirely tonal, built from per-biome themes, each with its own dedicated metal track. Nothing else in the first-person space commits this hard to rhythm as a mechanical foundation rather than a soundtrack garnish.

The soundtrack earns the commitment. Every biome carries a distinct heavy theme, and the music does real work: reload animations resolve on the beat, weapon handling locks into the tempo, and the songs sustain their quality across the whole descent. An optional loose timing mode relaxes the beat window for those whose attention is split, which makes the core loop tractable without dissolving it. When the synergy clicks, reloading becomes a percussion part and the gunfight turns into an extended jam session with hostile time signatures.

The crosshair is the system that teaches everything else. Its subtle pulse telegraphs exactly when a dodge will land on beat, a detail easy to miss for hours and transformative once spotted, converting deaths from random to instructive. Run structure follows the classic roguelike curve: procedurally mixed floors, coin shops, sacrifice altars, banks and challenge rooms between encounters, and unlockable characters such as Freya and Hilde altering the kit. Early runs collapse to ranged pressure; late runs snowball, and one lucky build (an SMG with infinite ammo plus the Dwarven Ring's exploding bullets) is the payoff the whole loop exists to produce.

Visibility is the game's real opponent. Dark zones, red-saturated palettes and enemies that blend into the walls make ranged attackers genuinely difficult to spot, and the tiniest pixel reads betray position at distance; spiders stand out while bats vanish into the gloom. Deaths cluster disproportionately around ranged enemies whose shots arrive from indistinct corners, and their weapons carry arbitrary range limits that feel less like design and more like an apology for the readability problem. No invincibility frames follow a hit, so overlapping volleys delete health before the source can be located.

The challenge modes compound the visibility trouble rather than relieving it. Retro Run, a Doom-flavoured pixelated variant, turns already murky arenas into abstract soup, and Super Time, which step-locks movement to the beat, produces a stuttery gait that repels almost immediately. At maximum sprint speed, geometry snags constantly, wedging the character against door frames mid-escape. Meanwhile the post-game gates itself behind hard-mode full clears and demanding kill counters, leaving the unlockable cast largely out of reach for anyone unwilling to master a game that punishes precision this harshly.

Run-to-run variance leans heavily on RNG upgrade discovery. One descent begins with an exploding-projectile snowball; another finds nothing for four floors, and the difference between triumph and repeated failure can be a single lucky shop. A 30-coin gamble that supposedly makes items permanent behaves inconsistently enough to feel like a placebo. Combined with the no-i-frames design, the result is a game whose difficulty comes less from the beat mechanics, which are fair and learnable, than from how much the roll of the dice and the murk of the arena permit a fight to be read at all.

Overall, this is a stylish, music-driven roguelike whose beat-synced gunplay, biome-specific metal soundtrack and snowballing builds deliver some of the most memorable first-person moments of its year, and whose only real sin is making the things that kill the player so hard to see. The crosshair telegraph, the loose timing option and the lucky-build highs carry a full clear comfortably. Forgive the murk and the RNG, and the rhythm fusion alone justifies the descent.
