A marine arrives at the UAC's Mars facility just as a doctor's teleportation experiments open the way to Hell, and the reboot retells the classic setup as slow horror rather than speed, a complete tonal pivot from the entries before it. The horror is environmental: flickering lights, whispering vents, and a slow drip of dread through PDA logs documenting outbreaks, disappearances and doctored safety reports. The ancient-civilisation and Soul Cube lore lands well, the original cover art hides in a tablet, and the early bathroom mirror remains the game's biggest jump-scare in a campaign otherwise built on restraint.

Atmosphere-as-horror is the defining design: near-total darkness, whispered audio logs and dread rather than scares, a tone that works even when deconstructed, since the reason it is scary is that nothing can be seen. It is the black sheep of its lineage, a shooter where an office hallway can kill, and no other entry in the series leans this hard on dread, audio logs and darkness.

The PDA audio-log worldbuilding is the campaign's quiet triumph, dozens of logs about power faults, missing staff and teleportation experiments that reward full listening and add hours to the run. Hidden-wall ambushes, secret panels swinging open with enemies behind them, become a recurring and memorable feature, exasperating but impossible to forget, and learning to expect them is its own small education. Sentry-bot escort sections are a genuine delight in a game otherwise built on solitude, and Dead Space's later DNA is easy to spot in the setup.

The loop is dark-corridor shooting, keycard and PDA hunting, occasional platforming and those escort set pieces. The storage-locker code maze never fully surrenders its logic, the service-bridge jumps draw ire, and the campaign spans roughly ten and a half hours with the two expansions adding another five or six. Sarge's BFG duel, the Guardian and the wave-based finale give each chapter its own punctuation, and Revenants supply the mid-campaign's most persistent headaches.

The late-game enemy spam sours the final third, with a small room holding two Hell Knights and a spawner standing as the low point of throwing everything at the player at once. Some enemies soak absurd numbers of point-blank shotgun shells, a cheap trick that recurs often enough to sting, and the SMG feels weak by comparison. The integrated flashlight of the modern edition is the right call over the original's gun-or-light choice, yet the beam itself stays weak and the recharge mechanic is pointless.

The base game's pacing dips in its mazes, but the expansions shine: the first is the best-paced of the three, ending the package on a high, and the second a nice little side episode with a confusing wave-based finale of its own. One hard crash on Switch cost a few minutes of waiting and a level restart, the first on that hardware. The CPU-complex maze is the run's lowest point, disorienting by design but frustrating in practice.

Overall, a dated but durable horror shooter whose dread and lore outweigh its cheapest tricks, with the black sheep earning its keep through atmosphere, audio-log storytelling and ambush design rather than speed or spectacle. Late-game spam, spongy point-blank enemies and a disorienting maze are real costs, but the full package, base campaign and both expansions included, is worth the trip for anyone curious about the series' slow-burn detour.
