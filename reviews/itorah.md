ITORAH sends the last human across a lush Mesoamerican-inspired island to uncover humanity's fate and stem a spreading plague, armed with Kabu, a wisecracking talking axe. Mushroom forests, storm archives, icy caverns and lava forges give the journey constant visual variety, and the tone stays warm and folkloric throughout. The village of Tamale, its smithies and a fiery arena duel anchor the story's stops, with the mysterious Violet Creator looming over the mythology. The hand-drawn art style earns relentless praise from the opening minutes to the credits, the game's defining quality and the reason it drew attention before release. Voice acting deserves a nod too, with a differentiated cast that lands better than the budget suggests.

Nintendo-flavoured references layer densely across the island. Silhouette sections evoke Donkey Kong Country, a boat sequence channels the King of Red Lions, masks recall Banjo-Kazooie, and the platforming asks for Metroid muscle memory, each homage landing as a moment of recognition rather than theft. The references sit inside an identity that remains its own, closer to a linear ability-gated adventure in the Indivisible visual vein than to any single influence. Spotting them becomes a parallel game. The homage density never overwhelms the underlying warmth of the folkloric setting.

Ability gates open new routes steadily: heal, wall jump, double jump, a downward pogo strike, an axe throw with boomerang recall and an upward catapult. The pogo unlock in particular decimates groups and redefines encounters once learned, and a memorable tentacle-beast chase out of the Forbidden Ruins provides the campaign's adrenaline spike. Shards for upgrades drop generously, obtainable simply by playing, with feathers boosting health and stamina and petrified echoes adding extra heals. Campfires save and restore. The connectivity earns real praise where shortcuts loop back, and the island feels interconnected in its best stretches.

Stamina is baffling in a way that outlasts the campaign. Sprinting drains it but attacking does nothing to it, regeneration behaves awkwardly, and the bar's existence resists justification from the first hour to the last. The offensive kit stays static throughout, with no upgradeable damage, which makes the combat's ceiling arrive early even as the boss design (a spider-taco opener, the pogo-focused duel with plague-infected Dahlia, Chantico's three-phase arena fight) validates the underlying depth. Combat versus stamina remains the design's central oddity. The bar never quite breaks the fun, but it never explains itself either.

Progression is broad but linear. Areas open once and rarely invite return, and by the end the structure barely qualifies as a metroidvania, trading route-finding depth for a tight story-driven six-hour adventure. One enemy bugged out invisible and unkillable across a full playthrough, the only stability blot in an otherwise clean run. The hold-to-save prompt demands five seconds of holding, an odd friction in a genre built on frequent checkpoints. None of these dents the warmth, but together they mark the edges of the game's ambition.

The plague lore lands surprisingly well. The finale's revelations about the plague's source and the axe's true identity arrive as genuine surprises that recontextualise the journey, and the resolution suits the warm folkloric register the island establishes. Itorah's own nature ties the mythology together peacefully rather than through force. Companion writing carries the journey, with the axe's banter never wearing thin across the whole campaign. A close that satisfies this completely is rarer than the genre's hit rate suggests.

Overall, ITORAH is a gorgeous hand-drawn Mesoamerican platformer whose vibrant art, reference-rich world and talking-axe companion deliver a tight, warm-hearted six-hour adventure, held back by an unexplained stamina bar, mostly linear structure and a single stability blot. The trade of metroidvania exploration for story focus mostly works in the game's favour. A really good game in the Indivisible visual vein, and a debut worth following.
